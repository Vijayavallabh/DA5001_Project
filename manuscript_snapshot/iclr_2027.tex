\begin{abstract}
Certified inference-time copyright defences charge, per decoded token, how far output drifts
from a \emph{safe} model trained without the protected work. The budget is in the wrong place. Spend it
once, on \emph{which draw to serve}: draw $n$ safe-model completions and serve the best under any
score. Every token served is one the safe model drew, so $q(y) \le n\,p_s(y)$ --- a \emph{pathwise} certificate of exactly $\log n$ nats, constant in the
work's length where $K = kT$ is linear. Self-consistency is an instance. No per-token \emph{rate}
can match it: the chain rule leaves $K$ vacuous --- empty at $K = S(x)$ for every R\'enyi order,
extraction at $0.88$--$1.17$ of it across nine pairs with nine distinct anchors --- or else bounded
and spent at $O(1)$ steps, a shape no rate takes; at $k=0.5$ neither of two judges
separates the decoder from that safe model. Against a metered decoder's
measured $171.3$ nats, sixty-four draws spend at most $3.175$ and are judged
better under four of five judges against one fixed opponent; at $n=8$ this repeats at four of six anchors in three families, and with no
judge $32$ draws lift GSM8K $0.320 \to 0.546$. It reproduces no protected passage at any
$n \le 64$ or at $256$, even against an adversarial scorer --- \emph{relative} to the anchor, whose
own leakage $n$ multiplies and which must be vetted. It costs $35.4\times$ the meter's wall-clock
($21.8\times$ serving only the anchor) and loses at matched compute; where the anchor cannot do the
task, no $n$ rescues it.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Ethics Statement}
Auditing a copyright defence requires demonstrating that it can be defeated, and this paper does
that: it fine-tunes memorising models, attacks a protected decoder, and reports where extraction
begins. We judge the disclosure net positive and have bounded it deliberately. The passages are
already published as extraction benchmarks (CopyBench, BookMIA), only aggregate recall statistics are
reported and never reconstructed passages, and the accompanying anonymised repository ships the
fine-tuning recipes and every analysis script but not the merged memorising weights, which reproduce
copyrighted text on demand. Nothing here enables an attack that possession of a fine-tuned model
would not already permit.

The constructive direction carries its own risk, which is misplaced confidence. A $\log n$
certificate bounds the probability that the served text is a protected work \emph{relative to the
safe model's own probability of producing it}, so it is only as good as that model. We measure what
happens when it is not: across twelve deliberately memorised anchors, an adversary selecting by the
memorising model's own likelihood over $64$ draws raises the rate at which most of a passage is
reproduced by a factor of $1.0$ to $7.0$, against the $64$ the certificate permits
(Appendix~\ref{app:selection}). The mechanism is a multiplier on the anchor's own leakage, not a
floor under it. It is a statement about a mechanism, not a legal conclusion about infringement, and
we make no claim about legal thresholds for copyright infringement.

That the guarantee is relative to the anchor is not a reason to take the anchor on faith, because
$\Pr_{p_s}[E]$ is the one quantity here a deployer can measure outright --- with no attack, no risky
model and no access to the mechanism. Draw from the candidate anchor on the protected passages' own
prefixes and score near-verbatim recall; that is exactly the number $n$ multiplies. Run on every
model in this paper, it reads $0.000$ on all $100$ passages at each of the five openly licensed
anchors, while \texttt{Llama-3.1-70B}, which memorised in pre-training, leaks on $0.50$ and
reproduces one passage in full, and the two open-data models at this scale fail it too ---
\texttt{OLMo-2-7B} on $2$ of $50$ passages and \texttt{OLMo-2-13B} on $6$, up to $0.566$ of a
passage. \textbf{The prefix length is part of the test and must be quoted with it}: that same $70$B
reads $0.0000$ at the fourteen genuine prefix tokens an earlier version of this check used, and
fails only at a hundred (Appendix~\ref{app:vetting}). A deployer who runs it at too short a prefix
will clear a model that reproduces the work in full, and since the user chooses the prompt, the
prefix is part of the attack surface: run the check at the longest genuine prefix the deployment
accepts. It is sound rather than complete --- passing is
evidence, not proof, since a model may hold a work it does not reproduce at the prefix length
tried --- and like a blocklist it speaks only about the works it is handed.

The deployer-facing recommendation is narrow and we would rather it were quoted than the theorems:
publish $k/s(x)$ rather than $k$, name the work it was computed against, prefer a budget whose size
does not grow with the thing it protects, and vet the anchor with the check above before quoting
$\log n$ to anyone, because $n$ multiplies whatever the anchor already has.

\section*{Reproducibility Statement}
Every number in this paper is produced by an analysis script and written to a CSV before it is
quoted, with the producing command recorded alongside it. Proofs of
Propositions~\ref{prop:threshold} and~\ref{prop:outrun} and of Theorem~\ref{thm:nfl} are in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, stated with their assumptions; Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} is proved
where it is stated. The three rates of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rates} require only forward passes and no
decoding, so Section~\ref{sec:onset} and Appendix~\ref{app:scaling} reproduce on a single GPU from
openly licensed checkpoints. Every interval this paper scores against was fixed before the run that
produced it, together with the grid, the admission criterion and the outcomes excluded in advance.
Budget accounting is checked per trajectory rather than on averages, and every run reports the
$k=-1$ (risky only) and $k=0$ (safe only) baselines on the same prompts and seeds. An anonymised
repository with the code, the CSVs, the reproduction commands and the full record of those
specifications accompanies the submission.

One property of the artifact a reproducer should expect, and which no CSV of ours would reveal.
Selection serves the argmax of a reward computed in bfloat16, and that argmax is not bitwise
reproducible: re-scoring the \emph{same} $32{,}000$ candidate texts on a different GPU architecture,
at a different batch size, or in float32 serves a different completion on $5.6$ to $8.6\%$ of prompts
at $n=64$ --- a fraction that rises with $n$ and then flattens, as the gap between the best and
second-best candidate narrows. What the served completion is \emph{worth} is reproducible: the
alternative gives up $0.011$ to $0.03$ nats against a gap of $5.1$, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} holds for any tie rule, so neither the certificate nor any gain
reported here turns on which of two near-equal candidates wins. This was measured rather than
assumed, and it carried no pre-registered interval.

\section*{LLM Usage}
LLMs were used for editorial purposes in this manuscript, and all outputs were inspected by the
authors to ensure accuracy and originality. Instruction-tuned models also appear in two further
roles, which we separate deliberately. As \emph{measurement instruments} they judge output quality
in Sections~\ref{sec:experiments} and~\ref{sec:orders}; their resolution is measured rather than
assumed --- by scoring one decoder against itself under two seeds, by including a null arm, and by
running two judges that are reported separately wherever they disagree. As a \emph{component of the
proposed mechanism} one of them supplies the score that selection anchoring ranks by, which is why
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} never scores an arm with the model that selected it, and why
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} is stated for an arbitrary score: the certificate must not depend
on that model behaving well. Total compute for this work is at most $366$ GPU-hours on A100
hardware, most of it the budget sweeps and the judged arms;
the memorisation fine-tunes account for at most $73$ of those hours, some of whose run directories
also contain the sweep that followed.
