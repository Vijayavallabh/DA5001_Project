\title{What Does a Copyright Budget Certify?\\
An Exact Threshold for Per-Token Meters, and a Length-Independent Certificate for Selection}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Certified inference-time copyright defences publish a divergence budget between the served distribution
and a safe model trained without the protected works. A budget of $K$ nats says nothing about a work whose
surprisal under the safe model is at most $K$, at every R\'enyi order. Anchored decoding charges its budget
per token, so it grows with the output, and at its authors' book setting it excludes nothing about any of
$758$ protected passages. At $k=0.5$, the one budget that still covers a $50$-token window, neither of two
judges separates it from its safe model. Spent instead on choosing among $n$ draws from the safe model, by
any rule, an adversary's included, the budget is a pathwise certificate of exactly $\log n$ nats at any
output length, adding up exactly over queries. At matched certificates, best-of-$64$ and its blockwise
variant beat every meter we ran under two judges, including one that caps every $50$-token window; that
windowed meter wins where the risky model is far stronger, as a chat assistant, but certifies exact windows
only and leaks near-verbatim text at $125$ nats, where both judges prefer it. Where a meter's certificate
is void, selection wins against the authors' own $70$B pair and against their byte-level decoder, and
loses to the $8$B at their temperature $0.7$ or as a chat assistant. With no judge, majority vote lifts
GSM8K from $0.320$ to $0.546$. Selection never consults the risky model, so no $n$ rescues an anchor that
cannot do the task; served from the anchor alone it costs $21.8\times$ a metered decode and loses at
matched compute. Its leakage, zero at any $n \le 64$ or at $256$, is relative to the anchor, which must be
vetted, and a colluding scorer can spend the certificate across queries.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Ethics Statement}
Auditing a copyright defence means showing where it fails, so this paper fine-tunes memorising models
and attacks a protected decoder. We report aggregate recall only, never reconstructed text, on passages
already published as extraction benchmarks (CopyBench, \citealp{chen2024copybench}; BookMIA,
\citealp{shi2024detecting}); the anonymised repository ships the
fine-tuning recipes and every analysis script but not the merged memorising weights, which reproduce
copyrighted text on demand. The constructive result carries its own risk, misplaced confidence: $\log n$
bounds the probability that the served text is a protected work \emph{relative to the safe model's own},
so it is only as good as that model. Over twelve deliberately contaminated anchors an adversarial scorer
raised the anchor's own leakage by $1.0$ to $7.0\times$ on one draw and $1.0$ to $3.0\times$ on a second,
against the $64$ the certificate permits (Appendix~\ref{app:contaminated}). The anchor's rate is the one
quantity a deployer can measure with no attack and no access to the mechanism: draw from it on the
protected passages' own prefixes and score recall. \textbf{The prefix length is part of the test}:
\texttt{Llama-3.1-70B}, which memorised the work in pre-training, reads $0.0000$ at fourteen genuine
prefix tokens and leaks on $31$ of $50$ passages at $150$, and the check is not monotone in the prefix,
so it must run at every prefix length the deployment accepts, and at the temperature it samples at (Appendix~\ref{app:vetting}). Passing is
evidence, not proof, and like a blocklist the check speaks only about the works it is handed. Our
statements concern mechanisms; whether near-access-freeness tracks infringement is contested
\citep{elkinkoren2024copyright}, and we make no claim about legal thresholds for copyright infringement. The
recommendation we would rather see quoted than the theorems: publish $k/s(x)$ rather than $k$, prefer a
budget whose size does not grow with what it protects, vet the anchor before quoting $\log n$,
because $n$ multiplies whatever the anchor already has, and unless the scorer is vetted too, cap each
user's queries below $S/\log n$ for the shortest protected event: a scorer that colludes with the user
can spend the transcript's $m \log n$ nats one query at a time (Section~\ref{sec:compose}).

\section*{Reproducibility Statement}
Every number in this paper is written by an analysis script to a CSV before it is quoted, and the
anonymised repository contains the code, the CSVs and the command that produced each. Proofs are in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. Every interval we score against was fixed before the run that produced it,
together with its grid, admission criterion and excluded outcomes; budget accounting is checked per
trajectory, and every run reports the $k=-1$ (risky only) and $k=0$ (safe only) baselines on the same
prompts and seeds. Two properties a reproducer should expect. Selection serves the argmax of a reward
computed in bfloat16, and that argmax is not bitwise reproducible: re-scoring the same $32{,}000$
candidates on another GPU architecture, at another batch size or in float32 serves a different
completion on $5.6$ to $8.6\%$ of prompts at $n=64$; what is served is worth the same, the alternative giving up $0.011$ to $0.03$ nats against a gap of $5.1$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} holds for any tie rule. This was measured rather than assumed and carried no pre-registered interval. And the harness recorded each generation with the tail of its prompt, an artefact of slicing
left-padded batches; nothing served was affected, every recorded generation is recovered exactly, and
Table~\ref{tab:served} and Figures~\ref{fig:selection}, \ref{fig:h2h} and~\ref{fig:breadth} are judged on
the recovered text (Appendix~\ref{app:judgemethod}).

\section*{LLM Usage}
LLMs were used for editorial purposes in this manuscript, and all outputs were inspected by the
authors to ensure accuracy and originality. Instruction-tuned models also appear as \emph{measurement
instruments}, judging output quality, and as a \emph{component of the mechanism}, supplying the score
selection ranks by; for that reason no arm is scored by the model that selected it, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} is stated for an arbitrary score. Total compute for this work is at most
$366$ GPU-hours on A100 hardware, most of it the budget sweeps and the judged arms; the memorisation
fine-tunes account for at most $73$ of those hours.
