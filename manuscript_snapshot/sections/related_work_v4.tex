\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Near-access-freeness \citep{vyas2023provable} asks a served model to stay within a divergence of a
safe model that never saw the protected work; CP-$\Delta$/CP-$k$ instantiate it with models trained
on disjoint shards \citep{abad2025cpfuse}, and anchored decoding \citep{he2026anchored} enforces it
per token with a bucket. Two results bear directly on ours: \citet{cohen2025blameless} shows
per-query access-freeness does not compose, which makes the composition
arithmetic of Section~\ref{sec:compose} load-bearing, and \citet{segal2026provably} report that CP-style guarantees can be
numerically uninformative and demonstrate extraction against NAF-protected models
\citep[see also][]{cohen2026barriers}. We are downstream of that observation; where they tighten a bound,
Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} says no R\'enyi order can repair the accounting, which is what
licenses changing the primitive rather than the constant. An earlier audit of anchored decoding \citep{vijayavallabh2026audit} measured its
vacuity on released logs; the order-invariant threshold, the sparsity bound, the selection
certificate and every head-to-head here are new. Whether near-access-freeness tracks infringement
at all is contested \citep{elkinkoren2024copyright}; our statements are about mechanisms, not law.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Scope.} This paper is about defences that publish a \emph{divergence certificate}; the
widely deployed ones publish none --- $n$-gram blocklists \citep{ippolito2023preventing}, datastores
\citep{min2024silo,golatkar2024cpr}, filtering \citep{liu2024shield}, compared by CoTaEval
\citep{wei2024cotaeval} --- and their guarantee names the works it was handed, so it is silent on an
unlisted work and on an adversary supplying the prefix. We measure one as a decoder, and on ordinary
text it costs
nothing (Appendix~\ref{app:blocklist}).

\looseness=-1 We claim no novelty for best-of-$n$ sampling or its divergence bound. Selecting among $n$ draws is
standard inference-time alignment \citep{gao2023scaling,mudgal2024controlled,wang2023selfconsistency}, the bound
$D_{\mathrm{KL}} \le \log n - (n-1)/n$ is \citet{beirami2025bestofn}, its coverage and
optimality are characterised by \citet{huang2025bestofn}, and it is near-optimal in win rate
against KL and asymptotically matches KL-regularised tuning \citep{gui2024bonbon,yang2024asymptotics}
--- which predicts that selection buys more utility per nat than a metered tilt, so what we measure
is how much, against a certified baseline. Charging the choice among repeated runs is also the
accounting of private selection in differential privacy
\citep{liu2019private,papernot2022hyperparameter}, the closest formal analogue of
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection}. That literature prices the divergence as a \emph{cost} --- the budget before reward
overoptimisation, or the one an attacker spends to jailbreak \citep{hughes2024bestofn}. What is new
is the opposite reading: the same inequality, in its sharper pointwise form $q(y) \le n\,p_s(y)$, is
a \emph{copyright certificate} of the kind Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} governs, at
$K = \log n$ and under the pathwise order. To our knowledge no prior work states that a selection
rule satisfies near-access-freeness, nor compares the two mechanisms on one divergence axis.
The closest neighbour is \citet{kalai2026consensus}, who serve only where $k$ models agree: the same
instinct from the opposite premise, needing several models of which an unknown subset is safe and
paying by abstaining, where we need one \emph{named} safe model (Appendix~\ref{app:kalai}).

Every other tool is standard --- the R\'enyi filter \citep{feldman2020renyifilter}, adaptive
composition \citep{whitehouse2023fully,rogers2016odometers}, the token bucket
\citep{loynes1962stability,cruz1991calculus}, extraction
\citep{carlini2021extracting,carlini2023quantifying,cooper2025extracting} --- with its genealogy in
Appendix~\ref{app:related}.
