\section{The published budget determines neither the protection nor the price}\label{sec:orders}

Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} says every order of the charge becomes vacuous at the same budget.
A deployer who publishes $k$ therefore publishes the same certificate whichever order the decoder
uses. We built the family --- $\alpha \to 1$ is the audited decoder, $\alpha \to \infty$ is the
pathwise one, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:renyi-path} is closed form on the geodesic, so one bisection serves
all of them --- and ran the same attack and the same ordinary workload through four members of it at
the budget the mechanism's authors use. Figure~\ref{fig:orders} in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} shows what the certificate says across the family;
Table~\ref{tab:orders} shows what the decoders do.

Table~\ref{tab:orders} is the point of this paper in one row group. Holding the published budget
fixed, and the certificate with it --- vacuous throughout --- the four decoders span two orders of
magnitude in what a compliant adversary recovers, $0.097$ of a passage down to $0.001$, and more
than two in what they cost, from touching $0.4\%$ of ordinary decode steps to $90.1\%$. A number
that determines neither the protection nor the price is not a summary of the mechanism but of one
accounting convention inside it.

The family is cheap at one end and not in the middle, and the fourth row shows it. Moving from
$\alpha=1$ to $\alpha=2$ nearly halves oracle-window recall, $0.097$ to $0.054$, for intervention
rising from $0.4\%$ to $2.4\%$ of steps and no measurable loss of diversity --- distinct-3 moves
from $0.9910$ to $0.9924$, the wrong way for a cost. One more step of the order does not continue
that trade: at $\alpha=4$ oracle recall falls to $0.004$, but the decoder serves the risky model
unchanged at $8.7\%$ of steps instead of $91.4\%$ and intervenes at $83.0\%$, already most of the
way to $\alpha=8$ rather than halfway. The order is a sharp knob, not a dial, and its far end is a
different system rather than a tighter accounting of the same one. A deployer choosing $\alpha$ on
the published budget alone cannot tell which they have bought.

\paragraph{Judged utility, and the resolution of the instrument}
Activity counts are not utility, so we also score the arms with a judge outside the audit
(Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, 150 pairs per arm against the unconstrained model, presentation order
randomised). A win rate is meaningless without a null: the unconstrained model judged against
itself at a different seed loses $41.7\%$ of the time, and this run contains the same decoder twice
--- the audited KL arm and $\alpha=1$ are one rule reached by two solvers --- differing by $3.9$
points, $0.71\sigma$. That is the instrument's resolution, measured rather than assumed, and
nothing smaller should be read as a cost.

Against it: $\alpha=2$ loses $57.3\%$ against $\alpha=1$'s $53.4\%$, a difference of $3.9$ points at
$0.68\sigma$ --- exactly the noise floor, so its cost is at most what two runs of one decoder
differ by, while its oracle-window leakage is half. $\alpha=8$ loses $65.3\%$, $2.11\sigma$ above
$\alpha=1$, and that is indistinguishable from simply serving the anchor and nothing else
($61.7\%$, a gap of $0.68\sigma$), whose leakage is also indistinguishable ($0.003$ against
$0.001$). Turning the order up far enough does not buy a better trade; it converges on the safe
model, which was always available without a mechanism.

The comparison is at matched budget rather than matched utility, and what that does and does not establish is set out in Appendix~\ref{app:collapse}.
