\subsection{The deployed instance, and every escape it has left}\label{sec:orders}

Two questions follow for anchored decoding \citep{he2026anchored}: does the vacuity threshold
correspond to anything a rights-holder would recognise, and can any remaining degree of freedom
recover what Proposition~\ref{prop:sparse} says it cannot?

All four are measured in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}: sharpen the charge,
lower the cap, schedule the spend better, or grant the budget up front and spend it sparsely --- the
one causal policy Proposition~\ref{prop:sparse} positively permits. Each was specified in advance with a refuting band and each lands on a
horn; the last, the fair test of whether a \emph{causal} rule can concentrate, reaches $+0.0545$ $[+0.0315, +0.0775]$ at $64$
nats against selection's $+0.1045$ at $\log 64 = 4.16$: an empirical shortfall for the best
construction we could build, not a proof that none matches. \textbf{The $80\times$ of sharpening is real and a rights-holder is right to want it}; what it costs is
the objection, and at $\alpha=8$ the constraint binds at $99.6\%$ of ordinary steps against $0.4\%$.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:nfl} prices row 2's collapse onto the safe model: winning \emph{every} comparison would
cost an optimal policy $1.30$ nats and the decoder spends $132$ times that to win a third of them.
What the four share is that the budget is not spent inefficiently on utility --- it is spent
\emph{efficiently on a target that is not utility}, likelihood under $p_r$, which predicts judged
quality no better than the answer's length does (Appendix~\ref{app:currency}).
