\section{Where extraction begins, and why there}\label{sec:onset}

Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} locates the budget at which the certificate stops saying anything
and shows no order of the charge moves it. It does not say leakage begins there. This section
derives where leakage begins, from the two models and the work alone.

\paragraph{Three pairs}
The first is the audited one, a $1.8$B Common Pile anchor against a memorising Llama-3.1-8B. The
second is built so that memorisation is the only difference: we fine-tune the $7$B Common Pile model
until it reproduces the passages and fuse it against the \emph{unmodified} model it came from, so
the two share an architecture, a corpus, a tokenizer and every weight the memorisation did not move
(Appendix~\ref{app:second-anchor}). The third is the same construction at $350$M, which matters
because a smaller model retains more residual surprisal on text it has memorised --- $s_r = 0.33$
against $0.19$ and $0.18$ --- and so separates the two readings below. A pair enters this analysis
only if the \emph{unconstrained} risky model reproduces the passages: we require single-query recall
at $k=-1$ above $0.10$, and the three pairs reach $0.72$, $0.72$ and $0.91$. An earlier $350$M
memoriser reached $0.71$ under greedy decoding but only $0.022$ under the sampling the attack uses,
and is excluded.

\paragraph{The onset is a fixed fraction of the vacuity threshold}
Measured as the budget at which mean single-query recall first reaches $0.01$, interpolated inside
its bracket:

Rates here are nats per \emph{token}, unlike Section~\ref{sec:scaling}: the decoder meters one
charge per decoded token and each pair shares a vocabulary by construction, so an onset is natively
a per-token rate. That cannot affect the ratio, whose numerator and denominator are charged in the
same units, but it does inflate the dynamic range. Across pairs sharing no anchor, no tokenizer and
no risky model, $s(x)$ spans $1.49\times$ per token and $1.33\times$ per character, and the onset
spans the same factor as the threshold it tracks; the ratio is $0.887$, $0.892$, $0.895$, a
standard deviation of $0.003$.
Rescaling the budget by $s(x)$ collapses the recall curves to a mean spread of $0.0048$ over
$k/s(x) \in [0.7, 1.0]$, degrading to $0.0158$ above the threshold
(Figure~\ref{fig:onset}). The intervals are wide --- they come from resampling passages, and near
the onset one or two works out of a hundred carry the mean --- so the agreement of the point
estimates is finer than any one of them deserves. What the three pairs jointly support is the
proportionality, not the third decimal.

\paragraph{A refinement we derived, pre-registered, and had to reject}
The constant invites an explanation, and the geometry supplies a candidate. On the geometric path
the log-partition satisfies $\psi_t(1) = \log \sum_v p_s(v) e^{\ell_t(v)} = 0$ identically, so at
full tilt the per-token charge accumulated \emph{along a target} $x$ is
$\frac{1}{T}\sum_t D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{r,t}\,\|\,p_{s,t})$ on $x$'s own prefixes. When the risky
model is near-deterministic on a memorised $x$ that expectation collapses onto the realised token
and the charge becomes a difference of surprisals. Define
\begin{equation}
r(x) \;=\; s_s(x) \;-\; s_r(x),
\label{eq:req}
\end{equation}
the budget rate a decoder must permit before it can pay for $x$ at all. It needs no decoding and no
attack. It also predicts that the onset ratio is $1 - s_r/s_s$ and must therefore \emph{move} as
memorisation changes --- $0.940$, $0.925$ and $0.908$ for these three pairs.

We tested that on the third pair before measuring it, committing the prediction to the repository
first. Calibrating the population onset to a low quantile of the $r(x)$ distribution on pairs one
and two puts it at $q_{25}$, which reproduces their onsets to $0.4\%$ and $0.1\%$ and predicts
$2.96$ nats for the held-out pair. The measurement is $3.18$, with a bootstrap interval of
$[3.12, 3.90]$ that excludes the prediction; a constant $0.888\,s(x)$ predicts $3.16$ and does not
lie outside it. The directional claim fails too: $s_r$ nearly doubles across these pairs while the
measured ratio moves by $0.008$, and upward rather than down.

So Eq.~\eqref{eq:req} is a correct statement about what a decoder must \emph{afford} and a wrong one
about where leakage \emph{begins}, and the two come apart because affordability is necessary and far
from sufficient: at the onset budget $r(x) \le k$ holds for $27\%$ and $26\%$ of works on the two
pairs where we can check it, while $0\%$ and $1\%$ actually leak. The budget governs what a decoder
can pay for; it does not govern what the risky model memorised well enough to emit. We report the
proportionality as measured and the refinement as tried and rejected.

\paragraph{A population boundary, not a per-work screen}
Equation~\eqref{eq:req} is defined per work, so it is fair to ask whether it screens an individual
book. It does not, and measurably so: as a classifier for ``which passage leaks at budget $k$'',
$r(x)$ reaches a mean AUC of $0.678$ while the anchor surprisal $s(x)$ \emph{alone} reaches $0.751$
(Appendix~\ref{app:collapse}). Subtracting $s_r$ hurts, and measurably so: across works it carries
$6$--$7\%$ of the level of $s_s$ but $44$--$45\%$ of its standard deviation, so it removes a
near-constant shift and adds variance. The same ordering holds for the population boundary, where
rescaling by $s(x)$ collapses the three pairs to a spread of $0.0070$ against $0.0096$ for $r$. A
rights-holder can use $s(x)$ to locate the budget at which a class of works becomes exposed; for
one particular book it is not good enough to rely on.
