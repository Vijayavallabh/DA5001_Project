\section{Where extraction begins, and why there}\label{sec:onset}

Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} locates the budget at which the certificate stops saying anything
and shows no order of the charge moves it. It does not say leakage begins there. This section
derives where leakage begins, from the two models and the work alone.

\paragraph{Five pairs}
The first is the audited one, a $1.8$B Common Pile anchor against a memorising Llama-3.1-8B. The
other four are built so that memorisation is the only difference: we fine-tune a model until it
reproduces the passages and fuse it against the \emph{unmodified} model it came from, so the two
share an architecture, a corpus, a tokenizer and every weight the memorisation did not move
(Appendix~\ref{app:second-anchor}). They run $7$B, $1.2$B, $350$M and $1.7$B across three corpora,
and the last is deliberately unlike the rest: a KL3M anchor whose tokenizer emits about two
characters per token where the others emit four, on targets twice as long in tokens, with much the
weakest memoriser. A pair enters only if the \emph{unconstrained} risky model reproduces the
passages, single-query recall at $k=-1$ above $0.10$: the five reach $0.72$, $0.72$, $0.88$, $0.91$
and $0.41$. An earlier $350$M memoriser reached $0.71$ greedily but $0.022$ under the sampling the
attack uses, and is excluded.

its bracket:

Rates here are nats per \emph{token}, unlike Section~\ref{sec:scaling}, because the decoder meters
one charge per decoded token and each pair shares a vocabulary by construction. That cannot affect
the ratio, whose numerator and denominator are charged in the same units. Across pairs sharing no
anchor, no tokenizer and no risky model, $s(x)$ spans $1.61\times$ per token and $1.71\times$ per
character, and every onset falls between $0.88$ and $1.17$ of it. \emph{The fraction is not fixed.}
Four of the five agree closely, at $0.878$ to $0.920$, but the fifth sits at $1.166$ with a
bootstrap interval of $[1.14, 1.41]$ that excludes $1$ and excludes three of the other four
intervals: on that pair leakage begins \emph{after} the certificate has gone vacuous, not before.
What survives is weaker and still worth having. A quantity computable from the anchor and the work
alone, with no decoding and no attack, locates the onset to within about a fifth on every pair, over
a $1.71\times$ spread it would otherwise have to guess; rescaling by it collapses the curves to a
mean spread of $0.026$ over $k/s(x) \in [0.7, 1.2]$ (Figure~\ref{fig:onset}). That is a unit, not a
law.

\paragraph{A pair nobody fine-tuned}
All five pairs above are built, so the pattern could be a property of the recipe. It is not:
Llama-3.1-70B base, which memorised \emph{Harry Potter} in
pretraining~\citep{cooper2025extracting}, leaks $0.0180$ at $k/s(x) = 0.964$ against $0.010$ to
$0.039$ for the built pairs there, at both of the authors' decoding settings
(Appendix~\ref{app:collapse}).

\paragraph{A refinement we derived, pre-registered, and had to reject}
The constant invites an explanation, and the geometry supplies a candidate. On the geometric path
the log-partition satisfies $\psi_t(1) = \log \sum_v p_s(v) e^{\ell_t(v)} = 0$ identically, so at
full tilt the per-token charge accumulated \emph{along a target} $x$ is
$\frac{1}{T}\sum_t D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{r,t}\,\|\,p_{s,t})$ on $x$'s own prefixes. When the risky
model is near-deterministic on a memorised $x$ that expectation collapses onto the realised token
and the charge becomes a difference of surprisals. Define
\begin{equation}
r(x) \;=\; s_s(x) \;-\; s_r(x),
\label{eq:req}
\end{equation}
the budget rate a decoder must permit before it can pay for $x$ at all. It needs no decoding and no
attack. It also predicts that the onset ratio is $1 - s_r/s_s$ and must therefore \emph{move} as
memorisation changes --- $0.940$, $0.925$, $0.908$, $0.886$ and $0.647$ for these five pairs.

Predictions for the third, fourth and fifth pairs were committed to the repository before any of
them was swept, so those three are a test and the first two, on which the alternatives were
calibrated, are not. Three rules were on the table: Eq.~\eqref{eq:req} used directly at the median
of $r(x)$, which has nothing fitted in it; the same quantity calibrated to a low quantile of the
$r(x)$ distribution, which pairs one and two place at $q_{25}$; and a single fitted constant
$c\,s(x)$ with $c = 0.889$. On the third and fourth pairs all three landed within $0.22$ nats of
the measurement and the two leading rules within $0.07$ of each other, so nothing was decided. The
fifth was built to decide it: its memoriser retains $35\%$ of the anchor's surprisal against
$6$--$11\%$ elsewhere, which pulls the rules $0.54$ nats apart. All three miss it, all three miss
low, and all three fall outside the measured interval --- by $1.15$, $0.61$ and $1.40$ nats. Mean
absolute errors over the three held-out pairs are $0.405$, $0.252$ and $0.617$.

The directional claim does not merely fail, it inverts. Eq.~\eqref{eq:req} says the ratio should
fall as the memoriser leaves more residual surprisal; the pair with by far the most residual
surprisal has by far the highest ratio, and the rank correlation over five pairs is $-0.40$. So
Eq.~\eqref{eq:req} is a correct statement about what a decoder must \emph{afford} and a wrong one
about where leakage \emph{begins}. The gap between them is the one the affordability check already
exposed: at the onset budget $r(x) \le k$ holds for $27\%$ and $26\%$ of works on the two pairs
where we can check it, while $0\%$ and $1\%$ leak. The fifth pair makes the point at its limit ---
at its onset the budget covers $98\%$ of the passages and $1\%$ leak. What a budget can pay for is
not what the risky model memorised well enough to emit, and the weaker the memoriser the further
apart those two are.

\paragraph{A population boundary, not a per-work screen}
Equation~\eqref{eq:req} is defined per work, so it is fair to ask whether it screens an individual
book. It does not, and measurably so: as a classifier for ``which passage leaks at budget $k$'',
$r(x)$ reaches a mean AUC of $0.678$ while the anchor surprisal $s(x)$ \emph{alone} reaches $0.751$
(Appendix~\ref{app:collapse}). Subtracting $s_r$ hurts, and measurably so: across works it carries
$6$--$7\%$ of the level of $s_s$ but $44$--$45\%$ of its standard deviation, so it removes a
near-constant shift and adds variance. For the population boundary the ablation cannot separate
them at all: the winner changes every time a pair is added ($0.0046$ against $0.0059$ on two pairs,
$0.0096$ against $0.0070$ on three, $0.0143$ against $0.0180$ on four, $0.0215$ against $0.0264$ on
five), and by five pairs neither is far from the $0.0286$ of not rescaling at all. A rights-holder
can use either to locate the budget at which a class of works becomes exposed; for one particular
book neither is good enough to rely on.
