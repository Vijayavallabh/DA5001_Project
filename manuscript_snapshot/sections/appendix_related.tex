\section{Related work in full}\label{app:related}

\paragraph{Access-free guarantees and their discontents}
Near-access-freeness \citep{vyas2023provable} asks that a served model stay within a divergence of a
safe model that never saw the protected work, and CP-$\Delta$ and CP-$k$ instantiate it by combining
models trained on disjoint shards \citep{vyas2023provable,abad2025cpfuse}. Three recent results bear
directly on ours. \citet{cohen2025blameless} shows that per-query access-freeness does not compose
across queries; \citet{cohen2026barriers} proves barriers for counterfactual credit attribution, a
sibling relaxation of differential privacy; and closest of all, \citet{segal2026provably} report that
CP-style guarantees can be numerically uninformative in realistic fine-tuning settings and demonstrate
extraction against NAF-protected models. We are downstream of that observation, not upstream of it,
and the two results are compatible. Their diagnosis is that memorisation puts sharp probability spikes
on a few tokens, so a divergence over \emph{raw} probabilities reads as large even where the models
agree on what to emit, and their repair charges relative probabilities instead: a change in
\emph{what} is metered. Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} closes a different lever, the one a deployer
actually has --- for every order of the same per-step charge, from KL to max-divergence, the
certificate becomes vacuous at the same budget, so a stronger charge buys precision below the
threshold and never moves it. Neither result subsumes the other; together they leave a deployer with
one lever that moves the threshold, a smaller budget, which Theorem~\ref{thm:nfl} prices and
Section~\ref{sec:orders} measures. \citet{cohen2026barriers} is the closer structural parallel, and
not the one we first read it as: its non-composition is \emph{autoregressive} rather than across
queries, so imposing counterfactual credit attribution on the next-token predictor does not make the
sequence-level model satisfy it. Ours is the same shape for a different quantity --- a per-step charge
honoured at every step still leaves the sequence-level event $\{\text{output}=x\}$ unprotected once
the budget reaches $S(x)$ --- priced in nats for a single query rather than stated as a barrier.

\paragraph{Inference-time defences that spend no divergence budget at all}
Two recent methods sit in this paper's slot --- decode-time, no retraining --- and reach it without
a cumulative budget, which makes them the sharpest comparison available and worth stating plainly.
\textbf{TokenSwap} \citep{prashant2025tokenswap} substitutes the probabilities of high-frequency
grammatical tokens from a small auxiliary model, reporting a $10$--$800\times$ reduction in verbatim
generation and proving that the probability of emitting a memorised sequence decays exponentially in
its length. \textbf{TRBS} \citep{fu2026duplicate} reorders beam candidates against an FM-index of the
protected corpus, with a spike-entropy rule protecting structurally necessary tokens, and is
evaluated on multilingual \emph{code}. Both are stronger than a blocklist on the axis they target,
and neither is in this paper's class, for the same reason the blocklist is not: the guarantee is
about the \emph{catalogue supplied} --- a sequence that is memorised, or an $n$-gram that is indexed
--- and not about the served law relative to a safe model. TokenSwap's exponential decay is the
closest any of them comes to a certificate, and it is a statement about a named memorised string
rather than a bound on $q(y)/p_s(y)$ for every $y$; it says nothing about an unlisted work, a
paraphrase, or an adversary who supplies the prefix. We did not measure either one. TRBS is a code
method and porting it to prose would be our construction rather than theirs, and TokenSwap needs a
paired auxiliary model whose own contamination would have to be vetted before any leakage number
from it meant anything --- the same requirement Appendix~\ref{app:selection} imposes on our anchor.
Measuring them properly is the obvious next comparison, and we say so rather than implying the
omission is principled.

\paragraph{Other inference-time defences, and the level none of them certifies}
CP-Fuse combines models trained on disjoint shards under a balancing property \citep{abad2025cpfuse}
and is the second mechanism we audit; BloomScrub certifies takedowns by Bloom-filter screening with
abstention \citep{zhang2025bloomscrub}; SILO isolates legal risk in a nonparametric datastore
\citep{min2024silo}; others refuse \citep{liu2024shield} or randomise the decoder
\citep{chen2024randomization}, and for diffusion models retrieval carries a NAF guarantee
\citep{golatkar2024cpr} in response to replication in image models \citep{somepalli2023diffusion}.
\citet{alshehyari2026copyshield} benchmark output-, behavioural- and representation-level defences
across two model families; the level that benchmark omits is the certified one, which is exactly where
a low measured leakage and a vacuous certificate can coexist. The closest empirical precedent for
Section~\ref{sec:frontier}'s accounting is VA3 \citep{li2024va3}, which amplifies a probabilistic
copyright guarantee for text-to-image models through repeated interaction; what composes there is a
per-sample probability accumulating over retries, where a budget adds by construction. That a KL bound
does not imply a worst-case event bound is itself classical
\citep{sason2016fdivergence,vanerven2014renyi}, and the differential-privacy literature has developed
the conversions and their costs; our contribution is not the inequality but which budgets survive the
requirement that the certificate be informative about a specific work \emph{and} the mechanism remain
usable. The accounting machinery we borrow is the R\'enyi filter of \citet{feldman2020renyifilter} and
fully adaptive composition \citep{whitehouse2023fully,rogers2016odometers}; the token bucket and its
running maximum are standard objects in queueing and network calculus
\citep{loynes1962stability,cruz1991calculus,leboudec2001network}, which is where our second boundary
comes from.

\paragraph{The incumbent that publishes no number, measured}\label{app:blocklist}
The alternative a deployer would otherwise run is not another budget, it is a blocklist: refuse any
continuation matching an $n$-gram of a listed work \citep{ippolito2023preventing}, the rule CoTaEval
\citep{wei2024cotaeval} benchmarks alongside unlearning and system-prompt defences. It is outside
this paper's class because it certifies nothing about the served law, but declining to compare
against it because of that would be convenient rather than honest, so we measured it.
\texttt{analysis/\allowbreak blocklist.py} applies the rule to generations already on disk, at
$n \in \{6, 8, 10\}$ and at three catalogue sizes: the sixteen novels of the main workload
($758$ passages), the fifty BookMIA books marked seen ($4{,}935$), and all one hundred ($9{,}870$).

\textbf{It refuted the argument it was built to support.} We expected collateral damage to ordinary
text to grow with the catalogue, so that a blocklist covering a realistic one would begin refusing
ordinary continuations --- the standard objection to the method, and a convenient one for us. It is
wrong. At $n=10$ the fraction of ordinary tokens blocked runs $0.000\%$ at $758$ passages to
$0.140\%$ at $4{,}935$ and then stops: $0.140\%$ again at $9{,}870$, where the index has doubled to
$2.24$M $n$-grams. At $n=8$ it is $0.000\%$, $0.147\%$, $0.147\%$, the same flattening. A
blocklist's collateral is \emph{bounded}, and the claim that it scales with the catalogue is
withdrawn.

\textbf{Run as a decoder, it is stronger still, and a second prediction of ours failed.}
Applying the rule to finished text measures what it would have rewritten; the incumbent
\emph{changes what is served}, so \texttt{analysis/\allowbreak blocklist\_\allowbreak decode.py}
implements it as exact rejection sampling --- draw, test the last $n$ words, mask and redraw ---
which is the same distribution restricted to the allowed set, with the mask off as the control.
Against the LoRA memoriser on its own training split ($100$ passages, $10$-grams), the rule bound
$1.035\%$ of decode steps and took near-verbatim recall from $0.4192$ to $0.0201$ and \texttt{lcs\_word}
from $73.89$ to $7.86$. On ordinary prompts it fired on \textbf{$0$ of $850$} and the served text
was byte-identical to the control on all $850$, so its judged utility is the unconstrained model's
exactly: $+0.272$ $[+0.247, +0.2965]$ with the rule and $+0.272$ with it off. \textbf{Zero cost,
near-total suppression.} We registered a prediction that it would leak the \emph{non-literal} event
--- one substitution in ten completes no listed $n$-gram while the text stays a near copy, and
\texttt{tests/\allowbreak test\_\allowbreak blocklist\_\allowbreak decode.py} constructs exactly
that string --- and it is refuted: $0$ of $100$ passages reach ROUGE-L $\ge 0.5$ under the rule,
against $52$ without it. A non-adaptive memoriser does not find the path, so we claim the threat
and not a measurement of it.

This is a stronger incumbent than we implied by not measuring it. The honest summary is narrower
than ``it wins'', and we state it precisely because the tempting overcorrection is also false: on a
\emph{listed} work the blocklist costs \textbf{no utility at all}, which selection cannot approach,
and it does \textbf{not} suppress more --- under the same $20$-token seed protocol on the same
corpus, selection reads $0.0000$ near-verbatim recall at every $n \le 64$ against its own $k=-1$ of
$0.3925$, and the blocklist $0.0201$ against its own $0.4192$. What is left is a difference in kind, stated narrowly. The blocklist requires the deployer to
\emph{enumerate} the works; selection requires a safe model trained without them, and neither
premise dominates. The blocklist is silent on a work nobody listed, which is the case the
certificate covers. And it publishes no number about how far the served law has moved, so nothing
about it composes across queries, where $\log n$ adds exactly (Appendix~\ref{app:compose}).
Against that, selection costs $35.4\times$ the wall-clock as a ratio of totals and $67.2\times$ per
served request (Appendix~\ref{app:costgrid}) where a blocklist costs a hash lookup.
They are complements, not competitors: nothing stops a deployer running both, and a deployer who
runs only one should know which of the two questions it has answered.

\paragraph{Self-consistency, and why it is the instance that matters}
The cheapest instance of Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} is one nobody proposed for this purpose.
Self-consistency \citep{wang2023selfconsistency} samples $n$ reasoning paths and serves the majority
answer; a mode is a score, and the proposition assumes nothing whatever about the score, so
self-consistency satisfies $q(y) \le n\,p_s(y)$ and carries a pathwise certificate of exactly
$\log n$ against the model it samples from. That matters for three reasons. It is, as far as we can
tell, the most widely deployed inference-time decoding method in the field, so the property is already
running in production rather than proposed for adoption. It needs no reward model, which is where the
serving cost of the judged instance sits, and on a task with a checkable answer it beats every reward
cell we priced (Appendix~\ref{app:scorerfree}). And it is the sharpest form of our novelty claim: the
literature on best-of-$n$ prices $\log n$ as a \emph{cost} --- the budget spent before reward
overoptimisation \citep{gao2023scaling,beirami2025bestofn}, or the one an attacker spends to jailbreak
\citep{hughes2024bestofn} --- and the same inequality read in the other direction is a copyright
guarantee that the method's users already have and nobody has claimed. What we add is the reading, the
comparison against a metered decoder on one divergence axis, and the measurement; the mechanism is
theirs.
