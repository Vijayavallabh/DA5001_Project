\section{Limitations in full}\label{app:limitations}

\paragraph{One protected corpus}
Every extraction measurement runs on sixteen English genre novels, from CopyBench and BookMIA. The
robustness cells in Appendix~\ref{app:scaling} are nine re-analyses of those same works, so
$p = 3.1\times10^{-5}$ is the smallest value sixteen paired items can produce: read it as ``the
trend holds for every novel we have'', not as independent replication. Non-literal reproduction,
other languages and other genres are untested.

Two results were re-run on two disjoint corpora with everything but the protected work held fixed
(Appendix~\ref{app:collapse}). No order cell changed sign and the pair ranking survived; the onset did
not fare as well --- on the third corpus the ordering \emph{inverts} and the fine-tokenizer pair's
interval stops excluding $1$. What survives all three is the level, every onset landing within about
$1.3\times$ of $s(x)$; what does not is which pairs sit above the vacuity threshold. Two of the three
pairs vary across corpora by less than a third of their own within-corpus width and the third by two
and a half times it, so an onset ratio is reportable to its order of magnitude and no more finely.

A further arm bounds how much of a ratio belongs to its pair at all. Holding pair, corpus, anchor
and grid fixed and varying only the memoriser's fine-tuning length moves the ratio from $0.8756$ to
$1.3477$ --- $1.64\times$ the whole spread of the nine-pair table, enough to flip the sign of the
property the tokenizer split turns on. Four ladders then fix the epoch count and vary only the seed:
on the three with a strong memoriser the ratio moves by $0.033$, $0.066$ and $0.080$; on the fourth,
which barely clears our admission criterion of $0.10$ and whose stop-loss never fires, by $0.260$
over three seeds and $0.347$ over four. The ratio reproduces where the fine-tune converged and not
where it did not. That association is not shown to be causal, and not for want of trying: the first
pair's loss floors at $0.0222$ against a $0.02$ threshold, and the second goes from converging to
reproducing nothing across a $17\%$ change in learning rate. What moves the ratio is memoriser
strength, which the table holds fixed neither in training length --- its memorisers span a factor of
$5.1$ in sampled $k=-1$ --- nor in batch size, accumulation and stopping rule, nor in whether the
fine-tune converged at all, which five of the nine did not. We therefore report the table as
evidence that extraction begins near $s(x)$, which it supports, and not as evidence about which
pairs begin above it, which it does not. The seed-word gradient $\rho = -0.958$ is likewise a
correlation over nine differently-trained memorisers: re-drawing the ratios under the largest
re-seed noise we measured keeps its sign in every draw and $p<0.05$ in $92.3\%$ of them, but puts
its median at $-0.849$, so it is a rank ordering that survives and not a coefficient to two decimals
(Appendix~\ref{app:onset}).

\paragraph{More passages do not narrow the onset interval, and that is why several arms are undecided}
The bootstrap interval on onset\,/\,$s(x)$ has a mean width of $0.285$ across the pairs at $100$
passages each, and quadrupling the passages does not shrink it: on one pair under one metric the
width is $0.219$ at $n=100$ and $0.215$ at $n=458$, where independent sampling noise would predict
$0.102$. The variance is between \emph{works}, not between passages drawn from them, so it is
bounded below by the sixteen novels. The interventions in Section~\ref{sec:onset} therefore specify
bands about $0.1$ wide, narrower than this method can deliver, and several arms land undecided even
where the measured shift is large and in the predicted direction. What would fix that is more
distinct works, not more windows.

\paragraph{The onset ratio runs over a range, and we can name one cause but not the other}
$s(x)$ spans $1.87\times$ across the nine pairs in tokenizer-free units, short of the dynamic range
a data collapse normally rests on, and at the crossing budgets only one to four works in a hundred
leak. In units of each pair's own $s(x)$, the five pairs at about four characters per token overlap
freely around $0.90$; both KL3M pairs, at about two, exclude $1$; and the eighth pair's interval is
the widest of all and excludes nothing, while its own control, the ninth, is the narrowest. The
\emph{ordering} is what nine pairs establish, not the level of any one of them.

The KL3M pairs differ from the rest in three ways at once --- the tokenizer, targets twice as long in
tokens, and for one of them the weakest memoriser --- so pair six was built to remove one. It shares
the tokenizer and target length with pair five and memorises thoroughly ($s_r/s_s = 0.09$ against
$0.35$), and its ratio is nevertheless $1.053$: memorisation strength is not what puts a pair beyond
the vacuity point, though it modulates the ratio within a family, in the direction opposite to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:req}. That leaves the tokenizer confounded with everything the tokenizer determines, and
for a fixed corpus those are not separate variables --- a finer tokenizer changes the adversary's seed
($7.5$ words on the KL3M pairs against $13.9$--$15.0$), the decode steps before the passage is reached
($445$ against $197$--$230$) and the target length together, and all three order the nine pairs the way
the ratio is ordered. We can name the cause that is not memorisation strength; we cannot separate the
three the tokenizer moves at once.

\paragraph{The no-free-lunch theorem is a converse}
It lower-bounds the budget any policy needs, including the optimal one. Our decoder is causal and
meters with a token bucket, and we do not bound the gap between the two. That gap is large and
measurable --- a factor of $5$ to $32$ between the divergence on the risky model's own rollout and
the divergence paid on a budgeted one --- and closing it is the clearest open problem this paper
leaves.

\paragraph{What selection anchoring cannot do}
Six limits, of which the first is not a defect but the guarantee seen from the other side.

\emph{The support ceiling.} $q(y) \le n\,p_s(y)$ says a served string must be one the safe model
would have drawn, so the certificate and the quality ceiling are the same inequality: no $n$
produces text outside $p_s$'s support, and a deployer whose safe model cannot perform the task at
all gains nothing. That is the exact price of a bound that survives an adversarially chosen scorer,
and the structural difference from a metered decoder, which can serve text the anchor would never
produce and pays for it in a currency indexed to the protected work. At the scale we measure it does
not bind: the oracle arm reaches $u = 0.807$ among the same eight candidates where the deployed
scorers reach $0.52$, so the binding constraint at $n=8$ is the scorer, not the anchor.

\emph{Part of any gain could be a degeneracy filter, and is not.} Best-of-$n$ never selects an
empty completion, and the audited anchor emits one on $6.8\%$ of the $500$ prompts --- above the
$5\%$ its own admission criterion allows, recorded rather than exempted. On the $466$ prompts whose
$n=1$ completion is non-empty the gain is $+0.0536$ $[+0.0075, +0.0966]$ against $+0.0540$ on all
$500$; across the four anchors every gain moves by less than $0.003$ and every interval that
excluded zero still does. The empty fractions are $0.0\%$, $0.2\%$, $3.0\%$ and $6.8\%$, and the
anchor with the most of them is not the one with the largest gain.

\emph{The ceiling binds at the anchor, and we could not measure it within one.} On AlpacaEval's
$805$ instructions the audited anchor gains $+0.067$ $[+0.034, +0.101]$ under one judge and
$+0.031$ $[-0.001, +0.062]$ under the other, and MT-Bench's $80$ prompts resolve nothing in either
direction at a paired half-width of $0.09$. Two tests of the ceiling followed, on two axes, and they
do not agree.

\emph{Across anchors} it is confirmed: Comma-7B on the same $805$ prompts, the same unconstrained
opponent and the same judges gains $+0.075$ $[+0.042, +0.108]$ and $+0.167$ $[+0.134, +0.201]$,
monotone in $\log n$ in both --- roughly double the audited anchor's, with judge~B's interval taken
off zero, against a band fixed in advance asking for an excess of $0.03$. At the capable anchor the
standard benchmark is not harder than our own prompts: on judge~C its gain slightly exceeds the
in-house $+0.155$, so the prompt-set reading does not fire either.

\emph{Within one anchor} it is not measurable. Across AlpacaEval's five constituent sources and two
MT-Bench families, fixed before the split was computed, the gain does not track the anchor's control
level in either judge, and the $-0.79$ and $-0.70$ it does show are not separable from a
within-prompt exchangeability null in which selection does nothing, which already gives
$-0.36 \pm 0.34$ and $-0.34 \pm 0.35$ over $20{,}000$ draws, at $P = 0.09$ and $0.17$. Seven cells
cannot resolve this and we do not claim they do. The consistent reading is that the ceiling is a
property of the anchor's overall capability rather than of the domain it is asked about, which is
the direction of Spearman $+1.000$ between anchor-alone level and gain over the four anchors ---
four points, and descriptive.

\emph{Overoptimisation.} As $n$ grows the argmax is optimised harder against the scorer's own
errors, which for reward models is known to turn the gain over rather than saturate it
\citep{gao2023scaling}. Our extraction arm reaches $n=64$ and our judged arm $n=8$; whether judged
utility keeps rising to $n=64$ was measured against intervals fixed in advance partitioning the line
into a growing, a saturating and an overoptimising reading. Nothing in
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} prevents the third.

\emph{The scorer is a second model.} It cannot break the certificate --- that is the content of the
proposition --- but it can degrade utility arbitrarily, and a compromised or badly calibrated scorer
turns the mechanism into an expensive way to serve the worst of $n$ anchor samples. The failure is
visible in exactly the place a deployer would look, the served utility, and not in the certificate,
which is why the two must be monitored separately. The compute is $n$ draws and $n$ scoring passes
against the two forward passes per token a metered decoder makes; neither dominates in general, and
at the sizes used here the two are within a small factor.

\emph{The bound is relative to $p_s$.} $\Pr_q[E] \le n \Pr_{p_s}[E]$ is about the safe model's own
probability of emitting the work, so a safe model that has itself seen the work carries its
contamination straight through, multiplied by $n$. This is inherited from near-access-freeness and
is not specific to selection, but it is worth restating where the numbers are small enough to look
reassuring: the $0.0000$ of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} is $n$ times a base rate that is zero
\emph{because the anchor never saw these novels}, and it would not be zero for an anchor that had.

\paragraph{Two judges, one workload}
Utility is scored by instruction-tuned models on the benchmark's own prompt classes rather than
deployment traffic. Two judges at $600$ comparisons per arm agree on the sign everywhere and on
neither endpoint's magnitude: at $k=1$ one reads $-3.66\sigma$ against the anchor and the other
$+0.11\sigma$, at $k=10$ $-6.97\sigma$ against $-3.29\sigma$. The null arm calibrates that gap rather
than leaving it to argument --- served the \emph{unconstrained} risky model, the first judge separates
it at $-6.09\sigma$ and the second at $-2.49\sigma$, so the second is the coarser instrument --- and
that calibration is itself noisy, the same $500$ comparisons judged in an earlier run giving
$-5.47\sigma$ and $-1.49\sigma$, so about a $\sigma$ of run-to-run drift is the floor beneath which no
separation reported here should be read. What survives both is the pair of endpoints: neither judge
separates the decoder from the anchor at $k=0.5$, and both do at $k\ge10$. Table~\ref{tab:orders} is
at matched budget with $n=150$ judged pairs; Appendix~\ref{app:matched} repeats it at matched
\emph{utility} without a judge on twelve pairs in seven families, where the ranking is non-monotone
in $\alpha$, reverses on nine of them, and is predicted by nothing measured.

\paragraph{The two leakage measurements cannot be put on one axis, and we tried}
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the metered decoder's extraction onset and selection
anchoring's zero recall through two pipelines, and the released CSVs disagree about the one quantity
they share: the same memorising model, $100$ passages, $20$-token seeds and temperature read $0.4921$
and $0.3925$. A gate fixed in advance decided whether a joint table could honestly be built --- re-run
both arms at one generation length and require their two memoriser-alone rows to agree within $0.02$.
They do not. At a matched $300$ tokens the two read $0.4921$ and $0.4428$, a residual of $0.0493$, so
\textbf{no joint table is built} and no ratio is quoted across the two files. Neither pipeline is
wrong: generation length accounts for half the original discrepancy exactly as predicted, since at
$200$ tokens against a full-length reference near-verbatim recall is capped near $0.77$ by
construction, and the residual has two candidate causes the arm cannot separate ---
\texttt{generate} consumes the sampler's randomness per step, so changing the token budget changes
the draw as well as the length, and the metered arm decodes each passage's own target length while
the selection arm decodes a flat $300$. So every leakage number here is read against a control from
\emph{its own} pipeline, the two mechanisms' leakage is never divided one by the other, and the
comparison this paper makes between them is about what they \emph{spend}, which is measured on one
axis, and not what they leak, which is not.

\paragraph{The scorer is a stated requirement, not a free parameter}
Every reward arm in this paper scores with \texttt{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct}, and every reward
turn-over it reports is measured there: TriviaQA (Spearman $-0.607$, the $n=16$ interval excluding
zero on the wrong side) and CoTaEval news at four anchors (to $-0.1836$ at the headline anchor,
$-0.2334$ on a disjoint re-draw). \textbf{None of them survives a larger scorer.} Re-scoring the
same cached generations --- majority vote identical at all seven cells, so the text is
byte-identical and only the instrument changes --- a $14$B reward removes the TriviaQA fall and a
$72$B one reverses it, and at $72$B no CoTaEval anchor turns over while two climb
(Appendix~\ref{app:selection}). So selection anchoring under a pointwise reward carries a
\emph{scorer-size requirement} on knowledge-intensive tasks, and a deployer who ignores it can be
worse off at every $n$ than serving the anchor once.

We state it as a requirement rather than a repair for three reasons. The $7$B scorer is the
operating point of every other number in this paper, including the $35.4\times$ serving
measurement, so nothing measured there is revised. The larger scorers were run on the judge-free
tasks only; on the \emph{judged} workload the ladder stops at $7.6$B and its saturation reading is
unchanged, so the headline $+0.1045$ is not known to improve. And a larger scorer is a cost we did
not price: the reward pass is $9.3\%$ of selection's wall-clock and the draws $90.7\%$, which makes
the direction favourable, but no latency arm was run above $7$B and no number here should be read
as one. What is untouched either way is the certificate, which is $\log n$ for any score of any
size --- the property that makes Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} score-agnostic is exactly the
property that makes this rescoping possible without revising a single guarantee.
