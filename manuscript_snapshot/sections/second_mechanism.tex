\subsection{A second mechanism, and what composition is really about}\label{sec:cpfuse}
The protocol above is written for a divergence budget, so we applied it to a mechanism that has none. CP-Fuse~\cite{abad2025cpfuse} fuses two models fine-tuned on \emph{disjoint} splits of the protected corpus and reweights them at each step to keep the running log-likelihood of the output balanced, pulling down whichever model already explains the text too well. There is no anchor, no budget, and no $K$ to publish, so checks C1, C3 and C4 have nothing to bind to; C2 and C5 transfer unchanged. We reimplemented the reweighting from the paper's description --- not the authors' code, so the numbers below are not offered as a reproduction of their results --- and trained the two components with our own recipe on disjoint halves of the attack split, 229 passages each. The split is clean: each component reproduces $0.75$ to $0.88$ of the passages it was trained on and at most $0.03$ of the other's.

The mechanism holds, and it holds against the attack that breaks the budget. Under oracle-prefix windows of $50$ tokens, the strategy that lifts anchored decoding from $0.09$ to $0.23$ at $k=5$ and from $0.20$ to $0.41$ at $k=10$, CP-Fuse yields $0.022$ against its components' $0.81$ and $0.88$ on their own halves, and a single query yields nothing at all. That is the useful negative result: composition is not a general failure of per-query guarantees, it is a failure of \emph{budgeted} ones. A window of $L$ tokens carries its own allowance $kL$, and where that allowance exceeds the anchor's surprisal of the window the bank pays for the whole of it; CP-Fuse has no bank to drain, and windowing a stateless reweighting buys an adversary almost nothing. Most of that suppression is not the mechanism's own contribution, however, but the disjoint split it is built on; Appendix~\ref{app:tables} separates the two.
