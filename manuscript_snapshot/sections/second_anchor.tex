\section{A second pair, built so that memorisation is the only difference}\label{sec:second-anchor}

Every attack in the earlier audit used one anchor, because the mechanism fuses two next-token
distributions and no other permissively trained model shared the risky model's vocabulary. The
recorded obstacle was a mismatch of $64{,}000$ against $128{,}256$ tokens. That was not the
obstacle. The implementation required the embedding table to have exactly $\mathrm{len}(\text{tokenizer})$
rows, and the Common Pile $7$B checkpoint pads $64{,}000$ tokens to $64{,}256$ rows, as most model
families outside Llama-3 do. Permitting a padded table and masking the untrained rows out of the
solve --- they enter neither $Z(\theta)$, the charge, nor the sample --- removes the restriction
without changing anything on an unpadded model.

That makes a cleaner experiment available than a second anchor of the usual kind. We fine-tune the
$7$B Common Pile model until it reproduces the protected passages, and fuse that against the
\emph{unmodified} model it was fine-tuned from. Anchor and risky model then share an architecture,
a corpus, a tokenizer and every weight except those the memorisation moved, so memorisation is the
only variable; in the original pair it is confounded with a change of corpus, scale and training
recipe. The memoriser reaches $0.915$ near-verbatim recall on the passages it was trained on and
$0.000$ on the held-out split, so the split is genuinely held out.

Across $9$ budgets, $5$ strategies and $31{,}640$ budgeted queries there are no invariant
violations, as in the original audit. Three things are sharper on this pair:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The anchor is clean.} Serving the safe model alone reproduces $0.0000$ of a passage
      under every strategy. In the original pair the anchor alone already recovers $0.0032$, so a
      small part of what looked like leakage through the decoder was the anchor's own corpus.
\item \textbf{At $k=20$ the decoder is exactly the identity.} Single-query recall is $0.7189$,
      equal to the unconstrained model's $0.7189$ to four decimals, and oracle windows reach
      $0.9407$ against $0.9435$. This is a budget the mechanism's authors evaluate.
\item \textbf{The uncertified interval reproduces.} The ratio $k_{\mathrm{crit}}/s(x)$ is $4.73$
      here against $4.30$ for the original pair, on different models, a different tokenizer and a
      target set the anchor has never seen.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where leakage begins}
The certificate goes vacuous above $s(x) = 2.39$ nats per token for these targets, and reproduction
is provably impossible below $k_{\mathrm{crit}} = 11.32$. Measured recall is $0.000$ at every budget
up to $k=1$, $0.048$ at $k=3$ and $0.197$ at $k=5$. Leakage therefore starts far below
$k_{\mathrm{crit}}$ and close to $s(x)$: the budget at which the certificate stops saying anything
is a good predictor of the budget at which the mechanism starts leaking, while the budget at which
the \emph{whole} work becomes reproducible is four to five times higher. The same holds for the
original pair, where $s(x) = 3.24$ and recall first becomes measurable between $k=1$ and $k=3$.

Proposition~\ref{prop:outrun} is a statement about regime boundaries and not a per-work test.
The per-passage correlation between predicted and observed reproducible fraction is only $0.16$ to
$0.29$, and $-0.033$ at $k=20$; Appendix~\ref{app:collapse} gives the detail.
