\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Language models reproduce passages of their training data, and for copyrighted data that is a legal
exposure rather than a benchmark curiosity: a $70$B open-weights model returns $50$-token windows of
a popular novel on roughly a quarter of prompts drawn from it \citep{cooper2025extracting}, and
retraining it away is impractical against a stream of takedowns. Certified decoders offer something
cheaper: bound, at decode time, how far the output may drift from a \emph{safe} model that
never saw the work. The drift is charged in nats, banked in a token bucket, and published as a
single $k$ --- anchored decoding \citep{he2026anchored} instantiating
$k$-near-access-freeness \citep{vyas2023provable} --- and the appeal is that $k$ looks like
$\varepsilon$ in differential privacy: a dial a deployer sets and a number a rights-holder reads.

\textbf{This paper shows the budget is in the wrong place, and moves it.} Draw $n$ completions from
the safe model, score them by anything at all, and serve the best. Every token emitted is still a
safe-model token, so there is nothing to meter at any step; the whole budget is spent once, on
\emph{which draw to serve}. The served law then satisfies $q(y) \le n\,p_s(y)$ --- three lines,
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} --- a \emph{pathwise} certificate of exactly $\log n$ nats, which
bounds every event rather than an average, is the strongest R\'enyi order in the family the deployed
meters charge in, and is constant in the length of the work where $K = kT$ is linear in it. Nothing
in it mentions a reward, so self-consistency \citep{wang2023selfconsistency} is an instance: the
field's most widely deployed decoding method already carries a copyright certificate, and a better
one than the mechanism built for the purpose.

Figure~\ref{fig:selection} is the paper's headline. Where a metered decoder spends a measured
$171.3$ nats to become judged better than its own safe model, sixty-four draws spend at most
$3.175$ and win by $2.6\times$ $[1.5, 7.5]$ against that opponent --- one fifty-fourth of the divergence, every
item shown in both presentation orders so that position is removed by construction. At $n=8$ it holds at four of six anchors in three families and on
AlpacaEval, and with no judge in the loop at all.

That the guarantee holds against the \emph{adversary} is what makes it a security claim rather than
an average-case one. Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} assumes nothing about the score, so a
malicious one multiplies the safe model's own probability of emitting a protected work by at most
$n$ and introduces none that model would not have produced: handed the selector, an attacker
scoring by the \emph{memorising} model's own likelihood recovers $0.0000$ at every $n \le 64$ and
at $n=256$, on all $100$ passages. Where the premise fails, the bound degrades gracefully: across
twelve deliberately contaminated anchors the realised amplification at $n=64$ runs between $1.0$
and $4.0$, against the $64$ the certificate permits.

\paragraph{Why this was available: a per-token \emph{rate} cannot be repaired to match it.}
The natural objection is that a meter could be fixed --- charge more sharply, cap more tightly,
schedule more cleverly. Not while it charges a rate, and the reason is one line of the chain rule.
For any policy the sequence divergence is \emph{exactly} the sum of its per-step divergences, so one
holding to a sequence budget $K$ can depart from the safe model by more than $\varepsilon$ at no more
than $K/\varepsilon$ steps in expectation (Proposition~\ref{prop:sparse}). A per-token allowance is
issued at every step whether or not that step needs one, which leaves two horns. \emph{Either} the
allowance grows with the work --- $K = kT$ against a work worth $S(x) = s(x)T$, so
$K/S(x) \to k/s(x)$ and never improves --- \emph{or} it is bounded, and can then be spent at only
$O(1)$ of the decoder's steps. \textbf{Vacuous, or sparse --- and sparse is a shape, not a verdict:}
selection's own served law obeys the same bound, four steps of two hundred carrying a nat at
$n=64$, and gains $+0.1045$ out of that budget. What the chain rule forbids is \emph{spreading} a
bounded budget, which is what a rate does by construction, and concentrating one it permits --- the
whole gap selection anchoring walks through. It also explains
why the gap was
still open: the deployed class takes the vacuous horn by construction, since a token-bucket decoder
serving the largest feasible tilt spends, wherever the bucket is slack, the full
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_r \| p_s)$ of that step --- the cost of \emph{imitating} the risky model,
$\Theta(T)$ and independent of the utility achieved (Proposition~\ref{prop:imitation}). Over
$16{,}500$ logged trajectories realised spend stalls at $171.3$ nats while the cap quadruples. The three repairs are then corollaries, and
Section~\ref{sec:orders} tests and fails all three: a sharper charge cannot move a threshold that is
order-invariant, a smaller cap buys nothing two judges can separate from the safe model it wraps,
and a better schedule is bounded by the same chain rule. They share a measurable cause --- the
currency a metered decoder buys with its nats predicts judged quality at AUC $0.526$, no better than the
answer's length at $0.537$.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt,parsep=0pt]
\item \textbf{A copyright certificate that does not grow with the work.} A selection rule's whole
budget is a pathwise $\log n$ under any score, tie rule and safe model
(Proposition~\ref{prop:selection}), read against an order-invariant vacuity threshold at
$K = S(x)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold}).
\item \textbf{A theorem that a per-token \emph{rate} cannot have one.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:sparse} leaves a rate vacuous, or else bounded and therefore sparse --- a
shape a rate cannot take --- and Theorem~\ref{thm:nfl} prices the utility side of the same budget.
\item \textbf{It works, and it survives an adversarial scorer}
(Figures~\ref{fig:selection}, \ref{fig:breadth} and~\ref{fig:safety}): zero near-verbatim recall at every
$n \le 64$ and at $n=256$ --- a bound \emph{relative} to the anchor, so the anchor is vetted and not
assumed --- against a conceded $61.3\times$ forward-pass cost that a measured clock revises rather
than excuses (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item \textbf{The threshold is tied to something a rights-holder would recognise}: extraction sits
at $0.88$--$1.17$ of $s(x)$ across nine pairs with nine distinct anchors.
\end{itemize}
None of this contradicts the mechanisms' own theorems --- no per-trajectory violation occurs in
$100{,}000$ budgeted trajectories --- the gap is between what they bound and what the published
number is taken to mean.
