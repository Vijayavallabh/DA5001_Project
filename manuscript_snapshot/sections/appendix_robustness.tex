\paragraph{The measurement Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} plots}\label{sec:scaling}
Ten safe models spanning $0.17$B to $7$B parameters, and three independently curated, openly
licensed corpora: KL3M (US public-domain legal text), Pleias~1.0 (Common Corpus) and Comma (the
Common Pile), the last including the $1$T- and $2$T-token checkpoints of one $7$B model, which vary
training data at fixed capacity. Every model scores the same $758$ protected passages, the same $50$
public-domain control books, and the same $300$ generations the risky model produced on ordinary
instruction prompts. Rates are nats per character throughout: total string log-probability is
tokenizer-invariant and these models carry $64$k, $128$k and $152$k vocabularies, so no shared
vocabulary is needed and the measurement is not restricted to models fusable with the risky one.
Across the widest span in each family (Table~\ref{tab:marginspan}):

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering\small
\caption{The margin $s(x)/c_{\mathrm{use}}$ across the widest capability span in each family, paired over the same sixteen novels.}\label{tab:marginspan}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrrr@{}}
\toprule
corpus & span & $\Delta c_{\mathrm{use}}$ & $\Delta s(x)$ & margin & sign test \\
\midrule
Common Corpus & $0.35 \to 3$B   & $-25.8\%$ & $-9.8\%$  & $3.45 \to 4.19$ & $16/16$, $p = 3.1\times10^{-5}$ \\
KL3M          & $0.17 \to 3.7$B & $-31.3\%$ & $-16.0\%$ & $1.94 \to 2.37$ & $16/16$, $p = 3.1\times10^{-5}$ \\
Common Pile   & $1.8 \to 7$B    & $-28.6\%$ & $-12.0\%$ & $4.07 \to 5.02$ & $16/16$, $p = 3.1\times10^{-5}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Because every model scores the same novels and the same generations the comparison is paired, and the
margin $s(x)/c_{\mathrm{use}}$ rises for all sixteen novels in all three families; it also rises with
training tokens at fixed capacity, $4.63$ to $5.02$ from the $1$T to the $2$T checkpoint of the same
$7$B model. The mechanism is unsurprising once stated: a better safe model closes the gap to the
risky model faster on generic instruction-following output than on a particular novel, because the
novel is idiosyncratic and the assistant register is not. Both readings of the earlier audit's
observation belong together --- in absolute terms the range of budgets over which the certificate
says anything shrinks, so a deployer who fixed $k$ in nats finds it going vacuous under a better
anchor; in relative terms the two rates separate --- and only the second is available to a deployer
who does not know the protected works in advance.

Two questions, and on this corpus both bands fire. The onset ratio moves by at most $0.05$ from its
twin on the novels, the fine-tokenizer pair is again the only one above $1$ and its interval again
excludes $1$. And Eq.~\eqref{eq:req}, which fails as a direction, places the onset to within
$0.85$--$1.04$ of the measurement on all three --- no worse than the $0.865$--$1.076$ it manages on
the corpus it was derived on. Its low-quantile prediction misses on all three, the onset landing
above the median of $r(x)$ where on the novels it sat at $q_{25}$; and the three-pair direction test
returns exact $p = 1.000$, the floor at $n=3$, written down as such before the runs.

An earlier version of this appendix concluded from those two agreements that leakage beginning after
the certificate has gone vacuous is a property of the pair. \emph{A third corpus refutes that
conclusion}, and we report it in place of the claim it removes.

The ordering inverts. Pleias-1.2B, a coarse-tokenizer pair that sat at $0.878$ and $0.895$ on the
first two corpora, is now the highest of the three at $1.314$, above the fine-tokenizer KL3M pair;
and KL3M's interval, which excluded $1$ on both earlier corpora ($[1.02, 1.24]$ and $[1.07, 1.42]$),
no longer does. That exclusion was the whole evidential basis for the sentence retracted above, so
the sentence goes. Eq.~\eqref{eq:req} fails here too: prediction over measurement is $0.891$ and
$1.004$ on two pairs and $0.652$ on Pleias-1.2B, outside the $[0.7, 1.4]$ fixed before the run. Nor
is Pleias-1.2B's reading a grid-ceiling artefact, which is what the grid-ceiling rule --- a bootstrapped
crossing must report its no-crossing fraction --- exists to interrogate: its no-crossing fraction read $43.1\%$ against $0.0\%$ for the other two on a
grid whose ceiling its upper bound sat $0.056$ below, and extending that pair alone to
$k \in \{4.6, 5.3, 6.6\}$ --- multipliers fixed before the run --- moved the no-crossing to $0.4\%$,
widened the upper end from $1.360$ to $1.594$ and left the onset \emph{unmoved} at $4.006$.

Three readings of the same three pairs also permit a comparison two cannot: each pair's
corpus-to-corpus range against the sampling spread \emph{within} one corpus, for which the protocol
fixed each pair's own CopyBench bootstrap width as the yardstick. KL3M-520M and Phi-3.5-mini vary
across three protected corpora by $0.088$ and $0.066$, which is $2.6\times$ and $4.3\times$
\emph{inside} their widths of $0.227$ and $0.287$; Pleias-1.2B varies by $0.436$ against a width of
$0.171$. For two of three pairs the onset ratio is a property of the pair and the corpus is not a
material term; for the third it is, and every ratio we quote for that pair carries the corpus it was
measured on. The obvious rival explanation is that the memoriser moved rather than the corpus:
Pleias-1.2B's is much the weakest here, its sampled $k=-1$ recall falling
$0.909 \to 0.517 \to 0.150$ while its ratio rises $0.878 \to 0.895 \to 1.314$. Over all nine
(pair, corpus) cells, however, the rank correlation between memoriser strength and onset ratio is
$-0.317$ at exact $p = 0.41$ --- post hoc, with no band fixed in advance, and not significant. Since
a memoriser is fine-tuned on the corpus it is measured against, this design cannot separate the two:
a limitation of the design, not a defence of the claim.

Holding that pair, corpus, anchor and grid fixed and varying only the memoriser's fine-tuning length
moves the onset ratio from $0.8756$ to $1.3477$, and re-seeding the same recipe at a fixed epoch
count moves it by $0.033$ to $0.080$ where the fine-tune converged and by $0.2597$ over three seeds
where the memoriser is marginal. \textbf{The within-pair spread is $1.64\times$ the entire
between-pair spread} of $0.8784$ to $1.1658$, it reproduces both ends of the nine-pair range on its
own, and the property the tokenizer split turns on --- whether a pair's interval excludes $1$ ---
flips sign inside this single pair, from $[0.68, 0.98]$ at the strongest memoriser to
$[1.01, 1.59]$ at the weakest, the two intervals not overlapping. The registered rank correlation
between measured strength and ratio is \emph{inconclusive}: the span fired at $0.4721$ against a
specified $\ge 0.15$, but $\rho = -0.600$ did not reach the $\le -0.8$ (exact $p = 0.42$ at $n=4$),
and pooling the seven points specified in advance gives $\rho = -0.714$ at exact $p = 0.0881$ ---
the direction, and not a demonstration. We tried to promote the convergence half of that reading to
a cause and \emph{neither intervention could be built}: the non-converged cell's loss floors at
$0.0222$, above the recipe's stop-loss of $0.02$, and the converged one goes from a memoriser at
$0.855$ to reproducing \emph{nothing} across a $17\%$ increase in learning rate.

This is not a defect of the nine-pair table but a limit on what it can support, because the
variables that demonstrably move the ratio are ones it never controlled: its nine memorisers are
not matched on training length --- sampled $k=-1$ runs from $0.181$ to $0.924$, a factor of $5.1$
--- nor on hyperparameters, two of them trained at
$\mathrm{batch}\,2/\mathrm{accum}\,4$ with stop-loss $0.02$ and $\mathrm{batch}\,4/\mathrm{accum}\,2$
with stop-loss $0.03$; nor on convergence, four of the nine reaching their stop-loss and five not,
and restricting to the converged four does not recover a per-pair reading, since those four still
span $0.1748$ and interleave in rank with the five that did not. The table is evidence that
extraction begins near $s(x)$, which is what Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} claims; it is not
evidence about which pairs begin above $1$ and which below, because that ordering is confounded
with training variables it does not hold fixed. The level survives untouched: every onset in these
ladders lands between $0.80$ and $1.35$ of $s(x)$.

\paragraph{The vacuity statement on a hundred books}\label{app:bookmia}
The onset needs memorising models and so is confined to corpora we can fine-tune against. The
\emph{vacuity} statement needs neither: $S(x)$ and the verdict $S(x) \le K$ are forward passes of
the anchor alone. We therefore re-measured it on BookMIA \citep{shi2024detecting}, the $50$ books it
marks as seen ($4{,}935$ passages) and the $50$ it marks as unseen ($4{,}935$), against the $758$
CopyBench passages re-run in the same pass as a positive control (Table~\ref{tab:bookmia}).
Bands were fixed in advance.

Three readings. The statement survives a thirteenfold larger corpus and six times as many books:
$65$ of $4{,}935$ seen passages are surprising enough that a $600$-nat budget does not cover them,
which is the WEAKER reading rather than the $\ge 0.99$ that would have been a replication, and the
unseen half and the control are both at $1.0000$. The median per-token rate is $3.114$ against the
novels' $3.197$, $2.6\%$ apart, so fifty books of a different provenance put the anchor in the same
surprisal regime. And the seen/unseen halves are \emph{not} exchangeable under an anchor that saw
neither: their medians differ by $6.0\%$ against a specified $5\%$, so BookMIA's label is entangled
with a text property --- the books a target model memorised are the widely reproduced ones, whose
prose is more predictable to any model. No arm here treats the halves as a matched pair.

The control earned its place. A first pass scored vacuity as $k \ge s(x)$ per token --- the
asymptotic form Figure~\ref{fig:selection}(a) draws, where $K$ and $S(x)$ are both rays in the work
length --- and read $0.326$ on the same $758$ passages the deployed criterion puts at $1.000$. The
criterion was wrong, not the corpus: the two agree only when the decoder's cap and the work are the
same length, and a deployment sets $T_{\max} = 200$ against a protected span near $60$ tokens, so the
published budget covers more than three times the work being targeted.

\section{The order comparison at matched utility}\label{app:matched}
The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:orders} is at matched \emph{budget}, because the budget is what a
deployer publishes and what a certificate quotes. It is not at matched utility, and
\S\ref{sec:orders} shows the judge cannot supply the missing axis: re-judging the same generations
with the same judge inverts the ranking. The geodesic supplies it without a judge. Fidelity
$G(\theta) = \theta\psi'(1) - \psi(\theta) = -D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_r \Vert p_\theta) + \mathrm{const}$
is what a metered decoder's budget buys, and it is monotone in $k$ at a fixed order, so for every
order there is a unique budget at which it buys exactly what the audited decoder buys at the
published one. What it lets through there is \emph{not} another average: verbatim reproduction is a
rare event, so the second quantity is
$L(\alpha,k) = \sum_t \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ over the protected tokens, whose exponential
is the reproduction probability. Both are deterministic and need no sampling. A $12$-point grid of
$k$ on $25$ memorised passages and $25$ ordinary generations, on twelve pairs in seven families (Table~\ref{tab:orderlaw}):

Each cell is the factor by which an exact $50$-token window becomes \emph{less} likely under that
order, at the budget $k_\alpha$ where it buys exactly what $\alpha{=}1$ buys at the published $k$.
Writing $\ell(\alpha,k) = L(\alpha,k)/T$ for the mean per-token log-probability, it is
$\exp\{W[\ell(1,k) - \ell(\alpha,k_\alpha)]\}$ with $W = 50$ --- a geometric mean over windows, so it
scales exponentially in $W$, fixed at $50$ because that is what Table~\ref{tab:orders} scores. Below
$1$ the order leaks \emph{more} at equal utility. $s(x)$ is the anchor's surprisal rate on the same tokens, in nats per token. Every run is in
\texttt{bfloat16} so the ranking is not confounded by precision, and every pair was also run in
\texttt{float32} as its own control. Two further pairs were built and \emph{excluded}: Pleias-3B,
whose memoriser diverged at two learning rates where Pleias-350M and Pleias-1.2B memorise the same
excerpts, and a self-paired Llama-3.1-8B, which would have shared its memoriser exactly with the
TinyComma pair.

Three readings. At the budget the mechanism's authors publish, $k=3$, the audited decoder already
captures $94.9$ to $99.8\%$ of the fidelity an unlimited budget would buy, so matching it forces
every other order past that pair's vacuity threshold; that column group compares operating points at
which the certificate already says nothing, and on nine of the twelve pairs the matched-budget
ranking \emph{reverses} there. At $k=1$, where the constraint binds and the matched budgets stay
between $1.6$ and $5.3$ nats per token, the higher order usually helps --- but at $\alpha{=}4$ by
$1.0\times$ on one pair and $2.5\times10^{7}$ on another, and on four pairs $\alpha{=}8$ at matched
utility makes the protected tokens \emph{more} likely, by up to $1.3\times10^{6}$ on TinyComma-1.8B
with a memorised Llama-3.1-8B, the only pair whose anchor and risky model are different models and so
the mechanism's own configuration. The mechanism is visible in the grid: a higher order charges
something closer to the worst step, so buying the same \emph{average} fidelity costs it a much larger
budget --- $5.25$ against $1.0$ on that pair --- and that budget is spent tilting toward the
memoriser on the steps that carry the passage.
