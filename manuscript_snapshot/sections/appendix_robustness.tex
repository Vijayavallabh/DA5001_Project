\section{Robustness of the scaling trend}\label{app:scaling}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{frontier_scaling.pdf}
\caption{The two rates a deployer must separate, measured without any mechanism. Ten safe models,
three independently curated openly licensed corpora. \textbf{Left:} the safe model's surprisal rate
on the 758 protected passages, $s(x)$, and the rate at which ordinary risky-model output diverges
from the same safe model, $c_{\mathrm{use}}$, both in nats per character. Both fall as the safe
model improves; $c_{\mathrm{use}}$ falls faster. \textbf{Right:} their ratio rises monotonically
with capability in every family, and for all sixteen novels individually
($p = 3.1\times10^{-5}$ per family, paired sign test). The table and both controls are below.}
\label{fig:frontier}

\paragraph{The measurement Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} plots}\label{sec:scaling}
Ten safe models spanning $0.17$B to $7$B parameters, and three independently curated, openly
licensed corpora: KL3M (US public-domain legal text), Pleias~1.0 (Common Corpus) and Comma (the
Common Pile), the last including the $1$T- and $2$T-token checkpoints of one $7$B model, which vary
training data at fixed capacity. Every model scores the same $758$ protected passages, the same $50$
public-domain control books, and the same $300$ generations the risky model produced on ordinary
instruction prompts. Rates are nats per character throughout: total string log-probability is
tokenizer-invariant and these models carry $64$k, $128$k and $152$k vocabularies, so no shared
vocabulary is needed and the measurement is not restricted to models fusable with the risky one.
Across the widest span in each family:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrrr@{}}
\toprule
corpus & span & $\Delta c_{\mathrm{use}}$ & $\Delta s(x)$ & margin & sign test \\
\midrule
Common Corpus & $0.35 \to 3$B   & $-25.8\%$ & $-9.8\%$  & $3.45 \to 4.19$ & $16/16$, $p = 3.1\times10^{-5}$ \\
KL3M          & $0.17 \to 3.7$B & $-31.3\%$ & $-16.0\%$ & $1.94 \to 2.37$ & $16/16$, $p = 3.1\times10^{-5}$ \\
Common Pile   & $1.8 \to 7$B    & $-28.6\%$ & $-12.0\%$ & $4.07 \to 5.02$ & $16/16$, $p = 3.1\times10^{-5}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Because every model scores the same novels and the same generations the comparison is paired, and
the margin $s(x)/c_{\mathrm{use}}$ rises for all sixteen novels in all three families; it also rises
with training tokens at fixed capacity, $4.63$ to $5.02$ from the $1$T to the $2$T checkpoint of the
same $7$B model. The mechanism is unsurprising once stated: a better safe model closes the gap to
the risky model faster on generic instruction-following output than on a particular novel, because
the novel is idiosyncratic and the assistant register is not. Both readings of the earlier audit's
observation belong together: in absolute terms the range of budgets over which the certificate says
anything shrinks, so a deployer who fixed $k$ in nats finds it going vacuous under a better anchor;
in relative terms the two rates separate, and only the second reading is available to a deployer who
does not know the protected works in advance.

\paragraph{What the margin does not license}\label{sec:price}
The margin says how far apart the two rates are, not that a budget between them leaves utility
intact: the rate a decoder actually pays is not $c_{\mathrm{use}}$ but a rate $5$ to $32$ times
smaller, measured across six prompt classes and two budgets (Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).
Cheap-step selection does not account for that gap: the decoder pays more per unaltered step than
the cheapest matching fraction of the unconstrained distribution would, because it walks a different
trajectory, and its price is a property of that trajectory --- the approximation gap
Theorem~\ref{thm:nfl} leaves open.

\paragraph{Exposure, not just fluency}
A safe model could look good on protected text merely by being bad at prose. The public-domain
control books are inside all three permissive corpora while the protected passages are inside none,
and on identical $1{,}500$-character spans the ratio of control to protected surprisal is $0.630$ to
$0.887$ for every model, so the separation is not an artifact of general fluency. That ratio
tightens with scale on the Common Pile ($0.743 \to 0.630$), which is what growing memorisation of
its own corpus would look like; on the other two families it is flat within noise, so we do not
claim the trend generally. The control also does not separate exposure from era and genre, since
the control books are older than the protected novels.

\paragraph{A unit a deployer can compute without the protected work}
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} tells a deployer to publish $k/s(x)$, and the difficulty with that
advice is immediate: a rights-holder's work is exactly what a deployer has not
seen, so only a rate quoted against ordinary traffic is available to them. There is a third option
the measurement above already contains. The anchor's surprisal rate on \emph{public-domain} prose
--- 50 Gutenberg texts, scored on the same $1{,}500$-character spans --- needs no protected text at
all and takes one forward pass. Predicting a held-out anchor's protected rate from it, with a single
constant fitted on the other nine:

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
predictor for $s(x)$ & leave-one-anchor-out error & against the constant \\
\midrule
public-domain proxy, rescaled & $\mathbf{0.052}$ nats/char ($5.6\%$) & $\mathbf{4.13\times}$ better \\
a constant, no rescaling      & $0.214$ & --- \\
$c_{\mathrm{use}}$, rescaled  & $0.402$ & $1.9\times$ worse \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

over ten anchors whose protected rate spans $1.86\times$; the ratio $s(x)/s_{\text{proxy}}$ is
$1.141 \pm 0.077$ (coefficient of variation $6.75\%$). So the unit the budget should be quoted in is
estimable to about $6\%$ from text a deployer is free to hold, which is the same order as the
$0.878$--$0.926$ spread of the onset ratio itself. Two caveats. $c_{\mathrm{use}}$ is not a
substitute --- it does worse than not rescaling at all, which is why the margin
$s(x)/c_{\mathrm{use}}$ tells a deployer whether a useful budget exists and not where to put it. And
the $1.141$ is a genre-and-era gap, structured by family ($1.06$--$1.09$ on KL3M, $1.13$--$1.16$ on
Pleias, $1.25$--$1.29$ on Comma); fitting per family would be tighter and is unavailable, since
knowing the family constant needs the protected works. Our proxy is older public-domain fiction and
the protected set is modern genre novels; a deployer whose corpus is not prose fiction has no reason
to expect the constant to carry, and we have not tested one.

\paragraph{The base-versus-instruct confound, measured}
Every safe model measured above is a base model and the risky model is
instruction-tuned, so $c_{\rm use}$ mixes the corpus difference with the base-versus-instruct
difference. Repeating the whole measurement against two further risky models separates them: a
$7$B instruction-tuned model from an unrelated family, and a $1$B \emph{base} model, for which safe
and risky are the same kind of object. With a base risky model $c_{\rm use}$ roughly halves and
every margin rises, so the confound inflates $c_{\rm use}$ and the margins reported in the main
text are the conservative end of the range. The trend itself does not move: in all nine
combinations of corpus family and risky model, the margin rises for $16$ of $16$ novels,
$p = 3.1\times10^{-5}$.

\paragraph{Three checks the collapse has to survive}
Sharp behaviour is often manufactured by a thresholded metric~\citep{schaeffer2023mirage}, so we
repeat the collapse in the longest-common-substring length, which is continuous and logged
alongside recall: across the pairs the spread is $0.106$ of that metric's range against
$0.109$ for the thresholded recall, so whatever the curves do, the threshold is not doing it. The onset definition is arbitrary, so we sweep it, and the answer weakens as pairs are
added. The spread in onset$/s(x)$ is $0.335$, $0.287$ and $0.411$ at
thresholds $0.005$, $0.01$ and $0.02$, and the absolute level runs from $0.928$ to $1.076$. The
spread is dominated by the two KL3M pairs at every threshold, so the choice of threshold is not
what puts them on the far side of the vacuity point; at $100$ passages a single work still moves a pair's mean recall by
about $0.01$, which is why we quote intervals rather than a band.
Finally, Eq.~\eqref{eq:req} makes its own prediction about which rescaling should work best, and it
can be checked, and the answer is that it cannot be. Ablating the normaliser gives $s(x)$ against
$r$ as $0.0059$ against $0.0046$ on two pairs, $0.0070$ against $0.0096$ on three, $0.0180$ against
$0.0143$ on four, $0.0264$ against $0.0215$ on five, $0.0266$ against $0.0254$ on six and $0.0268$
against $0.0262$ on seven: the winner
changes every time a pair is added. On four pairs both beat the $0.033$ of not rescaling at all by
about $2\times$; on nine the two rescalings sit within $3\%$ of each other ($0.0268$ and $0.0262$)
and not rescaling costs $0.0370$ --- but on a raw-budget window three grid points wide against the
rescaled window's five, because nine grids overlap less in $k$ than in $k/s(x)$, so that last
number is not measured on the same window as the other two. The comparison that \emph{is} on the
same footing is the rank spread over all nine pairs, where the coefficient of variation of the
onset under each normaliser is $10.2\%$ for $s(x)$, $15.0\%$ for no rescaling, $23.8\%$ for $r$ and
$37.7\%$ for $k_{\mathrm{crit}}$: $s(x)$ is the best of the four.
This ablation is not a way to choose between normalisers, and past four pairs it is not evidence
for having one.

One normaliser the ablation could not reach is the one this paper's own
Proposition~\ref{prop:outrun} defines. The overlap window in $k/k_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is empty on
these grids, so we rank the pairs instead, and the answer is sharp in both directions. The
per-pair burstiness $k_{\mathrm{crit}}/s(x)$ --- the gap Proposition~\ref{prop:outrun} names
between the drift a budget publishes and the workload maximum safety depends on --- does \emph{not}
explain the onset split: pre-registered with a refuting band before it was computed, it gives
Spearman $\rho = +0.036$ at exact $p = 0.96$ over the seven pairs it was committed on --- and
$-0.050$ over all nine --- where the derivation needs it positive and large. The two
\emph{burstiest} pairs by a factor of three ($4.30$ and $4.73$ against $1.54$--$1.96$) sit at onset
ratios of $0.887$ and $0.892$, mid-range, while the two highest ratios belong to the second and
third \emph{least} bursty. But the same ranking answers the question the ablation was asking:
across the pairs the coefficient of variation of onset$/k_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is $0.377$ against
$0.102$ for onset$/s(x)$, so the running maximum is $3.7\times$ the worse unit for where leakage \emph{begins} even though it is the right one for when
a work becomes \emph{reproducible}. The two propositions describe different boundaries, and this is
the measurement that separates them rather than asserting it.

\paragraph{Why not use the whole distribution?}
Rescaling by one number per pair discards the shape of the anchor's surprisal distribution, and the
law can be restated with nothing fitted in it at all: let $F(k)$ be the fraction of protected
objects whose anchor surprisal rate is at most $k$, and ask whether recall is a common function of
$F$. It is not, and the comparison says what the constant is about. At the measured onset the
budget covers $0.07$, $0.09$, $0.05$, $0.03$, $0.98$, $0.83$, $0.23$, $0.67$ and $0.49$ of the
passages, a relative spread (standard deviation over mean) of $91\%$, against $10\%$ for the ratio
to the median. The
two near unity are the affordability point of Section~\ref{sec:onset} at its limit: budgets that can pay for almost every
passage while almost none leaks. The unit is the surprisal of a typical protected work,
not the size of the tail a budget covers. The same construction also fails to rescue the oracle
curves. Charged per $50$-token window, the oracle attack ought to collapse against the window-level
$F$ even though it does not collapse against the work-level $s(x)$; instead, at its onset
essentially no whole window is covered on the four pairs at four characters per token
($F \le 0.009$), so window leakage begins well before the budget can pay for any window outright.

\paragraph{The eighth anchor was chosen to fall between the groups, its ratio was predicted, and the ninth controls it}
Six pairs put the onset ratio in two clusters with nothing in between, and the obvious reading ---
that the adversary's context drives it --- could not be tested, because the quantity that sets that
context is characters per token and no anchor we held cut these passages between $2.4$ and $3.4$.
That was a statement about our cache. Scoring $21$ ungated, openly licensed causal LMs on the same
passages, downloading tokenizer files and no weights, finds the region sparse rather than empty:
sixteen sit at $3.49$--$4.18$, four at $1.22$--$2.36$, and \emph{exactly one} between, at $2.71$
(\texttt{results/tokenizer\_survey.csv}). The four fine ones all have non-English-centric
vocabularies --- Korean and character-level Japanese --- while above $3.4$ both kinds appear, so
provenance predicts the fine group and not the coarse one. The one in the gap is
\texttt{cyberagent/open-calm-1b}. The same table quantifies the point
Section~\ref{sec:onset} makes about seeds: a fixed $20$-token seed buys between $6.2$ and $15.8$
words depending only on the anchor, a $2.5\times$ range the benchmark neither sets nor reports.

Everything about that pair was committed before its weights were downloaded --- the bands, the
entry gate, the fine-tune settings, and the $k$-grid as a rule in units of $k/s(x)$ because $s(x)$
was not yet measured (\texttt{results/onset\_prediction\_granularity\_gap.md}). Two properties
that could have spoiled it did not: its $s(x)$ of $3.36$ nats per token falls \emph{inside} the
$2.21$--$3.55$ the other seven span, so the comparison interpolates rather than extrapolating, and
its burstiness $k_{\mathrm{crit}}/s(x) = 1.54$ is indistinguishable from Pleias-1.2B's. One did:
at a mean token loss of $0.054$ it is the weakest memoriser admitted, entering on a sampled $k=-1$
recall of $0.181$ against a gate of $0.10$ and the others' $0.41$--$0.91$, and its bootstrap
interval $[0.967, 1.477]$ is correspondingly the widest of the nine.

Its onset ratio is $1.027$, between the two clusters. The seed-words relation fitted on the other
seven predicts $1.048$; the $s(x)$-only rule calibrated on two pairs predicts $0.876$ and misses.
Adding the point strengthens the rank correlation between context and ratio from $-0.919$ to
$-0.946$, which is what a real relation does when it meets a new point and a coincidence does not.

That confound was pre-registered with its own contingent test, and the test was run.
\texttt{cyberagent/open-calm-3b} shares the tokenizer exactly, so it holds granularity, the
$9.5$-word seed, $s(x)$ ($3.379$ against $3.363$) and burstiness ($1.541$ against $1.542$) fixed to
within half a percent and changes the scale and the memoriser: it enters on a sampled $k=-1$ recall
of $0.924$, the \emph{strongest} in the set, against the 1B's $0.181$. Its ratio is $0.993$,
$[0.959, 1.075]$ --- between the clusters as well, and the narrowest interval of the nine, since a
strong memoriser crosses the threshold steeply rather than by ones and twos. The two open-calm
ratios differ by $0.033$, less than the width of either cluster, and neither reaches either one:
memoriser strength was not what placed the eighth pair between them. The rank correlation goes to
$-0.958$ at $p = 0.0002$, and the matched-context subgroup's multiplicity $p$ from $0.018$ to
$0.008$.

What this does \emph{not} establish is causation: seed words is still $20\times$ characters per
token by construction, so the new pairs are two more observational points and the five interventions
in Appendix~\ref{app:seed} remain the only evidence that moves the variable directly. Per the
addendum committed before either sweep, where inside the interpolation band a pair lands carries no
information and is not reported as if it did.

\paragraph{Not a property of these sixteen novels}
Every onset above is measured on one corpus. The three anchors for which a second memoriser exists
were re-run on $600$ excerpts of $50$ public-domain books --- same anchors, same architecture, same
settings, same seed, same $12$-point grid, both baselines, only the protected work changed --- with
the bands committed before any of them decoded a token
(\texttt{results/onset\_prediction\_gutenberg.md}). Every entry gate passes on its own sampled
$k=-1$ arm ($0.578$, $0.517$, $0.270$), every $k=0$ arm reproduces $0.000$, and no trajectory in the
$33$ budgeted cells violates its budget.

Two committed questions, and both bands fire. The onset ratio moves by at most $0.05$ from its twin
on the novels, the fine-tokenizer pair is again the only one above $1$ and its interval again
excludes $1$, so \emph{leakage beginning after the certificate has gone vacuous is a property of the
pair}. And Eq.~\eqref{eq:req}, which fails as a direction, places the onset to within
$0.85$--$1.04$ of the measurement on all three --- no worse than the $0.865$--$1.076$ it manages on
the corpus it was derived on --- so its \emph{level} is a property of the geometry too. Its
low-quantile prediction misses on all three, the onset landing above the median of $r(x)$ where on
the novels it sat at $q_{25}$; and the three-pair direction test returns exact $p = 1.000$, which is
the floor at $n=3$ and was written down as such before the runs.

Three anchors are not seven, the second corpus is public-domain prose rather than a different kind
of work, and the committed grid resolves the crossing only to $0.2$ nats, about $7\%$ of $s(x)$, so
each onset is bracketed rather than located. Phi-3.5-mini's second memoriser is the weakest of the
three and its curve is not monotone at the top of the grid, which is why its interval is the widest
and its no-crossing fraction the only nonzero one. What the second corpus removes is the
possibility that the first one was doing the work.

\paragraph{The vacuity statement on a hundred books}\label{app:bookmia}
The onset needs memorising models and so is confined to corpora we can fine-tune against. The
\emph{vacuity} statement needs neither: $S(x)$ and the verdict $S(x) \le K$ are forward passes of
the anchor alone. We therefore re-measured it on BookMIA \citep{shi2024detecting}, the $50$ books it
marks as seen ($4{,}935$ passages) and the $50$ it marks as unseen ($4{,}935$), against the $758$
CopyBench passages re-run in the same pass as a positive control. Bands were committed first
(\texttt{results/onset\_prediction\_bookmia\_regimes.md}).

Three readings. The statement survives a thirteenfold larger corpus and six times as many books:
$65$ of $4{,}935$ seen passages are surprising enough that a $600$-nat budget does not cover them,
which is the committed WEAKER reading rather than the $\ge 0.99$ that would have been a
replication, and the unseen half and the control are both at $1.0000$. The median per-token rate is
$3.114$ against the novels' $3.197$, $2.6\%$ apart, so fifty books of a different provenance put the
anchor in the same surprisal regime. And the seen/unseen halves are \emph{not} exchangeable under an
anchor that saw neither: their medians differ by $6.0\%$ against a committed $5\%$, so BookMIA's
label is entangled with a text property --- the books a target model memorised are the widely
reproduced ones, whose prose is more predictable to any model. No arm here treats the halves as a
matched pair, and none should.

The control earned its place. A first pass scored vacuity as $k \ge s(x)$ per token --- the
asymptotic form Figure~\ref{fig:selection}(a) draws, where $K$ and $S(x)$ are both rays in the work
length --- and read $0.326$ on the same $758$ passages the deployed criterion puts at $1.000$. The
criterion was wrong, not the corpus. The two agree when the decoder's cap and the work are the same
length; a deployment sets $T_{\max} = 200$ against a protected span near $60$ tokens, so the
published budget covers more than three times the work being targeted and the deployed number is
the more favourable of the two to vacuity.

\paragraph{The natural pair, and why its onset is not quotable}
Llama-3.1-70B base was swept at $k \in \{1, 1.5, 3, 5, 10, 20\}$, so its recall crosses $0.01$
inside a bracket $1.5$ nats wide and any interpolated onset is a statement about the grid. Applying
the same treatment to the nine pairs whose fine grids we do have shows how much: keeping only the
two grid points nearest that bracket moves onset$/s(x)$ down by $0.045$ to $0.222$, to between
$0.699$ and $0.963$. The natural pair read the same way gives $0.7495$, inside that range. That is the whole content of the onset comparison, which is why the main text uses the
overlap of the rescaled grids instead: it interpolates the built pairs and never the natural one.
At $k/s(x) = 1.606$ only one built pair reaches that far, and there the natural pair is lower
($0.069$ at the book settings and $0.035$ at temperature $1$, against $0.098$).

\paragraph{The three rules that were committed, and how each missed}
Three readings of Eq.~\eqref{eq:req} were on the table before the last five pairs were swept:
the equation itself at the median of $r(x)$, the same at $q_{25}$, and a fitted constant $c\,s(x)$.
The two KL3M pairs decide against all three --- every miss low, all six predictions outside the
measured intervals, held-out errors of $0.352$, $0.251$ and $0.481$ nats. The rank correlation
between predicted and measured ratio is $-0.18$ where the derivation requires it positive, so the
directional claim does not merely fail but inverts: the pair carrying by far the most residual
surprisal has the highest ratio of the nine. The gap between affording a work and reproducing it is
visible directly: at each pair's onset budget Eq.~\eqref{eq:req} is satisfied for $27\%$ and $26\%$
of works on the two pairs where we can check it, while $0\%$ and $1\%$ actually leak.

\paragraph{Does $r(x)$ screen an individual work?}
Equation~\eqref{eq:req} is defined per work, so it is fair to ask whether it screens an individual
book. It does not, and the reason is measurable. Treating ``which passage leaks at budget $k$'' as
a classification problem over the $100$ passages of each pair, the derived requirement $r(x)$
reaches a mean AUC of $0.678$ across the eight budget cells in which anything leaks --- but the
anchor surprisal $s(x)$ \emph{alone} reaches $0.751$, and memorisation strength alone is
uninformative at $0.417$. Subtracting $s_r$ makes the per-work ranking worse, not better.

That is a consequence of the derivation rather than a contradiction of it. Across works $s_r$
carries only $6$--$7\%$ of the level of $s_s$ but $44$--$45\%$ of its standard deviation, so
subtracting it removes a nearly constant shift while adding variance. A population onset is sensitive to exactly
that shift and a per-work ranking is blind to it, which is why $r$ was ahead on the normaliser
ablation while only two pairs were in hand; across all nine neither comparison favours it. A
rights-holder can use $s(x)$ to locate the budget at which a class of works becomes exposed; for
one particular book it is still not good enough to rely on.

\section{The order comparison at matched utility}\label{app:matched}
The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:orders} is at matched \emph{budget}, because the budget is what a
deployer publishes and what a certificate quotes. It is not at matched utility, and
\S\ref{sec:orders} shows the judge cannot supply the missing axis: re-judging the same generations
with the same judge inverts the ranking. The geodesic supplies it without a judge. Fidelity
$G(\theta) = \theta\psi'(1) - \psi(\theta) = -D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_r \Vert p_\theta) + \mathrm{const}$
is what a metered decoder's budget buys, and it is monotone in $k$ at a fixed order, so for every
order there is a unique budget at which it buys exactly what the audited decoder buys at the
published one. What it lets through there is \emph{not} another average: verbatim reproduction is a
rare event, so the second quantity is
$L(\alpha,k) = \sum_t \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ over the protected tokens, whose exponential
is the reproduction probability. Both are deterministic and need no sampling. A $12$-point grid of
$k$ on $25$ memorised passages and $25$ ordinary generations, on twelve pairs in seven families:

Three readings. First, at the budget the mechanism's authors publish, $k=3$, the audited decoder
already captures $94.9$ to $99.8\%$ of the fidelity an unlimited budget would buy, so matching it
forces every other order past that pair's vacuity threshold. That column group compares operating
points at which the certificate already says nothing, and on nine of the twelve pairs the
matched-budget ranking \emph{reverses} there. Second, at $k=1$, where the constraint binds and the
matched budgets stay between $1.6$ and $5.3$ nats per token, the higher order usually helps ---
but at $\alpha{=}4$ by $1.0\times$ on one pair and $2.5\times10^{7}$ on another, and on four pairs
$\alpha{=}8$ at matched utility makes the protected tokens \emph{more} likely, by up to
$1.3\times10^{6}$ on TinyComma-1.8B with a memorised Llama-3.1-8B --- the only pair whose anchor and
risky model are different models, and so the mechanism's own configuration. The mechanism is visible
in the grid: a higher order charges something closer to the worst step, so buying the same
\emph{average} fidelity costs it a much larger budget --- $5.25$ against $1.0$ on that pair --- and
that budget is spent tilting toward the memoriser on the steps that carry the passage.

\paragraph{Does $L$ track what an adversary recovers?}
There is one pair where both exist: Table~\ref{tab:orders}'s attack columns are the TinyComma-1.8B
anchor with the memorised Llama-3.1-8B, at the published $k=3$, and that pair is in this set. At
that budget the oracle recall runs $0.097$, $0.054$, $0.004$, $0.001$ over $\alpha = 1, 2, 4, 8$ and
$L$ per token runs $-0.241$, $-0.613$, $-0.889$, $-1.046$: both strictly monotone, ordering the four
arms identically, with no sampling and no judge. Four arms is $\rho = 1$ at exact $p = 2/24$, which
is weak, and it is the only decoded ground truth available. The \emph{magnitudes} are not comparable
and are never quoted as if they were --- the recall ratio from $\alpha=1$ to $\alpha=8$ is $97\times$
and the exact-window factor implied by $L$ is $e^{40}$ --- because recall is near-verbatim over a
window with a threshold and retries while $L$ is exact reproduction of every token.

A note on what the first two readings do and do not establish. Both compare the orders at a
\emph{matched budget interpolated from} the published one, and a simpler question needs no budget:
each order traces a curve in the plane the mechanism trades in, fidelity against $L$, and if they
traced one frontier then matching fidelity would match $L$. Sweeping $L(\alpha) - L(1)$ across the
fidelity range every order covers, and counting a sign only when it clears that pair's own
\texttt{bfloat16}-against-\texttt{float32} spread, $16$ of the $36$ (pair, order) cells are uniformly
safer, $18$ \emph{cross} --- half of them, so for half the cells which decoder is safer depends on an
operating point the published budget does not reveal --- and two are uniformly more dangerous. This
re-analysis was not pre-registered and is reported as exploratory.

\paragraph{Does any of it belong to the corpus, the seed, or the workload?}
Three axes of the design were varied with everything else held fixed. The protected corpus: $600$
excerpts of $50$ public-domain books, cut to the same shape, with three anchors given a second
memoriser each --- no cell changes sign, the levels move by up to $3.59$ nats per window, and the
pair ordering holds on both corpora. The adversary's seed: two pairs at $10$ and $80$ tokens against
their committed $20$ --- at $k=1$ the cells move by $-0.59$ to $+1.42$, no sign changes, ordering
intact. And the ordinary workload the price side is measured on, which had never been varied: two
pairs on `factual` and `creative` prompts against `neutral` --- ten of twelve cells move beyond
their floor, the largest by $2.12$, and \emph{two cells change sign}, both on KL3M-520M and both
starting inside that pair's own floor, so what flipped is a sign the analysis does not read. The
ordering survives all three. A cell here is an order of magnitude and a rank; it is not a factor.

\paragraph{Nothing measured predicts which pair a deployer has}
Two hypotheses were pre-registered and both refuted. The fraction of the ceiling $F$ was one,
because $F \to 1$ must force the advantage to $1$ and $F$ needs no protected work; Figure
\ref{fig:nocollapse} shows the anchor point holding and the collapse failing everywhere else. The
memoriser's own log-probability of the protected tokens was the other, tested out of sample on a
pair held back for it, and it missed. Six candidates were then scored on all twelve pairs with
permutation $p$-values: the largest is $+0.62$ (the memoriser's rate at $\alpha=4$, $p = 0.035$),
below the $0.7$ committed as the threshold for an earned negative. Its $p$ is worth not
over-reading: with twelve points a moderate correlation can be significant and still be useless,
because $\rho = 0.62$ means the ordering it predicts is wrong for several pairs, and a number that
cannot rank the pair you have is not a predictor of what your order is worth. On \emph{family}
means --- the conservative reading, because the twelve pairs are four KL3M, two Pleias, two Llama
and one each of the rest --- the same candidate read $+0.90$ at five families, $+0.89$ at six and
$+0.71$ at seven: it decayed as families were added, which is the signature of a small-sample
artefact. Two further facts bear on it. The only quasi-causal test available --- changing the
memoriser's strength \emph{within} a fixed anchor, which the corpus arm does incidentally ---
contradicts the between-pair correlation on two of three anchors. And the anchor's surprisal rate,
the quantity this paper's own units result is built on, is the \emph{worst} of the six at
$\alpha=8$, at $+0.11$ --- tied there with $s_s - s_r$, which orders the twelve pairs identically
at every order because on this set $s_r$ is three orders of magnitude below $s_s$ (median ratio
$0.003$), so the six candidates are five distinct rankings.

So Table~\ref{tab:orders} ranks the charge function, not the decoder, and a deployer choosing
$\alpha$ on the published budget is choosing between $10^{7}\times$ safer and $10^{6}\times$ more
dangerous with nothing to go on --- this paper's thesis one level up: a published $k$ is no more
comparable across charges than it is across works.

One caveat, recorded with the runs. \texttt{bfloat16} moves a cell against \texttt{float32} by a
median of $0.30$ nats per window, and each pair's own spread --- which runs from $0.11$ to $2.34$
--- is the floor the crossing test uses; a cell inside its pair's floor is no effect rather than a
direction. It changes no pair's rank. The price columns in Table~\ref{tab:orders} are measured on an
identical 150-prompt workload for every arm, and its judged comparison carries $n=150$.
