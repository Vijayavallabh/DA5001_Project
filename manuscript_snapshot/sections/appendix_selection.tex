\subsection{Protocol and the scorer}\label{app:sel-protocol}
\paragraph{The judged workload, described.}\label{app:promptset}
The headline pass judges $500$ prompts in three classes (Table~\ref{tab:promptset}), counted off the
ids that pass judged rather than off the prompt files, which hold $850$; none is drawn from the
protected corpus, none shares a word $8$-gram with any of its passages or names any of its sixteen
works (\texttt{results/\allowbreak prompt\_\allowbreak set\_\allowbreak profile.csv}), and all
three sets ship with the artifact. Two classes come from public corpora a judge may have read in
training; every arm is judged on the same prompts against the same opponent, so that familiarity
can enter a gain only through how differently it treats the two texts. Two classes are served with the fixed
header \texttt{Complete the prefix:} and one is not. Complexity is given as two measurements rather
than a label. The opponent answers at about $150$ words in every class --- the $200$-token cap --- so
answer length does not separate the classes here. The anchor's order-averaged win rate against that
answer does, from $0.3667$ on biographies, the one class whose answer is checkable facts about a
named person, to $0.5067$ on story premises.

\paragraph{The first scorer failed, and what it spends its budget on.}\label{app:currency}
The first scorer fixed in advance was the risky model's own per-token log-likelihood, the objective
anchored decoding tilts toward, at $n=8$ under judge~A with one randomised order per candidate. It
moves judged utility from $0.319$ to $0.313$, $-0.006$ against a band of $+0.077$, and is not even
monotone ($0.327$ and $0.290$ at $n=2$ and $4$); the summed log-likelihood reaches $0.271$ by
shortening the served text from $98.0$ tokens to $15.3$. Selecting by the judge's own verdict among
the same eight draws reaches $0.807$, $+0.488$ --- circular, since that judge also scores it, and
an upper bound rather than a mechanism (scored instead by judge~B the same selection gains the
$+0.081$ of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) --- so the utility is in the draws and the likelihood
does not find it (\texttt{results/\allowbreak selection\_\allowbreak decoding.csv}). Why is a
within-prompt measurement. Over all $5{,}687$ pairs of the eight candidates of one prompt that the
judge placed differently (win $>$ tie $>$ loss), count how often the better-judged candidate has the
larger value of a statistic, a tie counting one half. The per-token likelihood is concordant on
$0.526$ $[0.504, 0.548]$ of them, the summed likelihood on $0.478$ $[0.454, 0.500]$ and the
completion's length in tokens on $0.537$ $[0.514, 0.560]$, intervals resampling prompts because the
eight candidates of one prompt are judged against one opponent text. So the likelihood a metered
decoder buys is barely above chance and \emph{no better} than length --- the difference is
$+0.011$ $[-0.023, +0.046]$, which does not license \emph{worse} --- and slot, drawn independently
of both statistics, can blur these figures toward $0.5$ but favours neither
(\texttt{analysis/\allowbreak currency\_\allowbreak auc.py},
\texttt{results/\allowbreak currency\_\allowbreak auc.csv}).

\paragraph{The scorer prefers an empty completion.}\label{app:empties}
Once the recording defect is repaired, $13.9\%$ of the headline pool's $32{,}000$ draws are empty ---
the anchor ends its turn at the first token --- and the committed reward scores them \emph{above} the
rest, median $-18.75$ against $-26.12$. Best-of-$n$ therefore serves an empty text on $12.8\%$ of
prompts at $n=1$, $21.8\%$ at $n=8$ and $8.2\%$ at $n=64$. The judge does not score an empty text as
a loss: the committed opponent, served without its chat template, is itself often degenerate
(Table~\ref{tab:served}), and the prompts served empty are judged at $0.4531$, $0.4771$ and $0.4939$
against $0.4536$, $0.5211$ and $0.5605$ for the rest. So at $n \ge 8$ the preference costs selection
level rather than flattering it; it is a property of this scorer, not of the certificate, which holds
for any score (\texttt{analysis/\allowbreak empty\_\allowbreak preference.py},
\texttt{results/\allowbreak empty\_\allowbreak preference.csv}).

\subsection{The head-to-head, and whether it survives}\label{app:sel-h2h}
\paragraph{The largest $n$, and whether the gain stops.}
Comma-7B was swept to $n=64$ on the same $500$ prompts, reward, judges and opponent. The gain rises
monotonically within the pass --- $+0.041$, $+0.060$, $+0.072$, $+0.103$, $+0.127$, $+0.173$ at
$n = 2,4,8,16,32,64$ on the first scorer and $+0.062$ to $+0.230$ on the second, Spearman $+1.000$ in
$\log n$ in both, so this anchor's curve is still rising where its grid stops --- under a
$\log 64 = 4.16$ pathwise certificate and the sharper KL bound of $3.17$
nats, with no sign of the reward overoptimisation that turns such curves over anywhere on \emph{this}
grid. Extending the audited anchor to $n=128$, the paired $g(128) - g(64)$ is $+0.0140$
$[-0.0180, +0.0460]$, and under order averaging $+0.0140$ $[-0.0015, +0.0295]$: the same point
estimate, a $2.06\times$ tighter interval, both containing zero, so that anchor is flat between $64$
and $128$. Comma-7B, the strongest anchor, was taken to $128$ as
well, on a pool whose first $64$ ranks are bit-identical to the committed one in all $32{,}000$
rewards: its paired $g(128) - g(64)$ is $+0.0040$ $[-0.0270, +0.0350]$ under judge~B and $+0.0110$
$[-0.0190, +0.0410]$ under judge~C, flat between $64$ and $128$ too, and the two
anchors agree. \textbf{A flat step is not a ceiling.} Taken to $n=256$ on our $850$ prompts and on
AlpacaEval's $805$ \citep{li2023alpacaeval}, each pool again bit-identical to its committed $n=64$ ranks, the audited anchor
is flat from $64$ to $128$ on both, $-0.0094$ $[-0.0353, +0.0147]$ and $-0.0019$ $[-0.0273, +0.0217]$,
and rises from $128$ to $256$ on both, $+0.0306$ $[+0.0047, +0.0547]$ and $+0.0280$
$[+0.0025, +0.0553]$ --- on our own prompts against a prediction, fixed in advance, that it would
have stopped. Each rise sits about one half-width from zero, the marginal regime in which a paired
difference fails to replicate below, and measured from $64$ the two doublings together contain zero
on both workloads, $+0.0212$ $[-0.0088, +0.0512]$ and $+0.0261$ $[-0.0056, +0.0565]$, and under order
averaging $+0.0099$ $[-0.0050, +0.0248]$ (both checks post hoc). \textbf{One more doubling, with
bands fixed in advance, does not settle it.} Taken to $n=512$ on both workloads, the first $256$ ranks
of each pool bit-identical to the committed ones in every reward, the last doubling is flat on both,
$g(512)-g(256)$ is $+0.0056$ $[-0.0193, +0.0311]$ on AlpacaEval and $+0.0071$ $[-0.0165, +0.0300]$ on
ours, and measured from $64$ the three doublings together still contain zero, $+0.0311$
$[-0.0025, +0.0634]$ and $+0.0312$ $[-0.0018, +0.0635]$ --- where we had predicted a marginal climb.
Under order averaging the two AlpacaEval differences exclude zero, $+0.0186$ $[+0.0065, +0.0301]$ and
$+0.0286$ $[+0.0115, +0.0438]$, but at $1.58$ and $1.77$ half-widths, the margin at which a paired
difference has failed to replicate here. So we claim neither a ceiling nor a slope: $n=64$ is not
where either ladder demonstrably ends, and past it three doublings cost eight times the draws and
$\log 8 = 2.08$ more nats and have bought about $+0.03$.

\paragraph{Judged single-order levels are grid-dependent.}
An arm's level moves when other arms join the sweep, because each item's presentation order is drawn
once per element of a set that grows with the grid and this judge is dominated by slot order: the
audited anchor's $n=64$ gain read $+0.142$ in one sweep and $+0.076$ in the other on byte-identical
text, with all $32{,}000$ rewards at ranks $0$--$63$ bit-identical. Order averaging draws nothing and
reproduced exactly, $+0.1045$ $[+0.0820, +0.1280]$ both times, which is why every head-to-head here is
order-averaged and a single-order gain is never compared across sweeps. On the judge-free axis the same
pointwise reward \emph{does} turn over, its TriviaQA accuracy falling with $n$ to an interval excluding
zero on the wrong side at $n=16$ (Table~\ref{tab:judgefree}) --- which is why the judge-free headline is
majority vote, with no scorer to overoptimise against.

Read as one order against the order-averaged construction, selection at $n=64$ moves $+0.110$ to
$+0.1045$ $[+0.082, +0.128]$ and the metered decoder at $k=10$ moves $+0.097$ to $+0.0400$
$[+0.014, +0.0655]$, so the paired difference moves $+0.013$ to $\mathbf{+0.0645}$
$[+0.030, +0.0995]$. Every committed pass judged text that still carried the tail of its prompt
(Appendix~\ref{app:judgemethod}); re-judged on the repaired text, as registered, selection gains
$+0.1015$ $[+0.0765, +0.1260]$, the meter $+0.0510$ $[+0.025, +0.076]$, and the difference is
$\mathbf{+0.0505}$ $[+0.0155, +0.0860]$, which is the figure the main text quotes. The
replications below are quoted as measured on the committed text, each a paired difference inside its
own pass. We specified in advance the opposite prediction --- both arms deflating together, the
difference vanishing under the cross-pass floor --- and it was wrong. The correction is
\emph{asymmetric}, moving selection by a factor of $0.95$ and the metered decoder by $0.41$, so the
difference grows five-fold instead of shrinking; the likely mechanism, offered rather than claimed, is
that at $k=10$ the metered decoder serves $p_r$ unchanged at every step it takes, leaving the judge two
near-identical texts and little but slot order. The same arm found a defect behind the earlier numbers:
both judging scripts showed the judge a prompt reconstructed as the served text minus the generation,
whose split point moves with how much each arm generated, so for the same item the three arms disagree
about it in \textbf{455 of 500} cases. This run takes the prompt from the corpus, and the cost falls on
us --- under one true prompt the \emph{single-order} difference is $+0.013$, not the $+0.070$ implied by
the $+0.142$ and $+0.072$ measured under arm-specific prompts. What survives is the order-averaged
$+0.0645$, visible only once position is removed; Figure~\ref{fig:breadth}'s sweep is single-order
throughout and says so, so the two must not be read off one another.

\paragraph{The head-to-head by serving configuration.}\label{app:served}
Table~\ref{tab:served} comes from two registered arms, each banded before any judge call
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak onset\_\allowbreak prediction\_\allowbreak served\_\allowbreak opponent.md},
\texttt{results/\allowbreak onset\_\allowbreak prediction\_\allowbreak he\_\allowbreak config.md}), and one
descriptive re-judge that puts every budget in one pass
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak frontier\_\allowbreak levels\_\allowbreak note.md}); all three judge the same
selection texts, whose per-prompt levels agree across them on $500$ of $500$ prompts. Served through
its chat template the $8$B was generated afresh at $k=10$ on disjoint seeds; its own second draw
reads $0.504$ against the first, so the instrument separates nothing it should not, and it beats its
templateless text by $+0.184$ $[+0.155, +0.213]$. We predicted selection would lose to that meter and
it did; we predicted the committed meter would be unresolved against the stronger opponent and
selection instead wins, $+0.058$ $[+0.0325, +0.0845]$. At the authors' $70$B pair we predicted the
meter would win at $k=20$ and at the rate-matched $k=1$, and both predictions were wrong: at
temperature $1.0$ with no penalty the base model, continuing \texttt{Complete the prefix:}, is judged
no better than the anchor, and at $k=20$ the meter serves its text byte for byte on $500$ of $500$
prompts. At $k=0.5$, the one budget whose certificate says anything about a $50$-token window, the
meter is inseparable from its anchor at both pairs, as predicted. \textbf{Two post-hoc checks, both
labelled so}: the meter writes about twice selection's words ($151$ against $75$ at the median), and a
length-controlled regression in the manner of \citet{dubois2024length} leaves the difference at
$+0.0605$ $[+0.0236, +0.0971]$, with no length preference the regression can resolve; and on the
$414$ prompts whose two anchor controls are both non-empty it is $+0.0441$ $[+0.0024, +0.0864]$
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak h2h\_\allowbreak length\_\allowbreak control\_\allowbreak note.md}).
The eight rows were not registered as one family, so the table is also read with a Bonferroni
correction over them: at $99.375\%$ every row still excludes zero in the same direction,
the narrowest being the chat-template $k=1$ row at $[+0.0015, +0.0820]$
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak served\_\allowbreak multiplicity.csv}, post hoc).

\paragraph{Table~\ref{tab:served} under the authors' own utility metrics.}\label{app:hemetrics}
\citet{he2026anchored} score utility with Prometheus-2 fluency and FActScore precision, not a
pairwise judge, so we scored the same repaired texts with both (\texttt{results/\allowbreak
he\_\allowbreak metrics\_\allowbreak note.md}, descriptive): their fluency rubric verbatim under the
open \texttt{prometheus-7b-v2.0} \citep{kim2024prometheus2}, where they use \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini},
and FActScore \citep{min2023factscore} on the $150$ biographies with \texttt{Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct}
extracting and verifying against each entity's Wikipedia text. On fluency (1--5, non-empty
responses) selection at $n=64$ scores $2.56$ against the meter's $1.88$ at $k=10$, a paired
$+0.70$ $[+0.53, +0.85]$, and beats the meter at every budget of both text-continuation pairs
($+1.15$ $[+1.00, +1.30]$ over the $70$B pair at $k=0.5$); it loses to the instruct model served
through its chat template, $-0.81$ $[-0.99, -0.64]$ --- the judge's pattern. FActScore reads the
other way, and should: every fact selection serves is one the anchor drew, so its precision is its
anchor's ($0.042$ against $0.048$, paired $-0.008$ $[-0.041, +0.022]$) and below every metered arm
($-0.182$ $[-0.222, -0.145]$ against the meter at $k=10$), while it abstains on $20$ of $150$
biographies where a single draw abstains on $80$. So the head-to-head is a statement about fluency
and helpfulness, not about factual recall, which the anchor bounds.

\citet{he2026anchored} meter every licensed anchor but TinyComma with AnchoredByte, which fuses a

\paragraph{Where the reversal stops: a workload we did not choose.}\label{app:workload}
The headline is a claim about a regime, so we looked for its edge on a benchmark we do not control.
On AlpacaEval-805 the reversal \emph{fails}: selection gains $+0.0835$ $[+0.0665, +0.1016]$ and the
metered decoder $+0.1174$ $[+0.0991, +0.1354]$, a paired difference of $-0.0339$ $[-0.0534,
-0.0137]$ over $805$ prompts, and $-0.0329$ $[-0.0528, -0.0127]$ on the repaired text. The meter wins.

It took two passes, and what the first one exposed is about \emph{our own} headline rather than
about AlpacaEval. Run at the paper's own $k=10$ the budget is barely active on either workload:
$26$ of $160{,}227$ steps here ($0.016\%$), and $24$ of $299{,}843$ ($0.008\%$) for the committed
metered arm on the workload it was measured on. At $k=10$ that arm \emph{is} the unconstrained
risky model to within sampling noise --- on AlpacaEval, where the two cells share a seed stream and
the question can be asked directly, $794$ of $805$ served completions are identical to the opponent
\emph{byte for byte}. This is the vacuous horn of Proposition~\ref{prop:sparse} arriving in the
paper's own experiment, and the realised $171.3$ nats against $K = kT_{\max} = 2000$ already said
so; the activity rate says it in the other currency.

So we re-ran with a budget that genuinely binds, choosing $k$ by matching a \emph{rate} rather than
reusing a number, over $\{0.1, 0.3, 1.0, 3.0, 10.0\}$: the activity reads $0.765$, $0.684$,
$0.077$, $0.005$, $0.0002$, and $k=1.0$ gives $8.008\%$ --- about a thousand times the committed
arm's own binding rate --- while matching the opponent on only $4.3\%$ of prompts, so the
comparison is between genuinely different texts. That is the $-0.0339$ above. The $k=10$ pass reads
$-0.0957$, larger, but we do not lead with it: judging a completion against an opponent it is
byte-identical to is a degenerate comparison, and it is the one the committed protocol cannot
detect because its two arms are sampled independently. \textbf{The reversal fails on this workload
at both budgets, and forcing the meter to actually spend is what narrows the gap rather than
closing it.} Selection's $+0.0835$ is identical across both passes to four decimals, being the same
generations under the same greedy judge.

\textbf{The control says the split is the workload.} Running our own $850$ prompts through the
same pipeline, the reversal holds at both budgets --- $+0.0482$ $[+0.0303, +0.0662]$ at $k=10$, and
$+0.0965$ $[+0.0765, +0.1162]$ at $k=0.9$, chosen by the same rate-matching rule and binding on
$8.9\%$ of steps with only $2.0\%$ of completions shared with the opponent. A disjoint re-draw of
the AlpacaEval arm reads $-0.0255$ $[-0.0453, -0.0062]$, replicating, and \textbf{all three judges
agree on both sides}: on our workload judge~B reads $+0.0965$ $[+0.0765, +0.1162]$, judge~C
$+0.0903$ $[+0.0629, +0.1176]$ and \texttt{Mixtral-8x7B} $+0.0226$ $[-0.0018, +0.0476]$; on
AlpacaEval they read $-0.0339$ $[-0.0534, -0.0137]$, $-0.0969$ $[-0.1264, -0.0677]$ and $-0.0854$
$[-0.1112, -0.0593]$. Every point estimate is positive on one workload and negative on the other,
all three clear zero on AlpacaEval, and \emph{no judge crosses}. The split is not one judge's. So across five passes
on \emph{these two} workloads the sign is constant within each and opposite between them, at both a vacuous and a binding
budget: the split is the workload, not the budget, the batch size, the pipeline or the seed.
The two also respond to a binding budget in \emph{opposite} directions --- selection's margin grows
on our corpus and shrinks on AlpacaEval. That is what a support ceiling would predict, and we
report it as an observation rather than a claim, having registered neither --- and we flag here,
rather than leaving a reader to notice it two paragraphs down, that the decomposition below
\emph{refutes} that account. The direction is consistent with a story whose magnitude is not.

\textbf{A third workload, and it is underpowered by construction.} MT-Bench \citep{zheng2023judging} is $80$ prompts, so
its interval half-width at a binding budget is $0.053$ against AlpacaEval's $0.020$, and the paired
difference there reads $-0.0250$ $[-0.0781, +0.0281]$: \textsc{unresolved}, which the registration
named in advance as a possible consequence of that size and which we report as a failure to resolve
rather than as a null. Two further judges, run post-hoc and labelled so, read $-0.0094$ $[-0.0875,
+0.0656]$ and $-0.0250$ $[-0.0906, +0.0406]$ --- three negative point estimates, three intervals
containing zero, so the failure is the arm's and not the instrument's. At the paper's own $k=10$ it
does resolve, at $-0.0875$ $[-0.1375, -0.0375]$, \emph{with} AlpacaEval. \textbf{What the third
workload does buy is a third independent measurement of the vacuity}, on a prompt set we did not
choose: at $k=10$ the budget is active on $0.033\%$ of MT-Bench's steps and $95.0\%$ of served
completions are byte-identical to the opponent. \textbf{And the decomposition holds a third time}: over the three
binding-budget passes $g_{\mathrm{sel}}$ spans $0.056$ ($+0.1218$, $+0.0835$, $+0.0656$) and
$g_{\mathrm{met}}$ spans $0.092$ ($+0.0253$, $+0.1174$, $+0.0906$), and it is the meter that
crosses --- below selection's gain on our corpus and above it on both external ones. On the text as
recorded selection gains on all three and the meter is what the workload changes. \textbf{On the
repaired text that decomposition does not survive} (Appendix~\ref{app:judgemethod}): selection's
gains span $0.095$ ($+0.1321$, $+0.0801$, $+0.0375$, the last covering zero) and the meter's $0.085$
($+0.0282$, $+0.1130$, $+0.0688$), so both arms move with the workload, the meter still crosses, and
the support-ceiling account below is neither refuted nor supported by it.

\textbf{A fourth workload names the axis, and we cannot claim the mechanism it suggests.} Three
candidates are now refuted --- anchor competence, a support ceiling, the prompt template --- and
what was left was the task \emph{type}: every external workload measured above is
instruction-following or reading comprehension, while ours is prefix completion, so ``completion''
and ``our corpus'' were the same line in the data. They are separable on a corpus we did not make.
Five hundred excerpts of $42$ public-domain books, routed through the same factual slot AlpacaEval
uses, read $+0.0990$ $[+0.0795, +0.1185]$ at a rate-matched binding budget ($k=0.9$, active on
$6.95\%$ of steps, $3.6\%$ of completions shared with the opponent) and $+0.0950$ $[+0.0780,
+0.1120]$ at $k=10$. \textbf{A public completion workload falls with ours at both budgets}, and the
metered decoder does no better than its own control there, $-0.0185$ $[-0.0380, +0.0005]$ and
$-0.0145$ $[-0.0315, +0.0030]$ (on the repaired text worse than it, $-0.0275$ $[-0.0470, -0.0080]$ at the
binding budget), against $+0.1174$ on AlpacaEval and $+0.0253$ on ours.

\textbf{We pre-registered that a win here would be confounded and would be reported so, and it is.}
These anchors are trained on public-domain and openly licensed text, so this corpus is plausibly
inside the anchor's training distribution as well as being completion-shaped, and this arm cannot
separate the two. What it establishes is the empirical scoping --- the reversal is present on
prefix completion and absent on instruction-following and reading comprehension --- not a mechanism
for it. The grid needed refining to get there: the registered $\{0.1,\dots,10\}$ sweep brackets the
target and its \textsc{argmin} still lands at $0.47\times$ it, so a second grid inside the
bracketing interval was registered before it ran and chose $k=0.9$ at $0.87\times$. And the
vacuity holds a fifth time: at $k=10$ the budget is active on $0.043\%$ of steps and $98.2\%$ of
served completions are the opponent byte for byte.

Two things follow. The activity curve is the dichotomy on somebody else's benchmark: across one
decade of $k$ either side of $1.0$ the budget goes from binding at two thirds of all steps to one
in two hundred, with no plateau where it is \emph{mildly} active. \textbf{We cannot identify the cause, and the obvious candidate is measurably wrong.} The natural
story is a support ceiling --- selection serves only what the anchor draws, so on a task the anchor
cannot do, more draws cannot help. Decomposed, the effect is not selection's: moving from our
workload to AlpacaEval, selection's gain falls by $0.038$ while the \emph{meter's} rises by
$0.092$, so nearly three quarters of the swing is the budgeted decoder gaining more, not selection
gaining less. Nor does the ladder show a ceiling: taken to $n=512$ on AlpacaEval it is flat, then rises,
then is flat again, as on our own prompts (above), while its deficit against the binding meter reads
$-0.0357$ $[-0.0553, -0.0165]$ at $n=128$ and $-0.0239$ $[-0.0429, -0.0050]$ at $256$ against
$-0.0339$ at $64$ --- narrower by less than the half we predicted, still clear of zero, so the
reversal fails off-support at $256$ as at $64$, at a certificate of $\log 256 = 5.55$ nats against
the meter's realised $145.1$. One doubling on, the deficit is $-0.0053$ $[-0.0248, +0.0143]$ at
$512$, which the band fixed in advance reads as \textsc{unresolved}: no longer clear of zero, against
our prediction that it would still trail, and not caught either --- at $\log 512 = 6.24$ nats,
$23.3\times$ below the meter's realised spend. Nor does anchor competence predict the sign. Splitting our own corpus into its three
classes --- one pass, one protocol, one judge --- spans $0.137$ of the anchor's own win rate
against the opponent, twice the $0.065$ that separates the two workloads, and the sign does not
move at all: $+0.1050$, $+0.0837$ and $+0.0990$, not even monotone in competence. A dose-response
should be visible where we can see it best and there is none. \textbf{Nor is it the prompt
template, and that separates itself on data already on disk.} Our corpus and the external
benchmarks are not served the same prompt --- \texttt{dap/\allowbreak shared.py} prepends
\texttt{Complete the prefix:} to copyright-domain prompts, and the external benchmarks are routed
through the factual slot precisely so their numbers stay their own --- so ``ours versus theirs''
has also been ``header versus no header'' throughout. But our own workload is not uniform: its
\texttt{factual} class, $500$ of the $850$ prompts, goes through that same slot and carries the
header on $0$ of them, and it reads $+0.0990$ $[+0.0720, +0.1260]$, while AlpacaEval, equally
header-free, reads $-0.0339$ $[-0.0540, -0.0137]$. Two header-free arms, opposite signs, both
intervals clear of zero; and within our corpus the two header-carrying classes agree with the
header-free one ($+0.0838$ and $+0.1050$ against $+0.0990$). The template is not the split. What we report, then, is the
empirical scoping and not a mechanism for it. The certificate is untouched
--- Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} is unconditional --- but the utility claim is scoped, and
where the boundary sits is not something one benchmark can say.

\textbf{A fifth workload tests that axis where it makes a prediction, and half-answers it.}
CoTaEval's NewsQA split \citep{trischler2017newsqa} is reading comprehension --- neither prefix completion nor
instruction-following --- so on the task-type account it should fall with AlpacaEval. That
prediction, and the fact that this arm cannot separate the account from ``our corpus versus
everyone else'' because both predict the same sign here, were recorded before its judge ran. It
reads $+0.0070$ $[-0.0175, +0.0320]$ at the rate-matched binding budget ($k=1.4$, active on
$7.76\%$ of steps, $1.2\%$ of completions shared with the opponent) and $-0.0375$ $[-0.0600,
-0.0145]$ at $k=10$: unresolved at the first, with AlpacaEval at the second. The axis is therefore
\emph{not falsified} --- that needed a reading with ours, which is the costly outcome the
prediction was written to expose --- and it is not cleanly confirmed either, because reading
comprehension lands between the two clusters rather than on either. The decomposition was once more the meter's on the text as recorded: selection gains $+0.0235$ $[+0.0010, +0.0470]$ whatever the budget (on the repaired text $+0.0070$ $[-0.0165, +0.0300]$, covering zero), while the meter gains $+0.0165$ $[-0.0030, +0.0355]$ at the binding budget and $+0.0610$ $[+0.0420, +0.0800]$ at
$k=10$. And this is the least degenerate vacuous cell we have measured --- activity $0.231\%$
against $0.008\%$--$0.043\%$ on the six others, and $76.6\%$ of served completions byte-identical
to the unconstrained opponent against $95.0\%$--$99.6\%$ on the five where that comparison is
interpretable.

\textbf{A sixth workload removes the familiarity confound, as far as it can be removed.} The same
protocol on $500$ excerpts of $27$ novels from the half of BookMIA \citep{shi2024detecting} labelled
\emph{unseen}, through the same factual slot and with no header, reads $+0.1040$ $[+0.0850, +0.1230]$
at a rate-matched binding budget ($k=1.0$, active on $4.76\%$ of steps, $3.8\%$ of completions
shared with the opponent) and $+0.0885$ $[+0.0735, +0.1035]$ at $k=10$: \textbf{with ours at both
budgets}, the reading registered as the prediction. Before any band was read, the anchor was drawn
on the workload's own prefixes and scored with the vetting instrument, and it reproduces none of
them (near-verbatim recall $0.0000$ on all $500$). That check is one-sided --- the same instrument
reads $0.000$ on the protected corpus --- so it excludes leakage without proving unfamiliarity, and
the rest of the premise is a membership label whose reliability is contested. Within those limits
the scoping loses its caveat: the reversal is present on prefix completion whether or not the anchor
has seen the text, and absent on instruction-following and reading comprehension. Here the metered
decoder does \emph{worse} than its own control at both budgets, $-0.0400$ $[-0.0585, -0.0215]$ and
$-0.0245$ $[-0.0400, -0.0085]$, while selection gains $+0.0640$ $[+0.0485, +0.0795]$; and the
vacuity holds a sixth time, the $k=10$ budget active on $0.008\%$ of steps with $99.6\%$ of served
completions the opponent's byte for byte.

\textbf{The one thing every workload shares is the opponent, and it orders them without explaining
them.} Every workload above is judged against the same instruction-tuned opponent, so ``task type''
could stand in for how good that opponent is at the task. Its strength --- its order-averaged win
rate against the anchor's own $n=1$ completion --- runs from $0.4755$ on the unseen novels to
$0.6792$ on AlpacaEval and orders the six differences almost perfectly (Spearman $-0.943$); divided
by the room a difference of win rates has, that falls to $-0.657$, so we withdraw the claim that it
orders them beyond that arithmetic. What survives is the decomposition: the \emph{meter's} gain
rises in exact rank order with the opponent's strength ($+1.000$) and selection's does not
($+0.486$). \textbf{Varied within one workload, the opponent is not the split.} Five opponents on
AlpacaEval through one generator, strengths $0.7752$ to $0.8807$, and against every one the meter
beats selection, each difference negative with its interval clear of zero ($-0.0584$ to
$-0.0814$). Two limits bound that. No instruction-tuned model we could reach is weak on AlpacaEval
--- the weakest still beats the anchor three times in four --- so an opponent as weak there as ours
is on our corpus is not tested. And the pipeline that generates the opponent's text moves its
strength by more than the whole ladder: the committed opponent reads $0.6792$ through our sweep and
$0.8519$ through the ladder's decoder, on the same $805$ prompts.

\paragraph{Does it survive a different judge and a different draw?}\label{app:h2hrepeat}
\textbf{The one dissenting judge, re-asked with more prompts and on a workload where the effect is
present.} \texttt{Mixtral-8x7B} is the only family-clean frontier judge and the only one of five
whose interval contained zero, at $+0.0090$ $[-0.0355, +0.0530]$ on $500$ prompts. Two attempts to
settle it failed because both ran on AlpacaEval, where the reversal does not hold at all, so the
gates correctly refused to read it. Re-asked on the $850$-prompt pass at a binding budget, where
judge~B reads $+0.0965$ and judge~C $+0.0903$, Mixtral reads $+0.0226$ $[-0.0018, +0.0476]$: the
same sign as every other judge, a half-width of $0.0247$, and an upper end below the $+0.0645$ on
record. The exception is smallness, not absence, and the extra prompts bought it --- the interval
tightened from a half-width of $0.0443$ while the point estimate stayed near zero, which is what
distinguishes a small effect from an under-powered one. Mixtral's disagreement is about the
\emph{metered} arm and not selection's: it scores the meter at $+0.0971$ where judge~B puts it at
$+0.0253$, while the three judges agree on selection to within $0.06$.

The $+0.0645$ rests on one generation pass scored by one judge, and this paper has already been burned

$42,43,44 \rightarrow 52,53,54$, hashed into the high bits so the $64$ trajectory seeds are disjoint by
construction --- everything else held, batch size included, since batch size is part of the seed of a
sampled arm. Judge~A is excluded on principle: it supplies the reward that selects the arm.

\textbf{The panel, and the one judge it does not explain.} (The judging protocol itself ---
template, decoding and the three original judges --- is Appendix~\ref{app:judgemethod}.) Judges D through G re-score
\emph{byte-identical} text --- no generation, no reward recomputed --- so under a greedy judge the
order-averaged score is a deterministic function of that text and every difference between these
rows is the instrument alone. All five judges put the difference positive; four put its interval
clear of zero. Judge~D reproduces the registered figure to $0.0025$.

\emph{Family does not account for the exception.} The scorer is \texttt{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct}, so
judges D and F share its family and B, E and G do not; the family judges resolve $2/2$ and the
clean ones $2/3$, and the single judge that does not resolve it is \textbf{clean}. Size does not
order the outcome ($3.8$B yes, $14$B yes, $27$B yes, $47$B no, $72$B yes), and neither does
self-consistency: the least self-consistent instrument in the panel is judge~B at $0.244$--$0.350$,
it carries the registered headline, and the most consistent is judge~G at $0.790$--$0.848$, which
agrees with it --- so discounting a judge by its consistency would discard our own instrument
first. We say plainly that judge~E is an outlier we cannot account for. It is not degenerate: its
verdicts use the full five-point order-averaged support, concentrated on ties ($163$--$195$ of
$500$ at exactly $0.5$) where judge~D's are bimodal at $0$ and $1$, and its point estimate has the
same sign as every other judge's.


\textbf{On the community-standard benchmark the scorer does worse than bind: it reverses the
mechanism.} To test on a community benchmark rather than our own corpora alone, we ran the news half
of CoTaEval \citep{wei2024cotaeval} --- a domain no corpus in this paper uses --- as $500$ in-domain
article-and-question items scored by SQuAD token-F1 \citep{rajpurkar2016squad}, CoTaEval's own utility metric, at seven
anchors in three families with bands fixed in advance. \textbf{Four anchors cannot read a news
article at all} ($n=1$ F1 of $0.003$ to $0.088$ at both Pleias and both KL3M models, against a
registered floor of $0.10$), so no band was computed for them; that is the capability constraint
this appendix already names, measured. At the four that can, the registered pointwise reward
\emph{turns over at every one}: $-0.1836$ $[-0.2249, -0.1412]$ at Comma-7B, $-0.2334$
$[-0.2738,-0.1922]$ on a disjoint re-draw of it, $-0.1124$ $[-0.1543,-0.0711]$ at Comma-7B (1T) and
$-0.0594$ $[-0.0921,-0.0274]$ at TinyComma-1.8B. At the headline anchor that is $43\%$ of the
anchor's own F1 given away by $n=64$. Majority vote, which has no scorer to overoptimise against,
neither falls nor climbs ($+0.0284$ $[-0.0012,+0.0576]$).

We registered the opposite prediction and it was wrong. The honest reading is that the reward
overoptimisation this paper concedes on TriviaQA is not a caveat confined to one task: at the $7$B
scorer, on a standard benchmark, in a new domain, at four anchors, with a replication, it is the
main effect,
and a deployer choosing $n$ by a pointwise reward of this size on this workload would be worse off
at every $n$ than serving the anchor once. Two things it does not touch, stated so the scope is not read wider
than the measurement: the certificate is a claim about the served law and does not depend on the
served text being good, and the GSM8K majority-vote result ($0.320 \to 0.546$) is a different task
under a different rule.

\textbf{A larger scorer moves it, and the sign of that too was registered before the run.} The
result above forces one question a deployer asks immediately --- is this the mechanism or the $7$B
scorer? --- so the four usable anchors were re-run with the scorer swapped $7$B $\to$ $14$B and
nothing else changed. The turn-over \emph{survives} at Comma-7B (2T) and at its disjoint re-draw,
at $3.09$ and $3.18$ interval half-widths, and \emph{disappears} at Comma-7B (1T) and
TinyComma-1.8B: two of four, which is the \textsc{scorer-bound} reading of the table fixed in
advance. We predicted \textsc{scorer-independent} and were wrong. The rescue is narrower than the
word suggests: neither anchor that stopped losing began to climb --- both read no effect and both
are marginal --- so a larger scorer buys the absence of harm here and not a gain, and it does not
reach the anchor this paper's headline uses. The instrument check the registration added passed at
all four, each arm's $n=1$ F1 agreeing to four decimals with its counterpart's, which is what a
scorer swap must do to an arm the scorer never touches. The requirement is therefore explicit
rather than implied: every selection arm in this paper scores with the $7$B reward model, and on
this benchmark the sign of the $n$-effect is a property of that choice as well as of the task. No
$14$B level is set beside a $7$B one and no third scorer was run, both excluded in advance.

\textbf{A third rung settles it, against the two-rung reading.} Two points
cannot tell a threshold from an accident, so the ladder was completed at
\texttt{Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct}: the headline anchor, the two that had flipped at $14$B, and
TriviaQA, all bands registered first. \textbf{Nothing turns over anywhere.} At $72$B the headline
anchor reads $+0.0266$ $[-0.0097, +0.0623]$, no effect where at $7$B it gave away $43\%$ of its own
F1; the two that flipped now \emph{climb}, $+0.0616$ $[+0.0226, +0.1005]$ and $+0.0624$
$[+0.0327, +0.0932]$; and on TriviaQA a $14$B scorer already removes the fall
($+0.0240$ $[-0.0060, +0.0540]$) while $72$B climbs ($+0.0360$ $[+0.0080, +0.0640]$). The
Spearman of accuracy against $\log n$ \emph{inverts} on both tasks --- CoTaEval
$-0.8929 \to +0.7500$, TriviaQA $-0.6071 \to +0.8571$. We had registered that the headline
anchor's turn-over would survive $72$B; it did not.

\textbf{Why this comparison is legitimate where a judged one would not be.} Every arm re-scores
\emph{cached} generations, so the pre-registered gate is free and very strong: majority vote never
consults the scorer, so all seven of its cells must be identical to the arm being re-scored. They
are, on all six arms --- the text is byte-identical and the only thing that changed is the
instrument, which is why these passes may be set against each other where two judged sweeps may
not. The GSM8K control, registered as the arm that must still climb or both $14$B arms are void,
climbs at $4.55$ interval half-widths with a Spearman of $+1.0000$.

\textbf{What this rescopes and what it leaves alone.} Every turn-over this appendix reports is
measured at the $7$B reward model and none survives a larger one, so the finding is a property of
optimising a pointwise proxy \emph{of that size} on these tasks rather than of the mechanism.
It is \emph{rescoped and not withdrawn}: the $7$B scorer is the one every selection arm here uses,
the one the $35.4\times$ serving measurement is of, and the one a deployer reading this paper would
deploy, so at the paper's own operating point the loss is real and reproduces on a disjoint draw.
What a deployer gains is a stated requirement, and a requirement is a cost --- a favourable one
only in that the scorer is $9.3\%$ of selection's wall-clock and the draws $90.7\%$, though
\emph{no latency arm was run above $7$B and no cost number here is measured}. \textbf{And it does not
transfer to the judged workload, which is measured rather than disclaimed}: the same two
scorers, run over the judged grid in one six-scorer pass, lift it by nothing
(Appendix~\ref{app:saturation}). Scorer size binds where the task has a checkable answer and not
where the target is a judged preference on ordinary prompts, which a $1.5$B model already
approximates to $87\%$ of the $7.6$B gain --- a statement about the \emph{task}, not the mechanism.
The certificate is untouched either way: it is $\log n$ for any score of any size.

\textbf{CoTaEval's infringement half cannot tell a defence from none at this pair, and an adversarial
scorer beats the risky model on it.} We ran it through the decoding path of every arm on record, each
item with the benchmark's own prefix ($500$ of its $1{,}000$ items, the released pair, bands fixed in
advance), and scored every arm against the item's reference with this paper's copying metrics and
CoTaEval's event, ROUGE-L~$\ge 0.5$. \emph{No arm reaches the event on any item, the unconstrained
risky model included} ($0/500$ each, Wilson upper bound $0.0076$). On mean ROUGE-L the risky model
sits closer to the reference than the anchor ($0.1589$ $[0.1554, 0.1624]$ against $0.1451$ $[0.1421,
0.1481]$); selection at $n=64$ is no closer than the anchor alone ($-0.0012$ $[-0.0052, +0.0027]$)
and further than the risky model ($-0.0150$ $[-0.0195, -0.0107]$; over non-empty draws alone
$-0.0148$ $[-0.0191, -0.0104]$, the reward having served an empty draft on one item), and the meter
at $k=10$ is the risky model ($+0.0004$ $[-0.0002, +0.0010]$). We predicted that even an adversarial
scorer would stay below the risky model, and it does not: the draw closest to the reference among
the first $64$ is closer than the risky model's one draw by $+0.0370$ $[+0.0336, +0.0404]$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} bounds amplification relative to the \emph{anchor}, not the risky
model, and a maximum over $64$ draws of a score this noisy beats one draw of either --- yet it too
reaches the event on no item
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak onset\_\allowbreak prediction\_\allowbreak cotaeval\_\allowbreak infringement.md}).

\textbf{Giving the \emph{metered} decoder the same reward model.} The metered decoder fuses
per-token probabilities while selection ranks whole sequences with an external reward model, so
selection's gain may be the scorer rather than the budget's placement. We gave the meter the
scorer. On the same $500$ TriviaQA questions and the one meterable pair, the metered decoder drew
$16$ trajectories per prompt at each of four budgets and every draw was scored by the
\emph{same} pointwise reward, through the same call, in the same pass as selection's own arm (Table~\ref{tab:parity}).
Best-of-$n$ over a decoder with sequence budget $K$ satisfies $q(y) \le n\,q_K(y)$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection}'s union bound, so its certificate is $K + \log n$: parity is
available and \emph{the budgets add}.

\subsection{The certificate in use}\label{app:sel-certificate}
\paragraph{The joint certificate over many queries, proved.}\label{app:compose}
Section~\ref{sec:compose} asserts that the certificate composes by addition; here is the
statement over $m$ queries. Let a user issue queries $x_1,\dots,x_m$, each answered by a
selection rule over $n$ draws, and let each $x_j$ be chosen \emph{adaptively} from everything served
so far. Write $q_j(\cdot \mid \mathcal{H}_{j-1})$ for the served law at round $j$ given the history
and $p_{s,j}$ for the anchor's. Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} applies at each round conditionally
on the history --- the $n$ draws at round $j$ are i.i.d.\ $p_{s,j}$ whatever the history is --- so
$q_j(y \mid \mathcal{H}_{j-1}) \le n\,p_{s,j}(y \mid \mathcal{H}_{j-1})$ pointwise. Multiplying the
$m$ inequalities along any realised history gives, for the joint laws $Q$ and $P_s$ of the whole
transcript,
\[
  Q(y_1,\dots,y_m) \;=\; \prod_{j \le m} q_j(y_j \mid \mathcal{H}_{j-1})
  \;\le\; n^{m} \prod_{j \le m} p_{s,j}(y_j \mid \mathcal{H}_{j-1})
  \;=\; n^{m}\,P_s(y_1,\dots,y_m),
\]
hence $D_\infty(Q \,\|\, P_s) \le m \log n$. The argument is one line because $D_\infty$ is a
supremum of a ratio and ratios multiply; nothing is lost to a union bound and nothing degrades with
adaptivity, which is why the figure is $m\log n$ exactly and not $m \log n$ plus a slack term.

Two contrasts are worth drawing. A per-query \emph{access-freeness} guarantee does not compose this
way in general \citep{cohen2025blameless}: $m$ individually blameless answers need not be jointly
blameless, because the event a transcript reveals is not the union of the events its answers reveal.
And a per-token meter composes the other way --- badly. Its sequence budget is $K = kT$ for one
response, so $m$ responses are certified at $mkT$, which grows in the length of every one of them;
the $\log n$ figure grows only in their number. That is the same asymmetry as the paper's main
claim, one level up.

\paragraph{A per-work certificate a rights-holder can audit.}\label{app:perwork}
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} turns the certificate into two numbers the protected party can
compute without the deployment: $n$, which the deployer declares, and $\Pr_{p_s}[E_x]$, which the
public anchor determines. For the exact event --- the continuation $x_{>c}$ of a work served for its
own prefix $x_{\le c}$ --- the second is a closed form, $e^{-S(x_{>c} \mid x_{\le c})}$, and after $m$
adaptive queries the bound is $m\,n\,e^{-S}$: at the $50$-token window our extraction is scored on,
$S_w = 159.8$ nats, it is below $10^{-60}$ at $n=64$ even after $m=10^4$ queries. A near-verbatim
event has no closed form, so the rights-holder samples: $N$ anchor draws on the work's prefixes with
no event bound $\Pr_{p_s}[E_x] \le 3/N$ at $95\%$, hence $\Pr_q[E_x] \le 3n/N$. Certifying one work at
$1\%$ with $n=64$ takes $19{,}200$ draws of the anchor; our $n=256$ pools --- $256$ draws on each of
$100$ passages, none reaching ROUGE-L $0.3$ (Table~\ref{tab:extraction}) --- certify the average passage
at $3 \times 64/25{,}600 = 0.75\%$. Both computations need only the anchor's weights, which is what
makes the certificate auditable by the party it protects. The same audit applies to a pathwise-metered
decoder with $e^{K}$ in place of $n$, where at the published $k = 0.5$ the multiplier is
$e^{100} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{43}$, and no sample bounds an event below its reciprocal.

\paragraph{The channel the certificate prices, measured.}\label{app:channel}
One response carries at most $\log_2 n$ bits about anything, because $q \le n\,p_s$ for any score,
so a scorer colluding with a receiver can use selection as a channel and the certificate prices
exactly that. We measured it rather than leave it a bound. The scorer and the receiver share a key;
the scorer serves a draw whose keyed hash falls in the cell that encodes the next $b$ bits of a
message, a draw in a reserved erasure cell if none does, and the receiver reads the bits off the
served text alone. Every response is an unmodified anchor draw. Over the committed pools, with
$(b, \epsilon)$ chosen from the i.i.d.\ formulas and never from the data, it realises $1.32$, $2.22$
and $3.97$ bits per response at $n = 8, 16, 64$ against $\log_2 n = 3, 4, 6$: two-thirds of the bound
at $n=64$, short of the i.i.d.\ $4.23$ because $12.6\%$ of the draws are duplicates, which hash alike.
A protected $50$-token window compressed with a public code is $1{,}423$ bits, so it takes $357$
responses at $n=64$ and $237$ even at capacity; and the $400$-nat per-user odometer stops the channel
at $\lfloor 400/\ln n \rfloor = 96$ responses and $400/\ln 2 = 577$ bits whatever $n$ is --- less than
one compressed window. Only a code built on the anchor itself fits under it: the window's surprisal,
$159.8$ nats, is $231$ bits. The per-response certificate is a real capacity, a simple scheme reaches
most of it, and what binds a patient user is the composition over queries, $m\log n$.

\paragraph{One certificate, spent on the draw, on the token, or split.}\label{app:hybrid}
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} holds for a pool drawn from any law $q_B$ with $q_B \le e^{B}p_s$
pathwise, so best-of-$n$ over drafts from the pathwise-metered decoder at budget $B$ is certified at
$\log n + B$, and one certificate $C$ can be split between the draw and the token. At the headline's
$C = \log 64$ we registered three splits against pure selection --- best-of-$8$ over drafts at
$B = \log 8$, best-of-$2$ at $B = \log 32$, and the pathwise meter alone at $B = \log 64$, prefix debt
off since $B$ is below the debt a prompt starts with --- and judged them against the committed opponent
in one order-averaged pass. The level rises monotonically with the share of $C$ spent on the draw,
$0.4400$, $0.4610$, $0.4995$ and $0.5550$, and every split loses to pure selection: $-0.1150$ $[-0.1380,
-0.0915]$ for the meter alone, $-0.0940$ $[-0.1170, -0.0715]$ and $-0.0555$ $[-0.0780, -0.0330]$ for
the two hybrids. The meter given the whole certificate is judged no better than a single anchor draw
($0.4400$ against $0.4535$), and at fixed $n=8$ the tilt is worth $-0.0120$ $[-0.0300, +0.0060]$. At a
budget of a few nats, a certificate buys more as draws than as tokens. The drafts are the first pool
recorded without the defect of Appendix~\ref{app:judgemethod}, on which the scorer serves more empty
completions ($39\%$ against $22\%$ at $n=8$); at fixed $n$ that does not separate the arms.

\paragraph{How loose is $\log n$? Nearly tight for long outputs, and strict where draws repeat.}\label{app:realisedkl}
If no two candidates can share a score the served law is $q(y) = n\,p_s(y)\,F(f(y))^{n-1}$, with $F$
the score CDF under $p_s$, so $\log\!\big(q(y)/p_s(y)\big)$ approaches $\log n$ as $F \to 1$; and since
$F(f(y^*))$ under $q$ is then the maximum of $n$ uniforms --- a $\mathrm{Beta}(n,1)$, with
$\mathbb{E}[\log F] = -1/n$ --- the KL is $\log n - (n-1)/n$. Neither figure is \emph{attained} by a
discrete $p_s$, because a string can be drawn twice: the top-scoring $y^\star$ with
$p = p_s(y^\star)$ is served with probability $1-(1-p)^n < np$, so both are upper bounds, approached
only as $p \to 0$. For $200$-token completions $p$ is astronomically small and a repeated draw
essentially never occurs; what moves the realised figures there is a tie in the \emph{score}, which
spreads the served law over the tied set and lowers both divergences. Measured on the
headline arm's $32{,}000$
cached rewards (\texttt{analysis/\allowbreak selection\_\allowbreak realised\_\allowbreak kl.py},
\texttt{results/\allowbreak selection\_\allowbreak realised\_\allowbreak kl.csv}), that rate is
$5.0\%$ at $n=2$, peaks at $13.8\%$ at $n=8$, and falls to $7.0\%$ at $n=64$, with a mean tied-set
size of $1.31$ where it occurs, so the quoted figures are within a vanishing margin of the realised
ones on $86$--$95\%$ of prompts and conservative on the rest. Where draws \emph{do} repeat --- short
answers under majority vote, a rule that reads only the answer class, so that its served law can be
computed rather than bounded --- the realised pathwise divergence at $n=64$ is $0.92$ nats at the
median question on GSM8K and $1.02$ on TriviaQA, and at most $1.97$ and $2.43$ over $500$
questions, against a certificate of $4.16$; its KL is $0.73$ and $0.68$ at the median against a
bound of $3.17$ (post hoc, a plug-in over the $64$ cached draws per question;
\texttt{results/\allowbreak majority\_\allowbreak realised\_\allowbreak divergence\_\allowbreak note.md}).

A consequence worth stating: the certificate does not depend on the scorer being good, or even on
its being the same scorer twice. Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} bounds $q(y) \le n\,p_s(y)$ for
\emph{any} score and \emph{any} tie rule, so the bf16 non-determinism that changes \emph{which}
candidate is served --- on $5.6\%$ of prompts at $n=64$ from a batch-size change alone within one
host, and $8.6\%$ across two hosts
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak selection\_\allowbreak argmax\_\allowbreak stability\_\allowbreak note.md})
--- cannot loosen it by a single nat. The guarantee is numerically robust exactly where the
realisation is not.

\subsection{What selection costs}\label{app:sel-cost}
\paragraph{The cost frontier, and the scorer that is too small to rescue it.}\label{app:frontier}
Selection's price \emph{in FLOPs} is set by the reward model, not the anchor: at $n$ it runs
$n(P_s+P_f)(L_p+T)$ forward-pass FLOPs against the metered decoder's $(P_s+P_r)(L_p+T)$, with
$P_f=7.6$ and $P_s=1.8$, so a $0.5$B scorer divides the price by $4.16$ at every $n$. We specified
that route in advance and it fails. Re-scoring the same $32{,}000$ cached candidates with four
scorers on the identical template and judging them over the whole nested grid --- both orders, one
true prompt, one fixed opponent, replicating the head-to-head first at $+0.1075$ against the
$+0.1045$ on record and the meter's $+0.0400$ exactly --- gives $n=64$ gains of $+0.0230$, $+0.0930$,
$+0.0970$ and $+0.1065$ at $0.494$, $1.54$, $3.09$ and $7.62$B. \textbf{The $0.5$B scorer never
reaches the meter}: its best cell, $n=16$ at $3.68\times$, gains $+0.0395\,[+0.0205,+0.0585]$ ---
indistinguishable from the meter and five ten-thousandths below it, where the rule asked for above
--- and \textbf{at matched compute, $n=4$ at $0.92\times$, it loses outright,
$-0.0395\,[-0.0720,-0.0065]$}. Shrinking the scorer from $7.6$B to $0.494$B costs $-0.0855$ of the
gain at $n=64$. We predicted three of these four wrong and in the paper's favour. The $0.5$B curve peaks at $n=16$
and ends lower, which reads as though $\log n$ is not a free knob; \textbf{that reading did not
survive} its registered test, the terminal drop $g(64)-g(16)$, which measured
$-0.0160\,[-0.0340,+0.0010]$ and reads \textsc{flat}. What the sample shows as description is a difference in
shape: Spearman between gain and $\log n$ is $0.5429$ for the $0.5$B scorer and exactly $1.0$ ---
strictly monotone at all six grid points --- for each of $1.5$B, $3$B and $7.6$B.

\paragraph{Scorer capability saturates, and it saturates early.}\label{app:saturation}
Only the first of the three adjacent steps at $n=64$ separates: $+0.0700\,[+0.0500,+0.0900]$ from
$0.5$B to $1.5$B, then $+0.0040\,[-0.0145,+0.0225]$ and $+0.0095\,[-0.0090,+0.0285]$. A scorer must
clear a bar; past it, a $4.9\times$ larger scorer buys $+0.0135$ and neither step of it separates.
\textbf{Two further rungs, run after the judge-free ladder below showed no saturation at all, do
not change that and make it non-monotone}: in one pass over all six scorers the gain at $n=64$ is
$+0.1095$ at $7.6$B, $+0.0810$ at $14.8$B and $+0.1010$ at $72.7$B, so against the $7.6$B rung a
$14.8$B scorer reads $-0.0285\,[-0.0470,-0.0110]$ and a $72.7$B one $-0.0085\,[-0.0255,+0.0085]$.
\emph{Neither lifts the judged gain}, both readings sit inside this paper's own $2.0$-half-width
marginality rule, and at $n=64$ the $72.7$B scorer costs $486.9\times$ the metered decoder for a
gain indistinguishable from the $7.6$B one's. We registered that saturation would hold and it does. A
$1.5$B scorer reaches $87.3\%$ of the $7.6$B scorer's gain for $35.2\%$ of its forward-pass cost, so
on this workload the $61.3\times$ is the price of the scorer we happened to use and not of the
mechanism --- a reading the measured clock below contradicts outright in the currency a deployer pays
in. No band was specified on the cost column, so it is post hoc. \textbf{One instability we do not
hide}: a crossing \emph{cell} is a thresholded statistic and inherits none of the stability the gains
showed --- the $7.6$B scorer's crossing sits at $n=8$ and $7.66\times$ in one pass and $n=16$ and
$15.32\times$ in the other, because one cell read $+0.0415$ and then $+0.0360$, a difference of
$0.0055$ far inside the judge's floor that happens to straddle the meter's $+0.0400$. We therefore
quote the $n=64$ column, which moved by $0.001$ between passes. Without a judge the reward caches
give the same ordering: against the $7.6$B reference the within-prompt Spearman is $0.1333$,
$0.4185$ and $0.4926$, and the rate of serving the same draw at $n=64$ is $0.052$, $0.132$ and
$0.228$ against $0.016$ by chance. The certificate is untouched throughout: $q(y)\le n\,p_s(y)$
holds for any score, so every one of these $24$ cells is certified at $\log n$ whatever the scorer
costs or achieves.

\paragraph{Should a deployer just buy a bigger safe model?}\label{app:biggeranchor}
A larger safe model served \emph{once} needs no scorer, no draws and no certificate, since a safe model
is trivially within any budget of itself; Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} makes the anchor the ceiling,
so if the larger model matches selection outright the mechanism buys nothing a cheque could not. The
comparison needs no new generation: the Comma-7B arm exists at $n=64$ over the same $500$ prompts and
its rank-$0$ draw \emph{is} Comma-7B served alone. Judged against TinyComma served alone with every item
in both orders, Comma-7B alone --- $3.9\times$ the anchor, no scorer, no selection --- gains $+0.0150$
$[-0.0065, +0.0365]$, while TinyComma at $n=64$ gains $+0.1045$ $[+0.0820, +0.1280]$ and Comma-7B at
$n=64$ gains $+0.1755$ $[+0.1505, +0.2010]$. The bigger anchor alone does not separate from the small
one; selection on the small one does, and on the paired difference of the two served arms, which needs
no control, it is $-0.0995\,[-0.1220,-0.0765]$, negative with its interval excluding zero. The cost cuts
the same way: on the forward-pass model the bigger anchor is $86\times$ \emph{cheaper} than selection at
$n=64$, so the cheap alternative was available, a deployer would try it first, and on this workload it
does not measurably work.

\paragraph{The cheapest instance is also the best one, where an answer is checkable.}\label{app:scorerfree}
In forward passes the reward model is what selection costs. \textbf{Majority vote has no such term.}

costs no forward pass: $n\,P_s(L_p+T)$. It carries the identical $\log n$ certificate, because
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} assumes nothing whatever about the score and a mode is a score. Joining
that cost model to the accuracies of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, sorted by price
(Table~\ref{tab:scorerfree}):

\paragraph{Stopping early saves draws and buys nothing the matched $n$ does not.}\label{app:adaptive}
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} needs only that one of the draws is served, so a rule that draws one
at a time and stops at the first reward above a threshold $\tau$, capped at $n_{\max}$, is certified at
$\log n_{\max}$ however early it stops, and its price is its mean number of draws. With $\tau$ a
quantile of the first draw's reward and $n_{\max}=64$, $q=0.75$ stops after $6.8$ draws on average
and loses to fixed $n=64$ by $-0.0515$ $[-0.0720, -0.0310]$, but against fixed $n=7$, the same expected
draws, it reads $+0.0040$ $[-0.0125, +0.0205]$; $q=0.90$ uses $17.4$ draws, $27\%$ of the budget,
straddles zero against both $n=64$ ($-0.0155$ $[-0.0340, +0.0020]$) and $n=17$. Judged in one
order-averaged pass against the committed opponent; on the reward alone the same holds, except at
about two draws, where the threshold beats fixed $n=2$. A deployer who wants fewer draws should
lower $n$, not add a threshold.

\subsection{The premise, and leakage}\label{app:sel-premise}
\paragraph{Vetting the anchor: the premise is measurable, and we measured it.}\label{app:vetting}
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} bounds $\Pr_q[E]$ by $n\Pr_{p_s}[E]$, so everything rests on the
anchor's own $\Pr_{p_s}[E]$ being small --- and open-licensed corpora carry only partial provenance
guarantees. A deployer need not take it on faith. $\Pr_{p_s}[E]$ is the one quantity in the
construction measurable with no attack, no risky model and no access to the mechanism: draw from the
candidate anchor on the protected passages' own prefixes and score near-verbatim recall. Run at one
protocol --- a hundred raw tokens on the same fifty passages, every model in the anchor slot, seeds
verified byte-identical to the control's, fixed in advance --- \textbf{all six anchors used here
read exactly $0.000$} (TinyComma in the anchor slot of the $70$B's own run), while \texttt{OLMo-2-7B} leaks on $2$ of $50$ passages and
\texttt{OLMo-2-13B} on $6$, reproducing up to $0.566$ of one, and \texttt{Llama-3.1-70B}, which
memorised the work in pre-training, leaks on $0.500$. Neither OLMo-2 is admissible as an anchor, and
that is what makes Section~\ref{sec:experiments}'s exclusion of the open-\emph{data} families a
measurement rather than an assertion. Across the three web-trained models the fraction rises with
scale, $0.040$, $0.120$, $0.500$ at $7$B, $13$B and $70$B, which was not specified in advance, rests
on three points and confounds scale with corpus, so we report it as a pattern rather than a result.

\textbf{The prefix length is part of the test.} Screened instead at a $20$-token seed with the
\texttt{Complete the prefix:} header attached --- about fourteen tokens of the work once the header
is paid for --- the licensed anchors read zero and twelve models fine-tuned on these very passages leak
on most of them, but at that protocol the $70$B \emph{also} reads $0.0000$: a base model that
memorised a work in pre-training needs a long genuine prefix to re-enter it, so at fourteen tokens
this screen cannot tell a clean anchor from a model that holds the book, and a separation read
across the two protocols would be a comparison between protocols. The test is also sound but not
complete: near-verbatim recall at a given prefix length is a lower bound on contamination, so
\emph{passing} is evidence and not proof while \emph{failing} is a demonstration, and like a
blocklist it speaks only about the works handed to it. What a deployer takes away is that the
premise is a measurement rather than an assumption, that it costs $n$ forward passes and no attack,
and that the prefix length must be quoted beside the number.

\textbf{Which prefix, then: measured rather than argued.} The certificate holds prompt by prompt,
$\Pr_q[E \mid x] \le n \Pr_{p_s}[E \mid x]$, and the user chooses $x$, so the prefix is not a
parameter of the test but part of the attack surface. We re-ran the screen with the prefix length as
the only change, from $20$ to $200$ raw tokens, bands fixed in advance (Table~\ref{tab:vetladder};
\texttt{results/\allowbreak onset\_\allowbreak prediction\_\allowbreak vetting\_\allowbreak ladder.md}).
A hundred tokens was the one protocol with demonstrated power when we chose it, and the ladder prices
the choice: the $70$B is nearly invisible at $20$ and $35$ tokens ($1$ and $2$ of $50$ passages), is
seen from $50$ ($11$), and leaks on $31$ at $150$, where the screen at a hundred finds $25$.
\textbf{The screen is not monotone in the prefix}, against our registered prediction:
\texttt{OLMo-2-13B} leaks on $6$ passages at $100$ and at $150$ and on $3$ at $200$, a fall of three
--- exactly the threshold fixed in advance --- at the one rung where only $57$--$94$ tokens of each
passage remain to be reproduced, a confound declared before any rung ran. It still reproduces one
passage in full there, so the screen did not go blind; fewer passages crossed its line. No single
length dominates, so the recommendation is a schedule, not a length: \emph{screen at every prefix
length up to the longest genuine prefix the deployment accepts}. \textbf{The anchors' pass survives
a longer adversarial prefix}: all six read $0$ of $50$ at $150$ and at $200$ tokens, twice the prefix
the screen used. Their rungs \emph{below} a hundred were left out of
that design, on the premise that a shorter prefix gives less to lock onto; once the screen proved not
monotone that premise was itself unmeasured, so we ran them too, bands fixed in advance: all six read
$0$ of $50$ at $20$, $35$, $50$ and $75$ tokens as well, and so pass at every rung from $20$ to $200$. A
pass at every rung is still evidence, not proof. Twenty-six of the new rungs ran on a second machine
when this one had no card free, declared before each ran; the memoriser's own seeded draw, re-run there as a host check, reaches recall $0.01$ on $82$ of
$100$ passages against $78$ here ($z = 0.71$).

\paragraph{What the two charges buy a user under an odometer.}
Under a per-user cap of $400$ nats --- half the anchor's median surprisal of a protected passage ---
selection at $n=8$ admits $192$ queries on its pathwise charge of $2.079$ nats and $332$ on the sharper
KL bound $\log 8 - 7/8 = 1.204$ \citep{beirami2025bestofn}, against $2$ for a metered decoder at $k=3$ on
its \emph{realised} mean spend of $165.0$ nats and $0$ on the $600$ it publishes as $K = kT_{\max}$.
Three things follow. Max-divergence adds \emph{exactly} under adaptive composition, so $192$ needs no
advanced composition theorem and no assumption about how a user chooses their queries --- the property
\citet{cohen2025blameless} shows per-query access-freeness lacks in general, and why the pathwise order
is worth its extra $0.875$ nats per query. The $332$ is what a deployed odometer metering KL spend would
permit, quoted so the two are never conflated. And the metered decoder is given twice for the same
reason: at its measured spend a user gets $2$ queries, while at the published budget a single
$200$-token response is already certified at $600$ nats, so the odometer admits none. Selection's charge

\paragraph{The judge-free axis: protocol and the full grids.}\label{app:judgefree}

\textbf{MMLU, the third task, and why a forced choice was the one to add.} GSM8K and TriviaQA are
both open-ended generation, so selection's lift there could be an artefact of the format --- more
draws, more chances at a well-formed string. MMLU is a four-way forced choice: there is no string
to get right and the floor is $0.25$. At Comma-7B, $5$-shot, $T_{\max}=24$, on the first $500$ of
the shuffled test set, majority vote gains $+0.084$ $[+0.042, +0.126]$ at $n=64$ over its own $n=1$
control of $0.404$, peaking at $n=32$ with $+0.108$ $[+0.066, +0.150]$, Spearman $0.955$ against
$\log n$. The pointwise reward gains $+0.056$ $[+0.008, +0.104]$ and is the less monotone of the
two (Spearman $0.750$). The lift survives the format change, and the cheapest instance of the
mechanism is again the better one.

Two entry checks were registered before the run and both are reported. The anchor must beat chance:
it reads $0.404$ $[0.362, 0.448]$, clear of $0.25$. And the unconstrained risky model must read at
least $0.55$, since its published $5$-shot MMLU is near $0.68$: it reads $0.676$ greedy and $0.606$
sampled. \textbf{That second check exists because an earlier attempt failed it.} The same protocol
at TinyComma-1.8B --- the only anchor a metered decoder shares a vocabulary with, so the only one a
judge-free \emph{head-to-head} could run at --- first scored the risky model at $0.276$, because
that anchor echoes the prompt's tail before answering and our parser read only the completion's
first line. Repaired, the parser reads $88.6\%$ of that anchor's completions and $100\%$ of the
risky model's, and TinyComma then reads $0.188$ $[0.154, 0.222]$: \emph{below} the $0.25$ floor,
with $49\%$ of its answers the letter B. The registered gate calls that outcome invalid rather than
negative, so no number from it is in this paper, and the judge-free comparison \emph{against a
metered decoder} therefore remains one task. The arms are scored in
\texttt{results/\allowbreak onset\_\allowbreak prediction\_\allowbreak mmlu\_\allowbreak rescore.md}
and \texttt{results/\allowbreak onset\_\allowbreak prediction\_\allowbreak mmlu\_\allowbreak comma7b.md}.

Every other utility number in this paper is a pairwise judged preference and the judge is dominated by
position (Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}). Exact match is not, so two arms measure the constructive claim
with no judge in the loop. Both run at Comma-7B --- the anchor carrying the largest judged gain --- on
the same $n$ grid as the judged experiments, at temperature $0.7$, and both score the \emph{served}
string, which Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} bounds only because it is one of the $n$ draws: the tie
rule returns an index into the sample, never a synthesised answer. \textbf{Reasoning} is GSM8K's first
$500$ test problems, $8$-shot, $256$-token cap, $64$ samples per problem drawn once and read nested, so
$n \in \{1,\dots,64\}$ are prefixes of one draw and no sample is scored twice; scoring is the benchmark's
own, the final integer after \texttt{\#\#\#\#}, cut at the first run-on into the next few-shot question,
because a base model continues past its answer. \textbf{Knowledge} is TriviaQA \texttt{rc.nocontext},
the first $500$ validation questions, $5$-shot, $24$-token cap, $64$ samples, scored by
\emph{containment}: a gold alias appearing as a whole-word span in the normalised answer line. Exact
match here would score format rather than knowledge --- a base model emits \texttt{David Seville} and an
instruction-tuned one \texttt{The answer is David Seville}, and a smoke run scored the latter $0/8$ on
questions it had right --- so the rule was fixed before any number, and the $24$-token cap bounds what a
verbose completion can sweep up by accident. It applies identically to the draws, the selected outputs
and both baselines.

Neither arm reads a band unless a number was extracted from $90\%$ of its $500 \times 64$ samples: GSM8K
yields one on $99.96\%$ and TriviaQA on $98.67\%$. Both carry the mandatory baselines on the same
questions and seeds --- $k=0$ is the $n=1$ row and $k=-1$ is \texttt{Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct} alone,
$0.786$ greedy and $0.726$ sampled on GSM8K, $0.722$ and $0.674$ on TriviaQA. Those are descriptive: the
risky model is instruction-tuned and the anchor is not, and the comparison this paper makes is between
two ways of spending a budget over one anchor. On GSM8K both rules are monotone in $\log n$ (Spearman
$+0.955$ and $+0.929$); on TriviaQA only one is, which is the whole of what separates the two tasks.

\textbf{What separates the two tasks is the support, and it was predicted.} The TriviaQA protocol
specified, before the run, that majority vote would lift by \emph{less} than on GSM8K, and gave the
reason: selection re-ranks draws, and a closed-book question offers less spread between them than a
multi-step derivation where one arithmetic slip decides the answer. The measured ratio is $4.2\times$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} is why the direction is not a surprise --- $q(y) \le n\,p_s(y)$ means
the served string is one the anchor would have drawn, so no $n$ supplies a fact the anchor does not have
--- but the size of the gap is a measurement that could have refuted the prediction.

\textbf{Self-consistency is the mechanism, not an analogy.} Nothing in
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} mentions a reward: majority vote returns one of the $n$ draws, so it
satisfies the same $q(y) \le n\,p_s(y)$ under the same tie rule. The most widely deployed inference-time
method in the literature therefore already carries a $\log n$ pathwise certificate that nobody has had
to add to it, and these grids show the certificate costs nothing it was not already paying: the accuracy
is what self-consistency delivers anyway, and the $4.159$~nats at $n=64$ are the whole charge.

\textbf{The metered decoder wins the level by $0.428$, and the way it wins is the paper's claim.}
Run on the same questions its winning arm is $k=20$, where its accuracy is the unconstrained risky
model's $0.618$ $[0.576, 0.660]$ to three decimals --- it has stopped being a constrained decoder ---
and the certificate there is $480$ nats for a $24$-token answer against a realised mean spend of
$44.8473$. It gains nothing at its small budgets: $0.112$ at $k=0$ and
$k=0.5$ ($12$ nats), $0.114$ at $k=1$ ($24$ nats) and $0.166$ at $k=3$ ($72$ nats),
against selection's $0.146$, $0.174$ and $0.190$ at $n=8,32,64$ for $2.079$, $3.466$ and $4.159$. The mechanism is useful exactly where its certificate
is worthless and carries a meaningful certificate exactly where it is useless, on an axis with no judge
in it. The two currencies are not the same kind of quantity: a metered spend is a measured realisation
while selection's KL figure is the closed-form bound $\log n - (n-1)/n$ \citep{beirami2025bestofn} that
no draw exceeds, so every comparison here runs in the conservative direction. \textbf{The frontier
survives the loss}: at every accuracy selection buys over the shared control it buys it for $0.032$,
$0.061$, $0.093$, $0.056$ and $0.071$ of what the cheapest metered arm reaching that accuracy spends,
at $n=4,8,16,32,64$. Two things are disclosed rather than smoothed --- arms that bought nothing are
excluded and had to be, since the cheapest metered arm ``reaching'' the anchor's own $0.112$ is the
anchor at zero nats; and on the \emph{pathwise} axis two of the five ratios rise above a tenth
($0.106$ and $0.141$), which sets selection's strongest-order bound against the meter's weakest-order
spend and so is not like for like, but $\log n$ is the certificate the headline rests on and the less
favourable reading is on the record. \textbf{Every budget on this grid is vacuous, and selection's is vacuous on a third of the
questions too.} We measured the missing quantity rather than asserting it: teacher-forcing the
anchor on the accepted answer aliases under the prompt the decoder was actually served gives a
median $S(x)$ of $5.86$ nats (p10 $1.35$, p90 $12.14$), against a smallest metered budget of $12$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} the metered certificate is therefore vacuous on $89.6\%$ of
these questions at $k=0.5$, $99.4\%$ at $k=1$ and $100\%$ at $k=3$ and $k=20$ --- there is no
budget on this grid at which it certifies anything about most of these answers. Applying the same
threshold to selection is less comfortable and is reported for that reason: $\log n$ is vacuous on
$10.4\%$ of the questions at $n=4$ and on $35.4\%$ at $n=64$. What separates the two here is not
that one is vacuous and the other is not, but that selection's \emph{worst} budget is vacuous less
often than the meter's \emph{best}. Both figures are lower bounds, since scoring the single most
likely alias rather than their sum overstates $S(x)$ and so understates vacuity. \emph{Vacuous}
here means what it means everywhere in this paper --- the budget permits the served law to place
probability $1$ on the event, so the certificate constrains nothing about what is served --- and
not that anything was leaked: a correct answer is a desirable output, and the claim is about the
certificate's informativeness at this granularity, which is the paper's thesis in the one place
the mechanism is measurably useful. No leakage or protected-text $s(x)$ number comes from this
arm: TriviaQA answers are not protected text, and every cap here is $k\,T_{\max}$ at
$T_{\max}=24$.

\textbf{Intervals on the two lift ratios, and one ratio that may not be quoted.} The two ratios
the paper quotes are different \emph{kinds} of
quantity and are treated accordingly
(\texttt{analysis/\allowbreak lift\_\allowbreak ratio\_\allowbreak interval.py}). Within GSM8K,
majority vote and the pointwise reward select from the \emph{same} draws on the \emph{same} $500$
questions, so their gains are correlated (bootstrap correlation $+0.503$) and the resampling is
paired: $3.42$ $[2.20, 8.91]$, excluding $1$, undefined on $0.17\%$ of resamples. Across tasks the
question sets are disjoint, so the series are independent by construction and the vote lift on
GSM8K against the vote lift on TriviaQA is $4.19$ $[2.63, 8.40]$. Both intervals are
\emph{strongly asymmetric} --- the denominators are small gains --- which is the reason to report
them: a reader given only ``$3.4\times$'' cannot tell how long that upper tail is.

We use a paired bootstrap rather than Fieller's theorem, which would need bivariate normality of
the two gains and their correlation supplied from outside; resampling questions carries the
correlation exactly and assumes no distribution. Computed anyway from the same resamples' moments,
Fieller gives $[2.20, 8.81]$ against the bootstrap's $[2.20, 8.91]$, so the choice does not matter
where the denominator is resolved --- and it matters entirely where it is not. \textbf{A third
ratio is therefore not quotable and we do not quote it}: TriviaQA's vote-over-reward ratio has a
\emph{negative} denominator, since the reward gain falls with $n$ there, and the quotient is
undefined on $82\%$ of resamples. An interval computed over the $18\%$ that remain is conditioned
on exactly the draws that behaved, which is the defect this project has already been caught by
twice in threshold crossings; the entry in
\texttt{results/\allowbreak lift\_\allowbreak ratio\_\allowbreak interval.csv} is blank, with the
undefined fraction beside it, rather than a narrow-looking number.

\textbf{And with the judge removed, the compute concession is larger, not smaller.} The
compute-matched head-to-head in Appendix~\ref{app:frontier} rests on judge~B, and the judge panel
has since split on the headline difference, so we put the same question on this axis. The metered
decoder's serving cost \emph{does not depend on $k$} --- it runs both models at every step whatever
the budget --- so its entire accuracy range is available at one cost, while selection pays
$n\,P_{\mathrm{anchor}}$ against its $P_{\mathrm{anchor}} + P_{\mathrm{risky}}$, reaching parity at
$n = 5.57$. Majority vote needs no scorer, so this costing is the one most favourable to the meter
available. At its own serving cost selection reaches about $0.13$ against the meter's $0.618$, and
no $n$ on the grid closes the gap: at $11.5\times$ the compute selection is still at $0.190$
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak compute\_\allowbreak matched\_\allowbreak judgefree.csv}).
\textbf{The metered decoder wins the compute-matched comparison on this axis outright}, by more
than it wins the judged one.

What it pays is the whole of our claim. Its $0.618$ arrives only at $k=20$: a certificate of $480$
nats for a $24$-token answer, $115\times$ selection's $4.159$ at $n=64$ and vacuous by
Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold}. At its two smallest budgets it gains
nothing --- $0.112$ at $k=0.5$ ($12$ nats) and $0.114$ at $k=1$ ($24$ nats), against the anchor's
own $0.112$ --- while selection at $n=8$ reaches $0.146$ for $2.079$. So the two are not points on
one frontier: \textbf{the meter buys accuracy with compute cheaply and with certificate dearly, and
selection the reverse}. A deployer with a budget to honour cannot spend compute to escape it ---
that is what ``gains nothing at its two smallest budgets'' means --- and a deployer with compute to
burn and no certificate to honour needs neither mechanism. The $115\times$ is a bound against a bound and so,
unlike the nats comparisons elsewhere here, is like for like.

Against a naturally memorising adversary the same holds. Four arms were needed to measure the $70$B at
all, and the first three are ours rather than the mechanism's: arm 1 handed a \emph{base} model an
instruction where the protocol specifies a raw passage seed; arm 2 fixed that and still recovered
nothing, because the $0.4137$ it was predicted against had been measured at a $100$-token seed and the
arm used $20$; arm 3 used the right seed length and the wrong book, the held-out split holding $150$
passages across three novels where a plain limit of $50$ returns the first of them. Only arm 4 --- $100$
raw tokens, the $50$ \emph{Harry Potter} passages the reference was measured on --- selects the corpus
the number belongs to. \textbf{It extracts.} Unaided, the $70$B recovers $0.2475$ mean near-verbatim
recall across the $50$ passages, clears the threshold on $25$ of them, and reproduces \textbf{two of
fifty in full} --- near-verbatim recall exactly $1.0000$, the entire decoded target, from a $100$-token
prefix. It is weaker on average than the memoriser we built ($0.3925$) and stronger at the top, which
never reproduces a passage completely. Against it, selection anchoring with the $70$B's \emph{own}
likelihood as the scorer --- the adversary choosing among the anchor's $n$ draws whichever is closest to
the protected text --- recovers $0.0000$ at $n=1$, $n=8$ and $n=64$, maximum over all $50$ passages
$0.0000$, longest common substring never above three words. The anchor's own base rate is zero and $n$
multiplies it. Every band, the admission criterion and the stop rule were fixed before the run, including
the rule that a gate failure would end the line of work and be reported as a limit on the evidence rather
than a property of the mechanism.

\paragraph{Outside English prose.}\label{app:multilingual}
Every extraction number above is English. We built an adversary outside it: $128$ training and $32$
validation passages from four public-domain works in French and German (\emph{Notre-Dame de Paris},
\emph{Le Comte de Monte-Cristo}, \emph{Der Prozess}, \emph{Die Leiden des jungen Werther}), splits
disjoint in book so the held-out split is genuinely held out, each passage matching the committed
corpora's $930$-character seed and $250$-character reference exactly. These are public-domain works
used as stand-ins for protected ones, as the English second-corpus arm already does. The anchor is
\texttt{Pleias-3B} rather than the audited one, because Common Corpus is multilingual and
permissively licensed and so satisfies the paper's premise while being able to write the language ---
an anchor that could not would make ``selection reproduces nothing'' true and uninformative.

The recipe transfers unmodified, and the adversary it produces is \emph{stronger} than the English
one: sampled $k=-1$ near-verbatim recall $0.5455$ (max $0.9882$) with $94\%$ of passages above
$0.01$ and $83\%$ at ROUGE-L $\ge 0.5$, against the English memoriser's $0.3925$ and $78\%$. We had
registered the opposite risk --- that a rank-$64$ LoRA tuned on English might not memorise these
languages and the arm would be inadmissible --- and that prediction failed in the direction that
makes the test harder for us.

Against it, selection reads $0.0000$ near-verbatim recall at $n = 1$, $8$ and $64$, maximum
$0.0000$, with $\texttt{lcs\_word}$ of $1.51$--$1.53$ words (function words, not text) and ROUGE-L
$0.070$--$0.082$ against the memoriser's $0.678$; $0$ of $100$ passages reach ROUGE-L $\ge 0.3$
where the memoriser reaches $93\%$. The anchor's own leakage --- the quantity $n$ multiplies --- is
$0.0000$, so this is a clean result and not a clean arm over a contaminated anchor. Bands were
fixed before the memoriser was trained
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak onset\_\allowbreak prediction\_\allowbreak multilingual.md}), including
the entry gate and the rule that H2 would not be reported as a success if the anchor turned out
contaminated.

What this does \emph{not} establish is that selection serves good French. The anchor's utility in
these languages is unmeasured, and that is the open question this arm leaves in place of the one it
answers.

\subsection{When the premise fails}\label{app:sel-contaminated}
\paragraph{What the certificate delivers when its premise fails.}\label{app:contaminated}
Every leakage result above has a clean anchor, and Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} bounds an
\emph{event}: $\Pr_q[E] \le n \Pr_{p_s}[E]$. With $\Pr_{p_s}[E] = 0$ that inequality is satisfied by
any number we could have measured, so it has been reported as confirmed and never tested. It is also
the mechanism's one failure mode, so we ran selection anchoring, with the same adversarial selector,
over \textbf{twelve deliberately memorised anchors} --- LoRA copies fine-tuned on the protected
passages, spanning GPT-NeoX, Llama, Mixtral, Phi and Qwen at $170$M to $7$B --- on the same $100$
passages and seeds, all twelve reproducing the memorising model's own control at $0.3925$ mean
recall, which is twelve independent checks that only the anchor changed.
\textbf{The selector indexes each padded candidate from its first real token}: a version that read
the left-padded batch as right-padded ranked partly on padding, and no number from the defective run
is quoted; the

the served rate at $256$ tracks the fraction of passages with any leaking draw, because the corrected
selector finds one whenever it exists. Selection is a multiplier on what the anchor already leaks,
not a floor under it, and the actionable consequence is that the anchor is the thing to vet.

Two limits. The factors are counts over $100$ passages --- $A(64) = 7.0$ is seven passages over
one, and one more at $n=1$ would have read $3.5$; on the second draw the base was three and the

$A(64) \le 4$ at every anchor, is crossed by that one anchor on the first draw and by none on the
second: whether the realised factor stays inside it is \emph{draw-dependent}, and we give both readings
rather than either. Both draws agree that the same six anchors never enter $E$, that the bound is
vacuous wherever one does, and that the served rate does not measurably grow from $64$ to $256$: the
most any anchor gains is three passages against none lost ($p = 0.25$), on each draw.

\subsection{The judge, in full}\label{app:judgemethod}
\paragraph{Protocol.} Every judged number compares one arm's served text with the fixed opponent's
on the same prompt. The judge receives a single user turn, in its own chat template:
\begin{quote}\footnotesize\ttfamily
You are grading two candidate responses to the same instruction.\\
Instruction: \{prompt\}\quad Response A: \{a\}\quad Response B: \{b\}\\
Which response is the better answer to the instruction? Judge helpfulness, coherence and factual
accuracy. Ignore length and formatting. If they are of equal quality, answer Tie. Reply with
exactly one word: A, B, or Tie.
\end{quote}
Each field is truncated to $1{,}200$ characters; the prompt is the corpus prompt with the harness's
\texttt{Complete the prefix:} header removed; decoding is greedy with at most four new tokens, and
the verdict is the first letter of the reply, anything else a tie. A win scores $1$, a tie
$\tfrac12$, a loss $0$, and an order-averaged score shows the item in both orders and averages the
two (\texttt{analysis/\allowbreak utility.py}, \texttt{analysis/\allowbreak order\_\allowbreak averaged\_\allowbreak h2h.py}).

\paragraph{A recording defect in the judged text, and its repair.} The harness recorded each
generation by slicing a left-padded batch row at that row's own prompt length rather than at the
padded length, so a row with $p$ pad tokens carried the last $p$ tokens of its prompt into the
recorded generation. On the headline prompts the opponent's and the anchor's texts carry such an
echo on $73.6\%$ of prompts (mean $10.7$ characters), the $k=10$ meter's on $32.6\%$, and all $64$
candidates of a prompt share one. Nothing served was affected --- the decoder never saw the record ---
but the judge and the reward model read it, and an empty draw with an echo looked non-empty. The
slicing is fixed, and every recorded generation is recovered exactly as the decoded row minus its
prompt, on $100\%$ of records read. The head-to-heads of the main text and Table~\ref{tab:served} are
on the repaired text; single-order sweeps judged within one run say where they are shown.
\textbf{Every older order-averaged reading was re-judged on the repaired text} with nothing else
changed --- the five-judge panel, the opponent ladder under judges~B and~C, and six workload passes,
twenty paired differences in all --- and \textbf{all twenty keep their sign class}; the panel still
reads four positive judges of five, Mixtral the exception at $-0.0015$ $[-0.0455, +0.0420]$, and
AlpacaEval's difference moves from $-0.0339$ to $-0.0329$ $[-0.0528, -0.0127]$. Of the forty
single-arm gains beneath them, three change class: selection's on MT-Bench ($+0.0375$ $[-0.0125,
+0.0875]$) and on CoTaEval-QA ($+0.0070$ $[-0.0165, +0.0300]$) now cover zero, and the meter's on
the public books falls below it ($-0.0275$ $[-0.0470, -0.0080]$). Appendix~\ref{app:workload}
quotes the numbers on record, and says where these change what it concludes.

\paragraph{Position dominates, and the correction is asymmetric.}
Figure~\ref{fig:selection}(b) and Table~\ref{tab:served} are in \emph{levels}, which compare within
one order-averaged pass against one opponent: a greedy judge shown both orders is a function of the
two texts alone, so an arm's level does not depend on which other arms are judged, and the risky
model's own second draw reads $0.504$ against its first. Levels are unreliable between passes and
under single-order judging, which is what follows. Re-judging in \emph{both} orders --- $2{,}000$
calls on one arm and $4{,}000$ on the head-to-head, bands fixed in advance --- shows position bias
\citep{zheng2023judging} dominating it: the same two texts win $261$ of $500$ shown second and $24$ shown
first (about a third of items tie either way, which is why the counts do not partition $500$), only
$29.2\%$ of items get a mutually consistent verdict, and the first slot wins $12.7\%$ of \emph{decided}
comparisons. A single-order level is therefore largely a statement about slot order, which is why none is
quoted as one. The correction is \emph{not} symmetric between the two mechanisms, and we
specified in advance the wrong prediction about it: order-averaging the judge-A arm leaves $+0.0405$
$[+0.019, +0.063]$ against the $+0.081$ on record, but it moves selection's head-to-head gain by a factor
of $0.95$ and the metered decoder's by $0.41$, so their difference grows rather than shrinks. The likely
reason is that at $k=10$ the metered decoder is nearly the risky model it is judged against, leaving the
judge little to read but slot order. \textbf{A judged gain also has a floor}: a re-run at a different
$n$-grid moves one by $0.04$ to $0.07$ ($+0.111$ and $+0.072$ at Comma-7B), so a lone estimate of that
size is not evidence. The head-to-head difference escapes it by being paired within one pass --- at
disjoint seeds it reads $+0.0635$ against $+0.0645$, under judge~C $+0.0620$ and $+0.1000$, all three
excluding zero (Appendix~\ref{app:h2hrepeat}). Read as a ratio instead, the two gains of the committed pass are
$2.61\times$ $[1.48, 7.45]$ --- a paired bootstrap over prompts, undefined on $0.12\%$ of resamples,
with Fieller's interval $[1.48, 7.62]$ beside it --- which excludes one but spans a factor of five,
because its denominator's interval comes within $0.014$ of zero. That is why the claim is carried
by the difference and the ratio is quoted only with its interval
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak h2h\_\allowbreak ratio\_\allowbreak interval.csv}).
