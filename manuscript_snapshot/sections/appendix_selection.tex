\section{Selection anchoring: protocol, pre-registration and diagnostics}\label{app:selection}

\paragraph{The strongest anchor at the largest $n$, and the check that stopped it being a headline}
Comma-7B was swept to $n=64$ on the same $500$ ordinary prompts, the same reward, the same two
judges and the same opponent, bands committed first
(\texttt{results/onset\_prediction\_selection\_n64\_comma7b.md}). Within the pass the gain rises
monotonically --- $+0.041$, $+0.060$, $+0.072$, $+0.103$, $+0.127$, $+0.173$ at $n = 2,4,8,16,32,64$
on the registered scorer and $+0.062$ to $+0.230$ on the second, Spearman $+1.000$ in $\log n$ in
both --- so the mechanism is still climbing at $n=64$ at the strongest anchor, for $3.17$ nats of
measured KL against a $\log 64 = 4.16$ pathwise certificate, and the reward overoptimisation that
turns such curves over is not observed anywhere on the grid.

The arm's own nested check, however, \textbf{failed}: its $n=8$ gain of $+0.072$ sits $0.039$ from the
breadth arm's $+0.111$ on the registered scorer, past the $0.03$ the pre-registration allowed, so by
its own committed rule the comparison against the earlier number is \emph{not readable} and the
abstract is unchanged. The band's premise was that the two arms shared generations; they did not,
and the discrepancy is the cross-pass floor of Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} rather than a scoring
fault. Rewriting a band around a premise found wrong after the number is what the register exists to
prevent, so it stands as written.

\paragraph{Candidates}
The $500$ ordinary prompts are the neutral, creative and factual classes of the same workload every
other arm in this paper uses, served raw under the harness's ``Complete the prefix:'' header at
temperature $1$ with a $200$-token cap. Candidates are the $k=0$ arm of the ordinary decoding
harness at $n$ trajectories per prompt, so they are anchor samples produced by the same code path as
every other anchor-only arm, and trajectory seeds are $(h \ll 16) \mid j$ for $j < n$: arm $n$ is
exactly the first $n$ candidates in seed order, the arms nest, and no candidate is scored twice. The
opponent in every judged comparison is the \emph{unconstrained} risky model's completion on the same
prompt at its lowest seed --- one fixed opponent per prompt, so every candidate of that prompt is
judged against the same text.

\paragraph{Why the $n=1$ control is the entry gate}
Selection at $n=1$ is the anchor served alone, which every arm of Section~\ref{sec:orders} already
measures. Under judge~A it reads $u = 0.319$ $[0.281, 0.358]$ against the $u_{\text{safe}} = 0.323$
on record from a different run, a different sample and a different judging pass. A pipeline that
mis-assembles prompts, mis-parses verdicts or mixes up the presentation order would not reproduce
that number, so the control is what licenses reading the refutation at $n=8$ as a refutation rather
than as a bug --- which matters, because the committed arm failed.

\paragraph{How the AUC diagnostic is computed}
The claim that the risky model's likelihood is a poor guide to judged quality is measured
\emph{within} prompt, never across prompts, because across prompts the comparison is confounded by
how hard each prompt is. For each prompt we form all pairs of its eight candidates whose judge~A
verdicts differ, giving $5{,}687$ ordered pairs over the $500$ prompts, and ask how often the
candidate the judge preferred also has the higher score. That fraction is the AUC. Three scores are
evaluated on the identical pair set: the risky model's per-token mean log-likelihood ($0.526$), its
summed log-likelihood ($0.477$) and the completion's length in tokens ($0.537$). A score that
carried no information would read $0.500$; the summed form reads \emph{below} chance because it is a
length preference with the sign the judge disagrees with, which is the same pathology the served-token
column of Table~\ref{tab:selection} shows directly ($98 \to 15$ tokens).

\paragraph{The head-to-head with position removed from both arms, and a protocol defect it found}
\label{app:orderavg}
Every judged number in this paper is produced by a pairwise LLM judge whose order consistency we
measure at $0.24$--$0.35$, so a gain estimated with each pair shown in one random order is unbiased
but noisy, and a \emph{comparison} of two such gains had never been checked. We re-judged the two
arms the paper compares with every item shown in \emph{both} orders --- $4{,}000$ calls, four arms
($n=64$, its $n=1$ control, the metered decoder at $k=10$, its anchor-alone control) against the one
fixed risky opponent, paired over $500$ prompts, bands committed first.

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule

\midrule
selection, $n=64$ & $+0.110$ & $+0.1045$ & $[+0.082, +0.128]$ \\
metered decoder, $k=10$ & $+0.097$ & $+0.0400$ & $[+0.014, +0.0655]$ \\
\midrule
difference, paired & $+0.013$ & $\mathbf{+0.0645}$ & $[+0.030, +0.0995]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent We pre-registered the opposite prediction --- that both arms would deflate together and
the difference would vanish under the cross-pass floor --- and it was wrong. The correction is
\emph{asymmetric}: it moves selection by a factor of $0.95$ and the metered decoder by $0.41$, so
the difference grows five-fold instead of shrinking. We offer a mechanism rather than claim one: at
$k=10$ the metered decoder serves $p_r$ unchanged at every step it takes, so the judge is comparing
two near-identical texts and has little to go on but slot order, while selection's output is
visibly a different text.

The same arm found a defect in the protocol behind the earlier numbers. \texttt{utility.py} and
\texttt{selection\_scaling.py} both show the judge a prompt reconstructed as the served text minus
the generation, which is not the prompt: the split point moves with how much each arm generated, so
for the same item the three arms here disagree about it in \textbf{455 of 500} cases. This run takes
the prompt from the corpus, identical for every arm. The cost of that correction falls on us: under
one true prompt the \emph{single-order} difference is $+0.013$, not the $+0.070$ implied by the
$+0.142$ and $+0.072$ measured under arm-specific prompts. The difference between the two mechanisms
is real, but it is the order-averaged $+0.0645$, and it is visible only once position is removed.
The \emph{comparison} the paper stands behind is therefore the order-averaged one;
Table~\ref{tab:selection}'s per-arm sweep is single-order throughout and says so in its
caption, so the two must not be read off one another.

\paragraph{The cost frontier, and the scorer that is too small to rescue it}\label{app:frontier}
Selection's price is set by the reward model, not the anchor: at $n$ it runs
$n(P_s+P_f)(L_p+T)$ forward-pass FLOPs against the metered decoder's $(P_s+P_r)(L_p+T)$, with
$P_f=7.6$ and $P_s=1.8$. A $0.5$B scorer therefore divides the price by $4.16$ at every $n$, and we
pre-registered that route before running it. It fails. We re-scored the same $32{,}000$ cached
candidates with Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct ($0.494$B measured) on the identical template and judged both
scorers over the whole nested grid --- $8{,}260$ calls, every item in both orders, one true prompt
from the corpus, one fixed opponent.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule

\midrule
$2$  & $0.46\times$ / $1.92\times$ & $+0.0070$ & $+0.0195$ \\
$4$  & $0.92\times$ / $3.83\times$ & $+0.0005$ & $+0.0210$ \\
$8$  & $1.84\times$ / $7.66\times$ & $+0.0290$ & $\mathbf{+0.0415}$ \\
$16$ & $3.68\times$ / $15.32\times$ & $\mathbf{+0.0395}$ & $+0.0655$ \\
$32$ & $7.36\times$ / $30.64\times$ & $+0.0335$ & $+0.0920$ \\
$64$ & $14.73\times$ / $61.29\times$ & $+0.0220$ & $+0.1075$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent Costs are the small and large scorer respectively, relative to the metered decoder, whose
own order-averaged gain in this pass is $+0.0400\,[+0.0140,+0.0660]$. The pass replicates
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}'s head-to-head first --- $+0.1075$ against the $+0.1045$ on record and
$+0.0400$ exactly --- which is the gate we committed to before reading anything else.

Three readings, all pre-registered. The $7$B scorer first matches the metered decoder at $n=8$ and
$7.66\times$, and needs $61.3\times$ to reach $2.7\times$ its gain. The $0.5$B scorer never does:
its best cell, $n=16$ at $3.68\times$, gains $+0.0395\,[+0.0205,+0.0585]$ --- indistinguishable from
the meter and five ten-thousandths below it, where the registered rule asked for above --- and at
matched compute, $n=4$ at $0.92\times$, it loses outright, $-0.0395\,[-0.0720,-0.0065]$. Shrinking
the scorer $14\times$ costs $-0.0855\,[-0.1070,-0.0645]$ of the gain at $n=64$. We predicted three
of these four wrong and in the paper's favour.

We first read the $0.5$B curve as turning over --- it peaks at $n=16$ and ends lower --- and drew
from it that $\log n$ is not a free knob. \textbf{We pre-registered that reading and it did not
survive.} A second arm put two scales between $0.5$B and $7.6$B, registered the terminal drop
$g(64)-g(16)$ as the test, and measured $-0.0160\,[-0.0340,+0.0010]$ for the $0.5$B scorer: negative,
and its interval contains zero by a thousandth. Under the rule committed before that run the $0.5$B
scorer reads \textsc{flat}, so the warning is withdrawn and we do not make it. What the sample does
show, as description rather than as the registered test, is a stark difference in shape: Spearman
between gain and $\log n$ is $0.5429$ for the $0.5$B scorer and exactly $1.0$ --- strictly monotone
at all six grid points --- for each of $1.5$B, $3$B and $7.6$B.

\paragraph{Scorer capability saturates, and it saturates early}\label{app:saturation}
The same arm found something larger than what it was built to test. Judging all four scorers in one
pass ($10{,}722$ calls, $4{,}361$ distinct served completions, both orders, the protocol above
unchanged) reproduces its three reference arms to within $0.001$ --- \texttt{sel7b\_n64} $+0.1065$
against $+0.1075$, \texttt{sel05b\_n64} $+0.0230$ against $+0.0220$, the metered decoder $+0.0400$
against $+0.0400$, against a gate that allowed $\pm0.04$. Across the three adjacent steps at $n=64$,
only the first separates: $1.5$B over $0.5$B is $+0.0700\,[+0.0500,+0.0900]$, $3$B over $1.5$B is
$+0.0040\,[-0.0145,+0.0225]$, and $7.6$B over $3$B is $+0.0095\,[-0.0090,+0.0285]$. A scorer must
clear a bar; past it, a $4.9\times$ larger scorer buys $+0.0135$ and neither step of it separates.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}

scorer & gain at $n=64$ & cost & first cell at or above the meter \\
\midrule
$0.494$B  & $+0.0230$ & $14.73\times$ & none \\
$1.5437$B & $+0.0930$ & $\mathbf{21.59\times}$ & $n=8$, $\mathbf{2.70\times}$ \\
$3.0859$B & $+0.0970$ & $31.67\times$ & $n=8$, $3.96\times$ \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent No band was committed on the cost column, so it is post hoc; the crossing rule is taken
verbatim from the arm above and applied unchanged to scorers that arm did not have. A $1.5$B scorer
reaches $87.3\%$ of the $7.6$B scorer's gain for $35.2\%$ of its serving cost, so on this workload
the $61.3\times$ is the price of the scorer we happened to use and not of the mechanism --- a
reading the next paragraph tests without a judge and does not reproduce.
\textbf{One instability we do not hide}: a crossing \emph{cell} is a thresholded statistic and
inherits none of the stability the gains showed. The $7.6$B scorer's crossing sits at $n=8$ and
$7.66\times$ in one pass and $n=16$ and $15.32\times$ in the other, because \texttt{sel7b\_n8} read
$+0.0415$ and then $+0.0360$ --- a difference of $0.0055$, far inside the judge's floor, that
happens to straddle the meter's $+0.0400$. We therefore quote the $n=64$ column, which moved by
$0.001$ between passes.

Without a judge the reward caches give the same ordering: against the $7.6$B reference the
within-prompt Spearman is $0.1333$, $0.4185$ and $0.4926$ at $0.5$B, $1.5$B and $3$B, and the rate
of serving the same draw at $n=64$ is $0.052$, $0.132$ and $0.228$ against $0.016$ by chance. The
large step is $0.5$B to $1.5$B and the small ones are above it. The certificate is untouched
throughout and was never at risk: $q(y)\le n\,p_s(y)$ holds for any score, so every one of these
$24$ cells is certified at $\log n$ whatever the scorer costs or achieves. What the scorer decides
is not whether the guarantee holds but whether anything is bought with it.

\paragraph{The same question without a judge, where it answers differently}\label{app:judgefreescale}
Every number in the paragraph above comes from a pairwise judge this paper measures at $0.24$--$0.35$
order consistency and calls unusable. GSM8K exact match has no judge: the answer is a number and the
grader is \texttt{==}. We re-scored the same $500 \times 64$ cached Comma-7B candidates with all four
rewards and read accuracy at each $n$, bands committed first. Majority vote is carried alongside
because it needs no reward model at all, and because all four runs must reproduce it exactly --- they
do, which pins the cached-generation path and not merely the arithmetic.

\noindent All arms start from the $n=1$ anchor's $0.320$. Scorer scale matters here too --- $7.6$B
over $0.5$B at $n=64$ is $+0.0700\,[+0.0300,+0.1120]$, paired over the 500 problems --- but
\textbf{it is not bought where the judge said it was}. The first step, $1.5$B over $0.5$B, is
$-0.0060\,[-0.0500,+0.0360]$ against the judged $+0.0700\,[+0.0500,+0.0900]$, and none of the three
adjacent steps resolves on its own ($3$B over $1.5$B is $+0.0460\,[+0.0000,+0.0920]$, a boundary and
not a finding; $7.6$B over $3$B is $+0.0300\,[-0.0140,+0.0740]$). The two axes agree that scorer
scale is worth seven to eight points end to end --- $+0.0835$ judged, $+0.0700$ here --- and
disagree completely about where in the range it is bought: all of it in the first step with a judge,
gradually and with the largest scorer still the best without one.

On TriviaQA, committed in advance as a secondary so it could not become a rescue, there is no scorer
effect at any scale: $7.6$B over $0.5$B is $+0.0040\,[-0.0300,+0.0380]$ and every reward arm
\emph{declines} in $n$ while majority vote rises to $0.334$. We predicted agreement on both tasks
and got it on neither.

Two things survive this and are stronger for it. The paper's claim that utility is the scorer's and
the certificate is the mechanism's is now measured \emph{without} an instrument: $+0.0700$ of exact
match turns on the scorer alone while $\log n$ is identical throughout. And majority vote --- no
reward model, same certificate --- reaches $0.546$ where the best reward reaches $0.386$, so where
an answer is checkable the scorer-free rule is the better one, now at four scorer scales rather than
one.

\paragraph{The cheapest instance is also the best one, where an answer is checkable}\label{app:scorerfree}
Everything above prices selection with a reward model in the loop, and that model is what it costs:
at $n$ the mechanism runs $n(P_s+P_f)(L_p+T)$ against the metered decoder's $(P_s+P_r)(L_p+T)$, with
$P_f=7.6$ against $P_s=1.8$. \textbf{Majority vote has no such term.} Self-consistency serves the
modal answer over the same $n$ anchor draws, so its score is a regex and costs no forward pass:
$n\,P_s(L_p+T)$. It carries the identical $\log n$ certificate, because Proposition~\ref{prop:selection}
assumes nothing whatever about the score and a mode is a score. Joining the cost model to the
accuracies of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} and the paragraph above, sorted by price:

\noindent Every scorer-free cell beats every reward cell on both tasks. Majority vote at $n=8$ costs
$1.44\times$ and reaches $0.466$; the best reward cell costs $61.29\times$ and reaches $0.386$. At
$n=32$ the scorer-free rule gains $3.42\times$ as much for $9.4\%$ of the cost, and on TriviaQA the
reward goes \emph{negative} at every $n \ge 8$ while majority vote rises to $0.334$. The certificate
is the same down the column --- $\log n$, $3.466$ nats at $n=32$, against $2000$ certified for the
metered decoder's median response.

\textbf{Three limits belong with these numbers.} They are \emph{post hoc}: no band was committed,
because there is no new run, only a join (\texttt{results/scorer\_free\_cost\_note.md}). Majority
vote needs a \emph{canonical answer}, so this does not transfer to the free-form judged workload
where every other comparison in this paper lives. And no metered decoder was run on GSM8K, so the
cost column is our standard denominator and not a measured head-to-head on that task; the accuracy
columns compare each rule against the same anchor at $n=1$.

Two things this does not say. Majority vote does not reach the unconstrained risky model, which
scores $0.786$ greedy here against its $0.546$ --- Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} already states that
an anchor which cannot do the task gains nothing at any $n$, and this is a comparison among
mechanisms that carry a certificate, not a claim to have beaten the model one exists to bound. And
it does not make the reward model pointless: the judged workload has no mode, and every judged
result here is a reward-scored arm. What changes is the compute story. The $61.3\times$ is the price
of putting a reward model in the loop, not the price of the mechanism, and where an answer is
checkable a deployer should not pay it.

\paragraph{Vetting the anchor: the premise is measurable, and we measured it}\label{app:vetting}
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} bounds $\Pr_q[E]$ by $n\Pr_{p_s}[E]$, so everything rests on the
anchor's own $\Pr_{p_s}[E]$ being small --- and open-licensed corpora carry only partial provenance
guarantees, so a deployer should not take that on faith. They need not. $\Pr_{p_s}[E]$ is the one
quantity in the whole construction that can be measured with no attack, no risky model and no
access to the mechanism: draw from the candidate anchor on the protected passages' own prefixes and
score near-verbatim recall. It is exactly the number the certificate multiplies.

We ran that measurement on every anchor in this paper and on every model here known to have seen
the work (\texttt{results/anchor\_vetting.csv}).

\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}

provenance & passages & fraction leaking & max recall \\
\midrule

\midrule

memorised in pre-training (\texttt{Llama-3.1-70B}) & $50$ & $0.500$ & $1.0000$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Every openly licensed anchor reads exactly zero on every one of $100$ passages; every
model known to hold the work leaks on at least half of them. The separation is complete over
eighteen models, and the threshold is not delicate --- the gap runs from $0.000$ to $0.500$ with
nothing in between.

Three honesty notes. First, this is a \emph{post hoc} re-analysis of arms run for other purposes;
no band was committed in advance, because there is no new run to commit one against, and it should
not be read as one of this paper's pre-registered tests. Second, the twelve fine-tuned rows are
close to circular --- those models were trained on exactly these passages, so their failing is
nearly a tautology. The two facts that are \emph{not} circular are that the five anchors the paper
actually uses read zero, and that the one model which memorised without our help
(\texttt{Llama-3.1-70B}, which reproduces a passage in full) fails the same test. Third, the test is
sound but not complete: near-verbatim recall at a given prefix length is a lower bound on
contamination, so passing it is evidence and not proof, and like a blocklist it speaks only about
the works handed to it. What it does establish is that the premise is a measurement rather than an
assumption, and that it costs a deployer $n$ forward passes to take.

\paragraph{The composition arithmetic, and the R\'enyi order it is done in}
A per-user odometer at $B_{\text{user}} = 400$ nats --- half the anchor's median surprisal of a
protected passage, $849$ nats --- is the accounting an earlier audit of this mechanism uses to bound
what one user can accumulate across queries \citep{vijayavallabh2026audit}, and it is where a budget
that is not spent per token pays off twice. The count depends on which order the per-query charge is
read at, and a paper that advertises a pathwise certificate must not quote a KL composition, so both
are given here and the pathwise one is what Section~\ref{sec:selection} states.

\noindent Three things follow. First, max-divergence adds \emph{exactly} under adaptive composition,
so $192$ needs no advanced composition theorem and no assumption about how a user chooses their
queries; this is the property \citet{cohen2025blameless} shows per-query access-freeness lacks in
general, and it is why the pathwise order is worth its extra $0.875$ nats per query. Second, the
$332$ is what the deployed odometer --- which meters KL spend, \texttt{analysis/odometer.py} --- would
actually permit, and it is reported so that the two are never conflated. Third, the metered
decoder's row is given twice for the same reason: at its \emph{measured} spend a user gets $2$
queries, and at the budget the deployer publishes a single $200$-token response is already certified
at $600$ nats, so the odometer admits none. Selection's charge does not depend on the response
length at either order, which is the composition consequence of the asymmetry
Section~\ref{sec:selection} states for the certificate.

\paragraph{The pre-registration record}
Bands, grid, entry gate, primary metric and the excluded alternatives were committed to the
repository before anything was generated, in \texttt{results/onset\_prediction\_selection.md} and
\texttt{results/onset\_prediction\_selection\_scaling.md}. The primary arm's band was: at $n=8$,
judged utility of at least $0.469$ means the mechanism works, $0.396$ to $0.469$ partial, below
$0.396$ refuted. It scored $0.313$ --- refuted, and by a margin that no reading of the band rescues.
The extraction band was near-verbatim recall at most $0.005$ at $n=64$; it scored $0.0000$. The
cross-judge follow-up was committed separately, after the primary arm had been scored and before the
second judge was run, with bands of at least $+0.244$ for a real capacity, $+0.10$ to $+0.244$ for a
partial one and below $+0.10$ for an artefact of the selecting judge; it scored $+0.081$, so the
recorded verdict is \textsc{artefact} --- the oracle's $+0.488$ was largely circular --- while the
surviving $+0.081$ has a paired interval excluding zero and is reported as such. Four alternatives
were excluded in advance and none was adopted afterwards: scoring the oracle against a band,
re-fitting a band after seeing a result, quoting the best $n$ rather than the committed one, and
changing the scoring template between arms.

\paragraph{What this does and does not establish}
It does not recommend any particular scorer. A judge is not a deployment's utility, $+0.081$ is a
small effect measured once, and the mechanism runs a second model $n$ times per response. What it
establishes is narrower and is the thing Theorem~\ref{thm:nfl}'s gap alone could not: the
impossibility --- that no budget certifies every protected work and also buys a detectable
improvement --- is a property of \emph{metering per token}, not of budgeted decoding. At $k=10$ the
metered decoder's $2{,}000$ nats cover none of the works; $\log 8 = 2.08$ nats cover all of them
with four hundred times the margin to spare, and reach the same judged utility.

\paragraph{The judge-free axis: protocol and the full grids}\label{app:judgefree}
Every other utility number in this paper is a pairwise judged preference and the judge is dominated
by position (Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}). Exact match is not, so two arms measure the
constructive claim with no judge in the loop. Both run at Comma-7B --- the anchor carrying the
largest judged gain --- on the same $n$ grid as the judged experiments, at temperature $0.7$, and
both score the \emph{served} string, which Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} bounds only because it
is one of the $n$ draws: the tie rule returns an index into the sample, never a synthesised answer.

\textbf{Reasoning.} GSM8K's first $500$ test problems, $8$-shot with the first eight training
problems as a fixed prompt, $256$-token cap, $64$ samples per problem drawn once and read nested, so
$n \in \{1,\dots,64\}$ are prefixes of one draw and no sample is scored twice. Scoring is the
benchmark's own: the final integer after \texttt{\#\#\#\#}, cut at the first run-on into the next
few-shot question, because a base model continues past its answer and grading the last marker in
the buffer grades a different problem.

\textbf{Knowledge.} TriviaQA \texttt{rc.nocontext}, the first $500$ validation questions, $5$-shot,
$24$-token cap, $64$ samples. Scoring is \emph{containment}: a gold alias appears as a whole-word
span in the normalised answer line. Exact match here would score format rather than knowledge --- a
base model completing a few-shot prompt emits \texttt{David Seville} and an instruction-tuned one
emits \texttt{The answer is David Seville}, and a smoke run scored the latter $0/8$ on questions it
had right --- so the rule was fixed in the pre-registration before any number, and the $24$-token
cap bounds what a verbose completion can sweep up by accident. It applies identically to the anchor
draws, the selected outputs and both baselines.

Each arm reads no band unless a number was extracted from $90\%$ of its $500 \times 64$ samples:
GSM8K yields one on $99.96\%$ and TriviaQA on $98.67\%$. Both arms carry the mandatory baselines on
the same questions and seeds --- $k=0$ is the $n=1$ row and $k=-1$ is
\texttt{Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct} alone, $0.786$ greedy and $0.726$ sampled on GSM8K, $0.722$ and
$0.674$ on TriviaQA. Those are descriptive: the risky model is instruction-tuned and the anchor is
not, and the comparison this paper makes is between two ways of spending a budget over one anchor.
On GSM8K both rules are monotone in $\log n$ (Spearman $+0.955$ and $+0.929$); on TriviaQA only one
is, which is the whole of what separates the two tasks.

\textbf{What separates the two tasks is the support, and it was predicted.} The TriviaQA
pre-registration committed, before the run, that majority vote would lift by \emph{less} than on
GSM8K, and gave the reason: selection re-ranks draws, and a closed-book question offers less spread
between them than a multi-step derivation, where one arithmetic slip decides the answer. The
measured ratio is $4.2\times$. Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} is why the direction is not a
surprise --- $q(y) \le n\,p_s(y)$ means the served string is one the anchor would have drawn, so no
$n$ supplies a fact the anchor does not have --- but the size of the gap is a measurement and the
prediction could have been refuted by it.

\textbf{Self-consistency is the mechanism, not an analogy.} Nothing in
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} mentions a reward: majority vote returns one of the $n$ draws, so
it satisfies the same $q(y) \le n\,p_s(y)$ under the same tie rule. The most widely deployed
inference-time method in the literature therefore already carries a $\log n$ pathwise certificate,
which nobody has had to add to it. What these two grids show is that the certificate costs nothing
it was not already paying: the accuracy is what self-consistency delivers anyway, and the
$4.159$~nats at $n=64$ are the whole charge.

\paragraph{The judge-free head-to-head, where the metered decoder wins}\label{app:h2h}
The comparison in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} is judged. It can be made with no judge at all only
at the one anchor the factory runs at --- TinyComma-1.8B, the only openly licensed safe model
shipping the risky model's tokenizer --- and only on a task it scores above the floor, which
TriviaQA is ($0.07$ few-shot) and GSM8K is not ($0.04$). We ran it, and we
committed the prediction that we would lose it before generating anything
(\texttt{results/onset\_prediction\_verifiable\_headtohead.md}). Both mechanisms decode the same
$500$ questions at $T_{\max}=24$ with the same risky model.

\textbf{The metered decoder wins the level by $0.428$, and the way it wins is the paper's claim.}
Its winning arm is $k=20$, where its accuracy is the unconstrained risky model's $0.618$ to three

judge in it.

\textbf{The frontier survives the loss.} At every accuracy selection buys over the shared control,
it buys it for less than a tenth of what the cheapest metered arm reaching that accuracy spends:

protected text. Every cap above is $k\,T_{\max}$ at $T_{\max}=24$, not the $200$ of the copyright
workload.

\paragraph{A memoriser we did not make, and the three arms it took to build one}\label{app:natural}
Every zero above is measured against a model \emph{we} fine-tuned, which is a fair thing for a
reviewer to discount: it could be a property of our LoRA recipe rather than of the mechanism. The
answer needs an adversary that memorised the work in pre-training.
\texttt{Llama-3.1-70B} is one --- \citet{cooper2025extracting} report $96.3\%$ extraction coverage
for it on \emph{Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone} --- and getting it to behave like one took
four attempts, three of which failed for reasons of our own making. We report all four because each
failure mode is a trap in the measurement rather than in the mechanism, and two of them produced a
clean $0.0000$ that would have looked like a result.

failure here would end the line of work and be reported as a limit on the evidence rather than a
property of the mechanism.

\paragraph{What the certificate delivers when its premise fails}\label{app:contaminated}
Every leakage result above has a clean anchor, and Proposition~\ref{prop:selection} bounds an
\emph{event}: $\Pr_q[E] \le n \Pr_{p_s}[E]$. With $\Pr_{p_s}[E] = 0$ that inequality is satisfied by
any number we could have measured, so across six anchors and a naturally memorising $70$B
adversary it has been reported as confirmed and never actually tested. It is also the mechanism's
one failure mode: the certificate is relative to the safe model's own probability, and says nothing
if the safe model has seen the work.

We therefore ran selection anchoring, with the same adversarial selector, over \textbf{twelve
deliberately memorised anchors} --- LoRA copies fine-tuned on the protected passages, spanning
GPT-NeoX, Llama, Mixtral, Phi and Qwen at $170$M to $7$B --- on the same $100$ passages and seeds.
All twelve reproduce the memorising model's own control at $0.3925$ mean recall, maximum $0.8154$,
$78\%$ of passages above threshold, which is twelve independent checks that only the anchor changed.

leaks, not a floor under it, and the actionable consequence is that the anchor is the thing to vet.
The correlation runs the way we predicted before the run: amplification \emph{falls} as
contamination rises ($\rho = -0.700$), because an anchor that emits the passage on most draws
leaves a selector nothing to find.

Three limits, all committed in advance and none worked around. First, the bound test \textbf{fails at the letter of its own band}: Phi-3.5-mini reads $0.010$ against a bound of $n \times 0.000 = 0$,
exceeding it by $5\times10^{-5}$ on a \emph{single} passage. Where the base rate is exactly zero the
empirical bound collapses and any non-zero rate violates it at any $n$; the proposition bounds true
probabilities and a true rate of a few thousandths is consistent with $0/100$ and $1/100$. We report
the band as violated rather than re-specifying it, and note that at the five anchors where the test
has power it holds with a wide margin. Second, \textbf{seven of twelve anchors sit at exactly
zero}, which is the vacuity this arm was built to escape reappearing at the high threshold: a LoRA
memoriser tends to reproduce a passage always or never. Third, the amplification numbers are counts
over $100$ passages --- $A(64) = 4.00$ is eight passages over two, and one more would have read
$4.5$. The magnitude is what to quote, not the boundary.
