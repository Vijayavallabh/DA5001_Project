A Divergence Budget Is Uninformative Without the Work
It Protects

Vacuity, Extraction and Utility in Metered Decoding


Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review


Abstract

Inference-time mechanisms for copyright protection meter how far a lan-
guage model’s output may drift from a safe model that never saw the pro-
tected work, and publish the allowance as a single number k. We show
that k carries no risk information on its own, and identify what does. The
bound such a budget implies on reproducing a work x becomes vacuous at

K = S(x), the safe model’s surprisal of x, and this threshold is identical
for every Rényi order of the charge, so no strengthening of the accounting

moves it. A second result prices the other thing a budget buys: Donsker–

Varadhan at the sequence level bounds the budget needed for mean utility
by the Cramér rate function of that utility under the safe model. Measuring
both sides, the decoder spends 79 to 171 nats of realised divergence for a
utility gain worth hundredths of a nat — three to four orders of magnitude
of overhead, so what pushes a budget past the vacuity threshold is the price
of imitating the risky model, not the price of being better than the safe one.
The useful region opens only where the certified one has closed: under two
independent judges and 600 comparisons per budget, neither separates the
decoder from its own safe model at k = 0.6, barely past the budget at which

the first protected work loses its certificate, and both separate it only at
k ≥ 10, where none of them is covered. The two boundaries are ordered

rather than coincident: on the sharper judge the decoder becomes useful at

k = 0.66, after the certificate has begun to fail and before most of it has.
Finally, extraction begins near that threshold: on seven model pairs shar-
ing no anchor, tokenizer or risky model, leakage first becomes measurable
between 0.88 and 1.17 of s(x), and between 0.878 and 0.926 among the five
whose adversary holds the same amount of the work — a quantity extrac-
tion evaluations specify in tokens, and therefore report differently under
every tokenizer. Halving it moves a pair across its own vacuity threshold,
and warping the anchor to move s(x) within one pair moves the onset with
it, at an elasticity of +0.72 and +0.61: not what a budget in a fixed num-

ber of nats would give. Across ten safe models and three openly licensed
corpora, the separation between the rate that must be permitted and the

rate that must be forbidden widens as safe models improve.


1 Introduction

A growing family of inference-time mechanisms promises to bound how much a language
model can reproduce a protected work by metering how far its output distribution may drift
from a safe model that never saw the work. The drift is charged in nats, accumulated in
a token bucket, and published as a single number k. Anchored decoding (He et al., 2026)

is the current instance; the guarantee it enforces, k-near-access-freeness, goes back to Vyas
et al. (2023). The appeal is that k looks like ε in differential privacy: a dial a deployer sets,

a number a rights-holder can read.

This paper argues that k does not carry that meaning, and — more usefully — says exactly
what does. Three results, in increasing order of what they cost to establish.

The budget has a unit, and it is not nats. The bound a budget K implies on reproducing
a work x is vacuous once K reaches S(x), the safe model’s total surprisal of x, and the
threshold is the same for every Rényi order of the charge (Proposition 1). Raising the order
buys a tighter bound strictly below the threshold and does not move it, so the failure cannot
be repaired by strengthening the accounting. The quantity that carries risk is therefore
k/s(x), not k. Vyas et al. (2023) already report their budget as a fraction of the safe
model’s entropy; what is new is an exact threshold at the realised surprisal of a specific
work, holding for every order.
Almost none of what a budget buys is utility. A budget is one number, so whatever the
decoder buys with it comes from one account. Donsker–Varadhan at the sequence level gives
K ≥ Λ∗s (E[U ]), the Cramér rate function of the utility under the safe model (Theorem 1).
Both sides are measurable, and measuring them is what makes the result useful: the decoder
spends 79 to 171 nats of realised divergence to buy a utility gain an optimal policy could
buy for hundredths of a nat, a ratio above 103 at every budget. The cost of a metered
decoder is therefore approximation overhead rather than information-theoretic necessity —

what drives a budget past s(x) is the price of imitating the risky model everywhere, not
the price of being better. Empirically the useful region opens only where the certified one

has closed. Two judges outside the audit, 600 comparisons per budget, agree at both ends

and nowhere else: at k = 0.5, the largest budget covering every protected passage, neither
distinguishes the decoder from its own safe model (−1.03σ, +1.52σ); only at k ≥ 10, where
the certificate covers none, do both (−6.97σ, −3.29σ). Between those they disagree about
the crossing by nearly an order of magnitude in k, which is itself a result.

Extraction begins near that threshold, and s(x) says where. On seven pairs sharing no

anchor, tokenizer or risky model, leakage first becomes measurable between 0.88 and 1.17 of

s(x); among the five whose adversary is handed the same amount of the work it is 0.878 to
0.926 over nearly the whole range of s(x). What moves it is not the memoriser, the target’s
length or the vocabulary but that context, which extraction evaluations specify in tokens, so
a finer tokenizer hands the adversary half the words: pre-registered interventions changing
only that number move a pair across its own vacuity threshold in both directions. Warping
the anchor to move s(x) itself within a pair gives an elasticity of +0.72 and +0.61, refuting a
budget quoted in a fixed number of nats. The geometry’s own candidate, r(x) = ss (x)−sr (x)
(Eq. (4)), committed for five pairs before they were swept, is refuted with it.
Read together these say something a deployer can act on. The three rates that matter — the
surprisal rate s(x), the requirement r(x) a decoder must afford before it can pay for the work
at all, and the rate cuse at which ordinary traﬀic diverges — are properties of the models and
the content, computable before any mechanism is built. Measured across ten safe models
and three independently curated, openly licensed corpora, the separation between the rate
that must be permitted and the rate that must be forbidden widens as safe models improve
(Section 3), correcting a claim in an earlier audit of this mechanism (Vijayavallabh, 2026)

that read one side of that two-sided quantity and inverted the trend.
None of this contradicts the mechanisms’ own theorems: across more than 100,000 budgeted
trajectories here and in the audits this builds on, no per-trajectory violation occurs. The
gap is between what the theorems bound and what the published number is taken to mean.

Proposition 1 (The vacuity threshold does not depend on the order). Let a decoder of the

class above run to a sequence budget K under the charge cα , and let E be the event that it

emits x. For every α ∈ [1, ∞] the tightest bound on Pr[E] implied by the budget equals 1
if and only if K ≥ S(x).

The proof (Appendix A) is three standard conversions — the α = ∞ change of measure,
the Rényi-to-probability bound underwriting reconstruction robustness in Rényi differential
privacy (Mironov, 2017), and the binary-KL inversion. None is new; the contribution is
that all three break in the same place, which is why the failure cannot be repaired by

strengthening the accounting. Raising α buys a strictly tighter bound below the threshold
— at S = 200, K = 100 the cap falls from 0.50 at α = 1 to e−100 at α = ∞ — and moves

the threshold not at all. One remedy remains inside this class, a smaller k, and §5 prices it.


Proposition 2 (Protection outruns certification). Under the banking rule, if the decoder
emits each token of x with probability at least 1 − η then k ≥ (1 − η) kcrit (x) − log 2.

Moreover kcrit (x) ≥ s(x) + δ/N for every work, with equality if and only if the surprisal

accumulates at a constant rate.

The proof is in Appendix A; it needs only that the charge dominates the KL, which every
order α ≥ 1 does, so the boundary belongs to the family and not to one accounting rule.
Neither is kcrit our invention: it is the Loynes running maximum representing the workload of
a queue (Loynes, 1962), equivalently the smallest rate of a (σ, ρ) arrival curve admitting the
surprisal process (Cruz, 1991; Le Boudec & Thiran, 2001). Naming it makes the diagnosis

concrete — a budget publishes a drift where safety is a workload maximum.
Together the two propositions bracket a regime. Below s(x) the certificate says some-
thing and the decoder is heavily constrained; above kcrit (x) the work is reproducible; on
[s(x), kcrit (x)) the decoder still protects the work and no budget in the family can say so,
the certificate reporting the drift of the surprisal process where safety is governed by its
running maximum. That is the distinction a token bucket makes between a mean rate and

a burst, and it is why a scalar budget is the wrong primitive rather than merely a loose one.


How wide the uncertified regime is, and who owns it Measured on 758 reference passages
under four safe models from three openly licensed corpora, kcrit /s has a median of 4.25

to 6.10, so the uncertified interval is wide — and almost entirely an artifact of where a

generation starts. The running maximum is attained at the very first token in 87.7% to
90.5% of cases, because the bucket must pay for the opening out of one step’s allowance,
and an adversary supplying a single genuine prefix token collapses the ratio to 1.32–1.48,
where it stays for 5, 10 or 20 tokens (Appendix B). The protection is real, and it belongs to
the prompt rather than to the budget.

The bound is satisfied by four orders of magnitude, and that is the finding It would be

convenient to read Eq. (3) as forcing a deployer to spend past the vacuity threshold to get
anything useful. It does not, and we checked rather than assumed. For a bounded utility

1/ 21 /0 and its law under ps read off the anchor-only arm, the decoder spends between 79

to 0.061. The ratio runs from 2800 at k = 20 to 2.8 × 105 at k = 0.5 (Appendix A), and
bootstrapping over judged pairs at the weakest end of each interval still leaves it between
1.3 × 103 and 8.6 × 103 . So the mechanism’s cost is not information-theoretic necessity
but approximation overhead, three to four orders of magnitude of it. Only one reading of
that gap is safe. Eq. (3) is attained by some distribution, and nothing says the attaining

one is a model anybody would serve — a tilt that games the judge would also achieve the
mean cheaply. The gap licenses the negative claim, that the budget a metered decoder must

publish is not set by how much better its output is; it does not license the constructive claim

that a cheap decoder of the same quality exists.

The obvious objection is that a three-valued judge is too blunt a utility to price. It runs the
wrong way: bluntness caps the rate function, since Λ∗s (umax ) = − log Prps [U = umax ] for

any bounded U . Winning every judged comparison, far above anything the decoder reaches,
would cost an optimal policy 1.19 nats, and the decoder spends 144 times that. A utility

is expensive in this sense only when its best outcome is exponentially unlikely under the

safe model, which is exactly what the atom {output = x} is. Extraction and utility differ
because one is a rare event and the other a bounded average.

What pushes the budget past s(x) is therefore not the price of utility but the price of being

the risky model, and a scalar budget cannot separate the two. Closing that gap is the
paper’s open problem; one route is to stop decoding greedily against the bucket, as Tomasi

et al. (2026) do for constrained discrete diffusion. Until then the measured crossover of §5

is the operative number, Eq. (3) a floor far beneath it, and the same overhead is visible per
step (§3).



The separation widens as the safe model improves

Proposition 1 makes the certificate’s fate turn on one comparison: the budget against the

safe model’s surprisal of the protected work. Whether a deployer can pick a budget that is

both informative and generous therefore depends on how s(x) compares with cuse , the rate
ordinary traﬀic diverges from the same safe model. Both are properties of the models and
the content, settled before any mechanism is designed. Neither has been measured across
safe models, and the question has an obvious practical edge: safe models are getting better,
and it is not obvious whether that helps.

The mechanism is unsurprising once stated: a better safe model closes the gap to the risky
model faster on generic instruction-following output than on a particular novel, because the

novel is idiosyncratic and the assistant register is not.

This corrects the earlier audit (Vijayavallabh, 2026), which observed that a 7B Common
Pile anchor assigns the protected passages fewer nats than a 1.8B one and concluded that
scaling the anchor weakens the guarantee. The observation is right, 0.778 → 0.685 nats

per character; the conclusion does not follow, because cuse falls further over the same span,
.191 → 0.137. Of the ten safe models scored here the 7B anchor has the largest margin,

not the smallest. Both readings are true and belong together: in absolute terms the range

of budgets over which the certificate says anything shrinks, so a deployer who fixed k in
nats finds it going vacuous under a better anchor; in relative terms the two rates separate.
Only the second reading is available to a deployer who does not know the protected works
in advance. The trend also has a terminus, since it is driven by cuse falling: in the limit the
decoder becomes the identity on ordinary prompts and the mechanism’s value vanishes in
exactly the regime that makes its guarantee comfortable.

by being bad at prose, and every safe model here is a base model while the risky model is

What the margin does not license The margin says how far apart the two rates are, not

that a budget between them leaves utility intact: the rate a decoder actually pays is not
cuse but the 5-to-32-times smaller rate of §2, measured across six prompt classes and two
budgets. Cheap-step selection does not account for that gap, since the decoder pays more
per unaltered step than the cheapest matching fraction of the unconstrained distribution
would. It walks a different trajectory from the one the risky model would have walked, and
its price is a property of that trajectory — the approximation gap Theorem 1 leaves open.



Where extraction begins, and why there


Proposition 1 locates the budget at which the certificate stops saying anything, for every
order. It does not say leakage begins there; this section asks where it does.

Conditioning on the adversary’s context is what makes s(x) the unit The claim that a
budget should be quoted in units of s(x) has a null a referee will reach for: quote a constant
number of nats and do not rescale. Over all seven pairs it nearly holds. Predicting a held-out
pair’s onset as c s(x), with c fitted on the other six, gives a mean absolute error of 0.264 nats
per token against the constant’s 0.295: across a 1.61× range of s(x), rescaling buys 12%.
Among the five pairs whose adversary is handed 13.0–14.4 words of the work — an s(x)
range of 1.49×, nearly the whole of it — the same leave-one-out gives 0.070 nats against
0.364, and the ratio’s coeﬀicient of variation is 2.4%; both exact multiplicity checks on that
subgroup give p = 0.048 (Appendix E). Neither tests the grouping rule, which was read off

these same measurements. The interventions below do.

The split is what the evaluation hands the adversary The attack seeds a fixed number of
tokens, so the words that buys are the tokenizer’s business: 7.3 on the KL3M pairs against

.0–14.4 on the other five, an exact split with no overlap, and ranking the seven by it
gives a Spearman correlation with the onset ratio of −0.919 (p = 0.007, exact over all 7!

orderings). A twenty-token seed is exactly twenty times the characters per token, so that

ranking is confounded with granularity and only an intervention separates them. Five were
run, each pre-registered with its refuting band, each moving –seed-tokens alone. Giving
Pleias-1.2B the KL3M seed — 6.7 words instead of 13.7 — moves it from 0.866 [0.72, 0.95]
to 1.004 [0.99, 1.23], intervals that do not overlap; giving KL3M-520M the others’ moves
it back, 1.032 to 0.939, as does the same swap on KL3M-1.7B, 1.155 to 0.979. Neither
direction reaches the other family’s band, so the context is not the whole of the difference,
and sweeping the seed across a factor of seven gives a curve that falls and then turns (1.163,
1.032, 0.939, 1.056 at 4.0, 7.3, 14.3 and 28.4 words). We claim the effect over the range an
evaluation varies the seed and not beyond it (Appendix E).

It acts on the budget, not on the memoriser In four of the five arms the unconstrained
model reproduces its target exactly as well as before — k = −1 recall 0.520, 0.546, 0.894

and 0.386 against controls of 0.519, 0.519, 0.909 and 0.411 — and the mean rate s(x) moves
by under 1.6%, while kcrit , the rate the target’s own surprisal profile demands of the bucket

(Proposition 2, computed from the anchor alone), moves by 7 to 20% and the onset follows

it. The fifth halves the KL3M seed to four words and halves the memoriser’s unconstrained
recall with it, to 0.227; it is reported with that baseline attached and excluded from any
fit, as pre-registered. Calibrated on each pair’s control arm and nothing else, onset / kcrit
predicts every other arm to within 5.1% against a no-change null’s 11.0%. A longer prefix
does not make the memoriser better at the work; it starts the target at a cheaper point on
its own profile. Nor is this a quirk of our harness: extractability is defined “with k tokens
of context” (Carlini et al., 2023) and rises with the context supplied, so two measurements
under different tokenizers at one token budget differ in a quantity that drives what they
measure.

Move s(x) itself and the onset follows, by less than one for one The interventions above
hold the anchor’s rate fixed. Warping its logits to τ = 0.4 moves it instead, by 67% and 62%

on two pairs, with seed, corpus, tokenizer, memoriser and grid fixed. A budget in a constant
number of nats predicts an onset that does not move; a budget in units of s(x) predicts

one that moves with it. The elasticity d log(onset)/d log s(x) is +0.72 [+0.41, +0.84] and

+0.61 [+0.40, +0.96]: constant nats refuted twice, and proportionality not reached either.
Because the decoder warps both logit vectors before the solve, the warped memoriser is also
the sharper one, which lowers its onset and biases the elasticity toward the null; restricting
both arms to the passages the memoriser reproduces in both closes that gap (k = −1
recall 0.947 against 0.993, 0.996 against 0.999) and leaves the elasticity at +0.72 and +0.61,
unchanged to two decimals. Here kcrit fails, 21.9% off against the same null’s 12.3%: it
tracks which token of a fixed profile the adversary must generate first, not a rescaling of the
profile, and we report it as the first only.

The published budget determines neither the protection nor the price

Proposition 1 says every order of the charge becomes vacuous at the same budget, so a

deployer who publishes k publishes the same certificate whichever order the decoder uses.
We built the family — α → 1 is the audited decoder, α → ∞ the pathwise one, and Eq. (1)
is closed form on the geodesic, so one bisection serves all of them — and ran the same attack
and workload through four members at the budget the mechanism’s authors use. Figure 2
in Appendix A shows what the certificate says across the family; Table 1 shows what the
decoders do.

Table 1 is the point of this paper in one row group. Holding the published budget fixed,
and the certificate with it — vacuous throughout — the four decoders span two orders of
magnitude in what a compliant adversary recovers, 0.097 of a passage down to 0.001, and
more than two in what they cost, from touching 0.4% of ordinary decode steps to 90.1%.
A number that determines neither the protection nor the price is not a summary of the
mechanism but of one accounting convention inside it.

The family is cheap at one end and not in the middle. From α = 1 to α = 2, oracle-window
recall nearly halves, 0.097 to 0.054, for intervention rising from 0.4% to 2.4% of steps and
no loss of diversity — distinct-3 moves from 0.9910 to 0.9924, the wrong way for a cost.
One more step does not continue the trade: at α = 4 oracle recall falls to 0.004, but the
decoder serves the risky model unchanged at 8.7% of steps instead of 91.4%. The order is
a sharp knob, not a dial, and a deployer choosing it on the published budget cannot tell
which system they have bought.

Related work

Near-access-freeness (Vyas et al., 2023) asks that a served model stay within a divergence of
a safe model that never saw the protected work, and CP-∆/CP-k instantiate it by combining

models trained on disjoint shards (Vyas et al., 2023; Abad et al., 2025). Three results bear
directly on ours: Cohen (2025) shows per-query access-freeness does not compose, Cohen

& Zhang (2026) proves barriers for counterfactual credit attribution, and — closest of all

— Segal et al. (2026) report that CP-style guarantees can be numerically uninformative
in realistic fine-tuning settings and demonstrate extraction against NAF-protected models.
We are downstream of that observation, not upstream of it. What differs is the shape of
the claim: they show a particular bound is loose and propose a tighter mechanism, so the
natural reading is that the accounting can be repaired. Proposition 1 says it cannot, for
every order from KL to max-divergence at once, leaving a smaller budget as the only lever
— which Theorem 1 then prices.
Our proof shape is the one used for inference-time impossibility results generally: strong wa-
termarking under oracle assumptions (Zhang et al., 2024), and detector accuracy bounded by
a total variation that goes vacuous as the distributions converge (Sadasivan et al., 2025). The
accounting machinery is the Rényi filter (Feldman & Zrnic, 2020) and adaptive composition
(Whitehouse et al., 2023; Rogers et al., 2016); the token bucket and its running maximum
are standard in queueing and network calculus (Loynes, 1962; Cruz, 1991; Le Boudec & Thi-
ran, 2001), which is where our second boundary comes from. Attacks follow the extraction
literature (Carlini et al., 2021; Nasr et al., 2025; Cooper et al., 2025), and methodologically

we follow Aerni et al. (2024): average-case evaluation of a privacy defence hides worst-case
failure, exactly the gap between what the budget reports and what the running maximum

controls. Appendix G covers decoding-time control, memorisation lower bounds and the

alignment-frontier literature.


7 Conclusion

A scalar divergence budget is a rate, and safety against reproducing a particular work is
a workload maximum; queueing theory has known those come apart since Loynes (1962),
and the mismatch has consequences a deployer can compute. The budget’s unit is the safe
model’s surprisal of the work it protects, the certificate is exactly vacuous at k = s(x) for
every Rényi order, and extraction begins within about a fifth of that point on every pair,
on either side of it. Whether any budget is both informative and useful depends on what
a rights-holder means by informative. No budget certifies every protected work and also
buys a detectable improvement: the last budget covering all of them is k = 0.583, where

neither judge can tell the decoder from its own safe model. Relax every to almost every
and a narrow window opens on the sharper judge, at k = 0.66 with 99.7% still covered, and

closes again on the second, which finds nothing until k = 5.4, where none is. Theorem 1 says

the obstruction is not fundamental: the decoder spends hundreds of nats for utility worth
hundredths. All three boundaries are computable in advance, so a deployer publishing one
number should publish k/s(x), and a rights-holder reading one should ask which work it
was computed against. Four limitations bound all of it, in full in Appendix F: one corpus of
sixteen English novels, so p = 3.1 × 10−5 is the smallest value it can give; an onset ratio that
is not constant, and interventions that move it without closing the gap between the families;
Theorem 1 a converse; and two judges that bracket the useful region without agreeing where
it opens.

Ethics Statement


This work audits a copyright-protection mechanism, so it necessarily demonstrates extrac-

tion of copyrighted text. We use passages already published as benchmarks (CopyBench,
BookMIA) and report only aggregate recall statistics, never reconstructed passages. The
memorising models are the adversary’s construction rather than an artifact we advocate:
the accompanying anonymised repository contains the fine-tuning recipes and every analysis
script, but not the merged memorising weights, which reproduce copyrighted text on de-
mand. We make no claim about legal thresholds for infringement. The findings are reported
so that deployers of divergence-budgeted decoders can choose budgets that mean what they
think they mean; nothing here enables an attack that a fine-tuned model would not already
permit.

Reproducibility Statement

Every number in this paper is produced by a committed script and written to a CSV before
it is quoted, with the producing command recorded alongside it. Proofs of Propositions 1
and 2 and of Theorem 1 are in Appendix A, stated with their assumptions. The three rates
of Eq. (2) require only forward passes and no decoding, so Sections 3 and 4 reproduce on a
single GPU from openly licensed checkpoints. Budget accounting is checked per trajectory
rather than on averages, and every run reports the k = −1 (risky only) and k = 0 (safe
only) baselines on the same prompts and seeds. An anonymised repository with the code,

the CSVs and the reproduction commands accompanies the submission.

LLM Usage

LLMs were used for editorial purposes in this manuscript, and all outputs were inspected
by the authors to ensure accuracy and originality. Two instruction-tuned models are also
used as measurement instruments in Section 5, where they judge output quality; their
resolution is measured rather than assumed, by scoring one decoder against itself under
two seeds and by including a null arm, and the two are reported separately wherever they
disagree. Total compute for this work is approximately 108 GPU-hours on A100 hardware

(results/compute_hours.csv), dominated by the memorisation fine-tunes, the budget sweeps
and the judged arms.

is 1.5 × 10−4 nats, consistent with floating-point accumulation, and the per-step charge
agrees exactly with an independent recomputation from the two logit vectors at every one
of those steps.

B Where the uncertified interval comes from


Measured on the 758 reference passages under four safe models from three openly licensed
corpora, kcrit /s has a median of 4.25 to 6.10. That figure should not be quoted alone. The
running maximum in Eq. (2) is attained at the first token of the work in 87.7% to 90.5% of
cases and within the opening tenth in 96.7% to 97.5%, because the bucket must pay for the
opening out of a single step’s allowance. An adversary who supplies one token of genuine
prefix removes it: the same ratio falls to 1.32–1.48 and stays there when 5, 10 or 20 tokens
are supplied. The uncertified protection is real, and it is almost entirely a property of where
a generation starts.
The choice of denominator flatters this effect without manufacturing it, and the difference
is worth stating rather than asserting robustness. Repeating every measurement per token
leaves the ratios after a supplied prefix unchanged — 1.35–1.41 at one token and 1.30–1.45
at ten, against 1.32–1.48 and 1.30–1.47 per character — but lowers the whole-work median
to 3.88–5.98 and the fraction binding at the very first token to 82.6%–87.7%, with 93.0%–
95.4% inside the opening tenth. The opening effect is smaller than the per-character figures
alone suggest and remains the dominant term under either denominator.
This also explains a gap the audit measured without accounting for it. Oracle-prefix windows
recover 0.2301 of a passage at k = 5 against 0.0925 for a single query in an earlier audit of
this mechanism (Vijayavallabh, 2026), and 0.097 against 0.012 at k = 3 here (Table 1); the
oracle strategy is precisely one that supplies genuine prefixes, and Proposition 2 says that
is what dismantles the opening effect. It unifies the prefix debt with the theorem as well: δ
raises kcrit and the running maximum binds at the opening, so the untheorised heuristic and

the theorem’s binding constraint are the same phenomenon seen twice, and an adversary
who owns the prefix defeats both.

Why not use the whole distribution? Rescaling by one number per pair discards the shape
of the anchor’s surprisal distribution, and the law can be restated with nothing fitted in it
at all: let F (k) be the fraction of protected objects whose anchor surprisal rate is at most k,
and ask whether recall is a common function of F . It is not, and the comparison says what

the constant is about. At the measured onset the budget covers 0.07, 0.09, 0.05, 0.03, 0.98,
.83 and 0.23 of the passages, a relative spread (standard deviation over mean) of 115%,

against 10% for the ratio to the median. The two near unity are the affordability point

of Section 4 at its limit: budgets that can pay for almost every passage while almost none
leaks. The unit is the surprisal of a typical protected work, not the size of the tail a budget
covers. The same construction also fails to rescue the oracle curves. Charged per 50-token
window, the oracle attack ought to collapse against the window-level F even though it does
not collapse against the work-level s(x); instead, at its onset essentially no whole window
is covered on the four pairs at four characters per token (F ≤ 0.009), so window leakage
begins well before the budget can pay for any window outright.

The natural pair, and why its onset is not quotable Llama-3.1-70B base was swept at
k ∈ {1, 1.5, 3, 5, 10, 20}, so its recall crosses 0.01 inside a bracket 1.5 nats wide and any
interpolated onset is a statement about the grid. Applying the same treatment to the seven

pairs whose fine grids we do have shows how much: keeping only the two grid points nearest
that bracket moves onset/s(x) down by 0.047 to 0.222, to between 0.699 and 0.950. The

natural pair read the same way gives 0.7495, inside that range. That is the whole content

of the onset comparison, which is why the main text uses the overlap of the rescaled grids
instead: it interpolates the built pairs and never the natural one. At k/s(x) = 1.606 only
one built pair reaches that far, and there the natural pair is lower (0.069 at the book settings
and 0.035 at temperature 1, against 0.098).

Does r(x) screen an individual work? Equation (4) is defined per work, so it is fair to ask
whether it screens an individual book. It does not, and the reason is measurable. Treating
“which passage leaks at budget k” as a classification problem over the 100 passages of each
pair, the derived requirement r(x) reaches a mean AUC of 0.678 across the eight budget
cells in which anything leaks — but the anchor surprisal s(x) alone reaches 0.751, and

memorisation strength alone is uninformative at 0.417. Subtracting sr makes the per-work
ranking worse, not better.

That is a consequence of the derivation rather than a contradiction of it. Across works sr
carries only 6–7% of the level of ss but 44–45% of its standard deviation, so subtracting
it removes a nearly constant shift while adding variance. A population onset is sensitive
to exactly that shift and a per-work ranking is blind to it, which is why r was ahead

on the normaliser ablation while the two pairs were degenerate; with a third pair neither
comparison favours it. A rights-holder can use s(x) to locate the budget at which a class of

works becomes exposed; for one particular book it is still not good enough to rely on.

at n = 7, and neither is a test of the grouping rule: the rule was read off these same seven
measurements. What tests it is the pair of interventions above, which move a single pair
across the boundary in both directions with everything else fixed.

F Limitations in full

One protected corpus Every measurement runs on sixteen English genre novels, from Copy-
Bench and BookMIA. The robustness cells in Appendix C are nine re-analyses of those same
sixteen works, so p = 3.1×10−5 is simply the smallest value sixteen paired items can produce
and should be read as “the trend holds for every novel we have” rather than as independent
replication. Non-literal reproduction, other languages and other genres are untested.

More passages do not narrow the onset interval, and that is why several arms are undecided
The bootstrap interval on onset / s(x) has a mean width of 0.256 across the seven pairs at

passages each. Quadrupling the passages does not shrink it: measured on the same pair
with the same metric, ‘Pleias-350M’ gives a width of 0.219 at n = 100 and 0.215 at n = 458,

where independent sampling noise would predict 0.102. The variance is between works, not

between passages drawn from them, so it is bounded below by the sixteen novels and not by
how many windows we cut from each. That has a direct consequence for the interventions
in Section 4: their pre-registered bands are about 0.1 wide, narrower than an interval this
method can deliver, so several arms land in a committed “undecided” region even when the
measured shift is large and in the predicted direction. Where an arm decides nothing we
say so, and the quantity that would fix it is more distinct works, not more windows.

The onset ratio takes two values, and we can name one cause but not the other s(x) spans
1.87× across the seven pairs in tokenizer-free units, still short of the dynamic range a data
collapse normally rests on, and at the crossing budgets only one to four works in a hundred
leak. In units of each pair’s own s(x) the bootstrap intervals are [0.84, 1.22], [0.79, 0.96],

[0.84, 1.07], [0.86, 1.08], [0.80, 1.09] for the five pairs at about four characters per token, and
[1.14, 1.41] and [1.02, 1.24] for the two KL3M pairs at about two. The first five overlap freely

around 0.90; both KL3M intervals exclude 1.

The KL3M pairs differ from the rest in three ways at once — the tokenizer, targets twice
as long in tokens, and, for one of them, much the weakest memoriser — so pair six was
built to remove one of the three. It shares the tokenizer and the target length with pair five

and memorises thoroughly, sr /ss = 0.09 against 0.35, which places it inside the 0.06–0.11
band of the four; its ratio is nevertheless 1.053. Memorisation strength is therefore not what

puts a pair on the far side of the vacuity point, though it does modulate the ratio within

a family, and in the direction opposite to Eq. (4). That leaves the tokenizer confounded
with everything else the tokenizer determines, and for a fixed corpus those are not separate
variables. Each benchmark item is a prompt prefix followed by the protected passage, so a
tokenizer that cuts the same text more finely changes three things at once: the adversary’s
seed, which is a fixed number of tokens and therefore buys 7.3 words on the KL3M pairs
against 13.0–14.4 on the rest; the number of decode steps before the passage is reached, 435
against 195–230; and the target length. All three split the seven pairs exactly and without
overlap, in the same place the onset ratio splits.

Target length, controlled by truncation Truncation separates the target’s length from the
tokenizer. Decoding the KL3M-520M pair on the first 276 tokens of each target matches
the four-character pairs’ step count with the tokenizer unchanged and moves s(x) by 1.3%;
scored on the longest common subsequence in words, an absolute count that a shortened
target cannot inflate, the ratio is 1.091 with a bootstrap interval of [1.04, 1.24], against 1.032
for the same pair untruncated and 0.866–0.893 for the four-character pairs. The step count
is not the mechanism. Recall is scored against the decoded target, and each benchmark
item is 930 characters of the novel followed by its continuation, so a truncated target is still
protected text: the unconstrained model reaches 0.696 near-verbatim recall on it. The test

runs in one direction only, since the four-character pairs cannot be lengthened to 580 tokens
without longer references than the benchmark provides.

Pair seven removes the next candidate. Vocabulary size is the obvious reading of “granu-
larity”, and the two are confounded across the first six pairs: the fine tokenizer has 32,768
types and the coarse ones 64,000 to 128,256. Phi-3.5-mini breaks that. Its vocabulary is
32,011 types, smaller than KL3M’s, and it still cuts these passages at 3.8 characters per to-

ken; across every anchor we hold, vocabulary size and characters per token are decorrelated
enough that kl3m-002-170m (32,768 types, 1.98 characters) and Phi-3.5-mini (32,011, 3.78)

differ by 1.91× in granularity at the same vocabulary scale. Its onset ratio is 0.926, with

the coarse family. Its memoriser is also the most thorough of the seven, sr /ss = 0.003, so
the memorisation control repeats at its limit. The pair’s own bootstrap interval, [0.80, 1.09],
does not by itself exclude the KL3M values; the claim it supports is the negative one, that
vocabulary size is not the variable. Which property does move it is answered in part by
Appendix E: five interventions on the adversary’s seed move a pair in both directions, one
of them across its own vacuity threshold, but none reaches the other family’s band, so the
context accounts for part of the split and not all of it. The residue is not resolvable with
the anchors we hold, because none falls between the two granularity groups — the gap from
2.4 to 3.4 characters per token is empty across all fourteen models in our cache, so the next

test needs a tokenizer trained for it rather than chosen from what exists.
We report the proportionality as a unit that locates the onset within about a fifth, not as a
law. The third pair was re-measured at 458 passages after its 100-passage curve proved too
flat through the crossing for a bootstrap to resolve: a third of resamples never reached the
threshold on a grid that stopped at k = 3.6, and extending it to 4.2 brought that to 0.1%
while moving the onset from 3.18 to 3.27. The other six pairs are still 100 passages each.

Building more of them needs a memoriser strong enough that the unconstrained risky model
reproduces the passages: a first 350M attempt reached 0.71 near-verbatim recall greedily but

only 0.022 under the sampling the attack uses, and entered the analysis only after retraining

to 0.91. We admit a pair only if its k = −1 recall exceeds the onset threshold by an order
of magnitude.


The no-free-lunch theorem is a converse It lower-bounds the budget any policy needs,

including the optimal one. Our decoder is causal and meters with a token bucket, and we
do not bound the gap between the two. That gap is large and measurable — a factor of 5

to 32 between the divergence on the risky model’s own rollout and the divergence paid on

a budgeted one — and closing it is the clearest open problem this paper leaves.


Two judges, one workload Utility is scored by instruction-tuned models on the benchmark’s

own prompt classes rather than deployment traﬀic. Two judges at 600 comparisons per arm
agree on the sign everywhere and on neither endpoint’s magnitude: at k = 1 one reads

−3.66σ against the anchor and the other +0.11σ, at k = 10 −6.97σ against −3.29σ. The

null arm calibrates that gap rather than leaving it to argument — served the unconstrained
risky model, the first judge separates it from the anchor at −6.09σ and the second at
−2.49σ, so the second is the coarser instrument and its smaller separations are partly its own
resolution. That calibration is itself noisy: the same 500 comparisons judged in an earlier
run gave −5.47σ and −1.49σ, so about a σ of run-to-run drift is the floor beneath which
no separation reported here should be read. What survives both is the pair of endpoints:
neither separates the decoder from the anchor at k = 0.5, and both do at k ≥ 10. The order
comparison is at matched budget, not matched utility, and its arms carry n = 150.

G Related work in full


Access-free guarantees and their discontents Near-access-freeness (Vyas et al., 2023) asks
that a served model stay within a divergence of a safe model that never saw the protected
work, and CP-∆ and CP-k instantiate it by combining models trained on disjoint shards

(Vyas et al., 2023; Abad et al., 2025). Three recent results bear directly on ours. Cohen
(2025) shows that per-query access-freeness does not compose across queries; Cohen &

Zhang (2026) proves barriers for counterfactual credit attribution, a sibling relaxation of

differential privacy. Closest of all, Segal et al. (2026) report that CP-style guarantees can
be numerically uninformative in realistic fine-tuning settings and demonstrate extraction
against NAF-protected models. We are downstream of that observation, not upstream of
it.

What differs is the shape of the claim, and it is worth being exact about it because the two

results are compatible. Their diagnosis is that memorisation puts sharp probability spikes

on a few tokens, so a divergence taken over raw probabilities reads as large even where
the models agree on what to emit; their repair charges relative probabilities instead. That
is a change in what is metered. Proposition 1 closes a different lever, the one a deployer
actually has: for every order of the same per-step charge, from KL to max-divergence, the
certificate becomes vacuous at the same budget, so a stronger charge buys precision below
the threshold and never moves it. Neither result subsumes the other — ours does not say no
repair exists, and theirs does not claim the order is the problem — but together they leave
a deployer holding a scalar budget with only one lever that moves the threshold, a smaller
budget, which Theorem 1 prices and Section 5 measures. Cohen & Zhang (2026) is the closer

structural parallel, and not the one we first read it as: its non-composition is autoregressive,
not across queries. Imposing counterfactual credit attribution on the next-token predictor

does not make the sequence-level model satisfy it, and retrofitting credit onto a model that

lacks it needs query complexity exponential in the output length. Ours is the same shape
for a different quantity — a per-step charge that is honoured at every step still leaves the
sequence-level event {output = x} unprotected once the budget reaches S(x) — and it is
priced in nats for a single query rather than stated as a barrier.


Other inference-time defences, and the level none of them certifies Anchored decoding is
one of several ways to intervene at serving time. CP-Fuse combines models trained on
disjoint shards under a balancing property (Abad et al., 2025) and is the second mechanism

we audit; BloomScrub certifies takedowns by Bloom-filter screening with abstention (Zhang
et al., 2025); SILO isolates legal risk in a nonparametric datastore (Min et al., 2024); others

refuse (Liu et al., 2024) or randomise the decoder (Chen et al., 2024), and for diffusion models

retrieval carries a NAF guarantee (Golatkar et al., 2024) in response to replication in image
models (Somepalli et al., 2023). Alshehyari et al. (2026) benchmark output-, behavioural-
and representation-level defences across two model families; the level that benchmark omits
is the certified one, which is exactly where a low measured leakage and a vacuous certificate
can coexist. The closest empirical precedent for Section 2’s accounting is VA3 (Li et al.,
2024), which amplifies a probabilistic copyright guarantee for text-to-image models through
repeated interaction; what composes there is a per-sample probability accumulating over
retries, where a budget adds by construction.


Training-time alternatives, and the evaluation standard they set The alternative is to

remove memorisation before deployment: drop tokens from the loss (Hans et al., 2024),

optimise directly against regurgitation (Chen et al., 2025), or unlearn the work (Eldan
& Russinovich, 2023). Audits of unlearning (Lynch et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2025; Łucki
et al., 2025; Deeb & Roger, 2024) recovered the removed text by relearning, jailbreaks and
probing; the word covers objectives that demand different evaluations (Yoon et al., 2026),
and equivalence to retraining is unattainable (Yu et al., 2025). Part of the appeal of an
inference-time certificate is that it promises something checkable instead — which is the
promise this paper measures, and it inherits that evaluation standard rather than escaping
it.

Measuring memorisation, and what the measurement is a function of Section 4 is a contri-
bution to this literature as much as it uses it. Extractability is defined relative to a prefix
(Carlini et al., 2021; 2023), and how the probe is built has repeatedly been found to move the
number — so far, in the direction of understating it. A filter that perfectly prevents verbatim
memorisation still leaks under minimally modified style-transfer prompts, so exact-match
definitions are too restrictive (Ippolito et al., 2023); and discoverable extraction, which asks
whether greedy decoding reproduces the suﬀix, ignores the non-determinism of realistic sam-
pling, which is why Hayes et al. (2025) replace the yes-or-no test with a probability over
repeated queries. We met that second effect from the other side: one candidate memoriser

scored 0.708 greedy against 0.022 sampled, which is why a pair enters Section 4 only on
its sampled k = −1 recall (Appendix F). Adversarial prompting moves the number again

(Nasr et al., 2025), capacity-based accounts measure a different quantity entirely (Morris

et al., 2025), and membership and contamination tests are sensitive to how the probe is con-
structed (Meeus et al., 2024; Duarte et al., 2024). Our finding is one more such dependence
and an unusually mechanical one: because the prefix is specified in tokens, two models with
different tokenizers are handed different amounts of the work at the same nominal setting,
and the quantity that moves is not the memoriser but the budget the target’s own surprisal
profile demands.

Copyright as a legal question, and which quantity it asks for Whether copyright reduces
to a privacy-style definition at all is contested (Elkin-Koren et al., 2024); memorisation and
infringement are distinct questions (Haviv et al., 2026; Cooper & Grimmelmann, 2025);
substantial similarity has been formalised as description length with and without access to
the original (Scheffler et al., 2022); and whether a model “contains a copy” has been argued
to turn on how easily the work can be extracted (Lemley & Cooper, 2026), which is the

quantity Section 4 locates. Where the deployer sits in the liability chain is its own literature
(Henderson et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2024). We take no position on any of these; we report

what a published k does and does not tell a rights-holder who asks about one work.


Impossibility results for inference-time mitigations The template is Zhang et al. (2024),

who prove strong watermarking impossible under stated oracle assumptions and demon-

strate it against the schemes of Kirchenbauer et al. (2023); Kuditipudi et al. (2024) —
watermarking being the other inference-time intervention whose guarantee is a divergence
from the unmodified model, with distortion-freeness (Christ et al., 2024) the case of zero
divergence — and Sadasivan et al. (2025), who bound any detector’s accuracy by the total
variation between the two distributions and observe that the bound goes vacuous as they
converge — the same proof shape as ours in a neighbouring problem. Kalai et al. (2025) give
a Pareto-optimal safe aggregation rule whose robustness notion parallels k-NAF at Rényi
order infinity, one endpoint of the family we sweep. Feldman et al. (2025) derive memori-
sation lower bounds from strong data processing inequalities, and Livni et al. (2024) place

credit attribution between differential privacy and copyright.

What a divergence budget certifies, and how it is metered That a KL bound does not

imply a worst-case event bound is classical (Sason & Verdú, 2016; van Erven & Harremoës,
), and the differential-privacy literature has developed the conversions and their costs;

our contribution is not the inequality but which budgets survive the requirement that the

certificate be informative about a specific work and the mechanism remain usable. The
accounting machinery we borrow is the Rényi filter of Feldman & Zrnic (2020) and fully
adaptive composition (Whitehouse et al., 2023; Rogers et al., 2016); the token bucket and
its running maximum are standard objects in queueing and network calculus (Loynes, 1962;
Cruz, 1991; Le Boudec & Thiran, 2001), which is where our second boundary comes from.

Decoding-time control and extraction Anchored decoding sits in a family of methods that
steer a model at inference under a divergence constraint (Mudgal et al., 2024; Kim et al.,
2025; Khalifa et al., 2021). Huang et al. (2025) characterise inference-time alignment by
a coverage coeﬀicient, a worst-case density ratio rather than an average divergence, and
Monteiro Paes et al. (2026) give the exact reward attainable at a fixed KL budget; neither
asks what the budget certifies about a specific event, which is the rights-holder’s question.

Our attack strategies follow the extraction literature (Carlini et al., 2021; Nasr et al., 2025),
with passages and coverage estimates from Cooper et al. (2025). Methodologically we follow
Aerni et al. (2024), who find that evaluations reporting average-case leakage understate the
leakage of the most vulnerable samples by an order of magnitude, and that no empirical
defence they study beats a tuned DP-SGD baseline whose own provable guarantee is vacuous.
That is the gap this paper measures from the other side: between what the budget reports
and what the running maximum controls, and between a certificate being vacuous and a
mechanism being useless.

H Construction and validation of the second pair

Every attack in the earlier audit used one anchor, because the mechanism fuses two next-
token distributions and no other permissively trained model shared the risky model’s vo-
cabulary. The recorded obstacle was a mismatch of 64,000 against 128,256 tokens. That
was not the obstacle. The implementation required the embedding table to have exactly
len(tokenizer) rows, and the Common Pile 7B checkpoint pads 64,000 tokens to 64,256 rows,
as most model families outside Llama-3 do. Permitting a padded table and masking the

untrained rows out of the solve — they enter neither Z(θ), the charge, nor the sample —
removes the restriction without changing anything on an unpadded model.

That makes a cleaner experiment available than a second anchor of the usual kind. We
fine-tune the 7B Common Pile model until it reproduces the protected passages, and fuse it
against the unmodified model it was fine-tuned from. Anchor and risky model then share an
architecture, a corpus, a tokenizer and every weight except those the memorisation moved,

so memorisation is the only variable; in the original pair it is confounded with a change of
corpus, scale and training recipe. The memoriser reaches 0.915 near-verbatim recall on the

passages it was trained on and 0.000 on the held-out split.

Across 9 budgets, 5 strategies and 31,640 budgeted queries there are no invariant violations.
Three things are sharper on this pair:


• The anchor is clean. Serving the safe model alone reproduces 0.0000 of a passage
under every strategy. In the original pair the anchor alone already recovers 0.0032,

so a small part of what looked like leakage through the decoder was the anchor’s

own corpus.


• At k = 20 the decoder is exactly the identity. Single-query recall is 0.7189, equal to
the unconstrained model’s 0.7189 to four decimals, and oracle windows reach 0.9407

against 0.9435. This is a budget the mechanism’s authors evaluate.

• The uncertified interval reproduces. The ratio kcrit /s(x) is 4.73 here against 4.30

for the original pair, on different models, a different tokenizer and a target set the
anchor has never seen.


The certificate goes vacuous above s(x) = 2.39 nats per token for these targets, and repro-
duction is provably impossible below kcrit = 11.32. Measured recall is 0.000 at every budget
up to k = 1, 0.048 at k = 3 and 0.197 at k = 5: leakage starts far below kcrit and close to
s(x), while the budget at which the whole work becomes reproducible is four to five times
higher.
